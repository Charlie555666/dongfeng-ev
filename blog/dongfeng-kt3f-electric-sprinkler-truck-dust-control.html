The Duty Cycle That Suits an EV Truck Perfectly

Watering duty is a slow-motion energy story. A sprinkler truck covers 20–40 km/h working speeds, sprays for most of every kilometre, and refills two to four times a shift. That profile breaks a diesel truck three ways and flatters an electric one:

  • Pump load dominates. The spray pump is a continuous draw that a diesel engine carries at wasteful partial load. An electric PTO delivers the pump's kilowatts at native efficiency — a 30–40% total energy reduction for the same water delivered.
  • Idle at refill. Diesel sprinklers idle through every tank refill. Electric ones consume nothing.
  • Slow-speed crawl. Watering pace is gentle on an electric drivetrain and brutal on a diesel after-treatment system — DPF clogging on slow-duty municipal diesels is a universal maintenance complaint that simply does not exist here.

Municipal Applications: The Quiet Dawn Shift

City street watering happens before traffic builds — 04:00 to 08:00 in dense districts. A diesel sprinkler at that hour is a noise complaint on wheels; the KT3F works at conversation volume. Municipal operators report three practical gains:

  1. Extended watering windows — earlier starts and hospital/school-zone access without exemption paperwork.
  2. Visible sustainability — city fleets under air-quality scrutiny show the electorate something tangible; procurement scoring increasingly rewards it.
  3. Dramatically lower running cost — a municipal sprinkler covering 120 km of spraying per day consumes roughly 150–180 kWh including pump: USD 13–20 at typical municipal tariffs, against USD 55–75 of diesel at emerging-market prices. Over 300 days that is USD 12,000–16,000 saved per truck per year.

Industrial Applications: Mines, Ports and Construction

The harder duty is haul-road dust suppression at mines and industrial sites, where dust is both a safety hazard (visibility on haul roads) and a regulatory one (particulate limits near communities). The KT3F's 4x2 format suits lighter duty routes; for the heavy end — highway, port and mine-road scale — the KT7A 6x4 (CATL 232 kWh, 25 t GVW, LvKong 164/308 kW) carries the same formula up in weight class. Between them they cover:

  • Open-pit haul-road watering paired with our TZ3V electric mining tippers — the entire pit runs without diesel particulates.
  • Port stockyard and terminal roads — watering operations under the same electrification programmes as electric terminal tractors.
  • Construction site access roads — compliance with site dust rules where diesel watering trucks undermine the emission narrative of green projects.
  • Highway median and shoulder irrigation — slow-lane duty that eats diesel maintenance budgets.

Why We Fitted a Smaller Battery

The KT3F's 166/200 kWh pack is deliberately modest — roughly a third of our mining tippers' capacity. Watering duty simply does not need more: daily distances are short, speeds are low, and the truck returns to depot every night. Right-sizing the battery keeps the FOB price in municipal budget territory (USD 75,000–100,000), keeps payload capacity in the tank rather than the pack, and still covers a full day's spraying. For customers wanting combined sweeping and washing duty, the KT1D sweeper-washer (CATL 232/266 kWh) shares the same electric sanitation platform.

Water System Details That Matter to Operators

  • Front duckbill sprays for primary road coverage at driving speed.
  • Rear spray bar and side jets for kerb and shoulder coverage.
  • High-pressure water cannon option for targeted washdown and remote-area spray arcs.
  • Electric pump with proportional control — spray rate matched to vehicle speed so water lands evenly regardless of traffic pace, something hydraulic-diesel systems approximate poorly.
