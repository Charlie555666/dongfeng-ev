Electric Truck HS Code and Customs Classification Guide

An electric truck buyer exporting from China faces a customs step that diesel buyers never planned for: the HS code that worked for a fossil-fuel goods vehicle may not capture the EV-specific subheading your port now requires. Getting the classification right on a Dongfeng KT5L electric cargo truck protects you from duty re-assessment and clearance delays.

What HS Code Covers a Pure Electric Truck

Pure electric goods vehicles generally classify under HS chapter 8704, the heading for motor vehicles for the transport of goods. Within that chapter many customs authorities use 8704.90 for other goods vehicles, while an increasing number have introduced EV-specific or energy-type subheadings that separate battery-electric trucks from diesel. The exact national subheading varies, so the first task for any importer is to confirm the EV line item with the destination customs authority rather than assuming the diesel code applies.

The HS code is only the start. Many ports also require a national taric or tariff line that names the energy source, and that line is what triggers an incentive or a surcharge. A 28-tonne electric cargo truck and a 28-tonne diesel cargo truck may sit in the same 8704 family but fall into different national lines, and the difference can be tens of thousands of dollars across a twenty-unit order.

Why EV Duty Rates Differ From Diesel

Several governments use tariff policy to push fleet decarbonisation, which means the duty and sometimes the VAT treatment on an electric truck can be lower than on a comparable diesel. Other markets apply the same rate but add a separate incentive or exemption that only triggers on the correct EV subheading. Missing that subheading can cost a fleet tens of thousands of dollars across a multi-unit order, because the incentive is claimed at entry, not later.

A second difference is the battery itself. Some jurisdictions treat the traction battery as part of the vehicle and others question whether it should be declared separately. The clean approach, and the one Dongfeng supports with its build sheet, is to declare the battery and motor as integral to the vehicle so the whole unit clears under one EV line. Splitting the battery into a second shipment invites a valuation dispute that can hold the truck at the dock for weeks.

Key Documents for EV Truck Clearance

Prepare the following paperwork before the vessel sails, because customs will want evidence that the vehicle is genuinely electric:

  • Commercial invoice stating EXW or FOB value and the exact model and battery capacity.
  • Bill of lading and packing list with the HS code pre-printed by the exporter.
  • Certificate of origin issued by the Chinese chamber of commerce.
  • Technical datasheet proving the drivetrain is battery-electric with no internal combustion engine.
  • Battery material safety declaration covering the CATL LFP pack.

KT5L Cargo Truck Classification Example

The KT5L electric cargo truck is the long-wheelbase cargo variant with a 282 kWh CATL LFP pack, liquid cooling and a 282 to 510 kW LvKong motor. On a 2026 FOB basis the unit lands at roughly $68k to $85k for the KT5M sibling and a comparable figure for the KT5L, and the customs value should reflect the battery and motor as integral to the vehicle, not as separate declared items. The Pakistan electric truck market in particular rewards clean classification because local assembly incentives hinge on proof of electric drivetrain.

Because the KT5L is a long-wheelbase truck, its gross vehicle weight typically places it in a higher capacity band than the KT5J, and that band can change the duty percentage at the border. The exporter should state the kerb weight, gross weight and payload on the datasheet so the broker selects the right line the first time. A mismatch between the declared weight and the physical plate is the single most common reason a clean EV shipment gets pulled for inspection.

Common Classification Mistakes

Avoid these errors that trigger re-assessment:

  • Using the diesel HS code by habit and missing the EV subheading.
  • Under-declaring battery capacity to lower value, which fails the technical check.
  • Splitting the charger or battery into a separate shipment without a linked entry.
  • Omitting the Certificate of Origin, which blocks preferential rates.
  • Letting the invoice describe the unit only as a truck without naming it battery-electric.

How to Read Your Customs Assessment

When the assessment arrives, do not just check the total duty, read the line items line by line. Confirm the HS code matches the EV subheading you expected, confirm the declared value equals your commercial invoice, and confirm the battery is listed as integral to the vehicle rather than as a separate goods item. A clean assessment shows one line for the truck with the correct EV code and no standalone battery or charger entry. If you see a diesel code or a split battery line, stop and query it before you pay, because correcting it after release is slower and may attract a penalty that the exporter cannot absorb.

Also check the weight band. The KT5L long-wheelbase cargo truck sits in a higher gross-vehicle-weight band than the KT5J, and that band can move the duty percentage at the border even when the HS chapter is the same. The assessment should state the declared kerb weight, gross weight and payload, and those numbers should match the plate on the truck. A mismatch is the most common reason a clean EV shipment gets pulled for a physical inspection, which costs you dwell time you cannot recover.

Pre-Shipment Checklist With Your Broker

Before the vessel sails, run this short checklist with a licensed broker in the destination market so there are no surprises on arrival:

  • Confirm the exact EV subheading and any required energy-type or battery suffix.
  • Verify the duty rate, VAT treatment and any green-vehicle exemption that applies.
  • Agree the declared value basis, EXW or FOB, with the exporter in writing.
  • Pre-lodge the Certificate of Origin and the technical datasheet.
  • Confirm whether the charger ships as a separate line or bundled with the truck.
  • Ask whether the port requires a pre-arrival declaration for electric vehicles.
