Electric Truck Depot Grid Connection: Lead Time Planning

A fleet can order twenty electric trucks today and still be unable to run a single one next year if the depot grid connection is not planned in parallel. The trucks are the visible purchase; the transformer, the utility approval and the civil works are the invisible bottleneck that decides whether your EV truck investment earns revenue or sits parked. For a 20-truck depot with 360 kW chargers, you need roughly 1.5-2.5 MVA of grid capacity, and in most markets the utility upgrade alone takes 6-18 months. This guide shows fleet operators how to plan the grid connection lead time so the power is ready when the trucks arrive.

Too many importers treat charging as a plug on the wall and only discover the constraint at commissioning. A Dongfeng EV truck with a 282-510 kW LvKong motor and a 210-600 kWh CATL LFP pack can draw from 120 kW to over 1 MW depending on the charger, and that draw must be planned at the substation, not the parking bay. The KT5L electric cargo truck with its 262 kWh pack is a manageable first unit, but once a fleet scales to mixed 600 kWh trucks the math changes fast.

Why grid connection is the real bottleneck, not the trucks

Truck production lead time from a Chinese factory is typically 30-90 days. Grid connection in Europe, the Gulf, South Asia and Latin America is 6-18 months because it involves utility engineering, transformer delivery and sometimes a new feeder line. If you order the trucks first and start the grid process after, you will own parked assets for a year. The rule is simple: start the utility application the same week you sign the truck letter of intent, and treat the transformer as a parallel project with its own milestone chart.

Sizing the transformer: how many MVA do you need

The table below maps fleet size and charger power to the transformer capacity you should request. Always add 20-30 percent headroom for future expansion and for the fact that chargers rarely run at perfect load balance.

5 trucks120-150 kW0.5-1.0 MVA3-9 months
20 trucks360 kW1.5-2.5 MVA6-18 months
20 trucks + MCS360 kW + 1 MW3.0-4.0 MVA12-24 months

A 20-truck depot at 360 kW per stall, with perhaps four to six stalls active at once, pulls 1.4-2.2 MW. Request 1.5-2.5 MVA to cover simultaneous charging, lighting, workshop and the 20-30 percent headroom. Undersize and you will throttle chargers, which lengthens turnaround and erodes the daily km a truck can cover, quietly cutting fleet utilization.

The utility paperwork trail, market by market

Every utility wants a load study, a single-line diagram and a forecast of charging sessions. Prepare a document that states each truck's pack size (210-600 kWh), its peak charge rate (120-360 kW, or 1 MW for MCS) and the daily energy throughput in kWh. Most utilities also require a certified electrician's sign-off and a deposit against the transformer. In some markets a new feeder pole or underground run adds 3-6 months on top of the standard queue, so ask early whether the existing line can carry the load before you commit to a site.

Phasing the chargers as trucks arrive

You do not need every charger live on day one. A sensible phasing plan is:

  • Phase 1: two 150 kW chargers for the first 5 trucks, live at depot opening.
  • Phase 2: add 360 kW stalls as the fleet reaches 10-15 trucks.
  • Phase 3: reserve one MCS bay if any 500-600 kWh units join.
  • Phase 4: add battery storage to shave peak demand charges.

Phasing lets you match the transformer application to the realistic near-term load while leaving capacity for growth. It also spreads capital spend so the depot is never paying for idle chargers, and it keeps the early energy bill proportional to the trucks actually in service.

Cable runs, switchgear and the hidden civil works

The trench for the high-voltage cable, the switchgear cabinet and the cable management reels are easy to forget and expensive to retrofit. Budget $20,000-60,000 of civil works for a 20-truck yard, more if the substation is far. Liquid-cooled 360 kW cables cost $3,000-8,000 each and need reels and drip loops. Plan the trench depth, the conduit and the grounding grid before the concrete is poured, because cutting it later means closing bays and losing charging days during the busiest season.

Battery storage and solar to cut peak demand

A 200-500 kWh stationary battery at the depot smooths the peak draw and can cut demand charges by 20-40 percent. Paired with a 50-200 kW rooftop or canopy solar array, the depot can charge trucks on midday sun and draw from the grid only at night. For a 20-truck site, a $80,000-200,000 storage addition often pays back in 3-5 years through lower utility demand tariffs, and it reduces the required MVA from the utility, shortening the lead time and the deposit risk.

Pakistan example and the regional pattern

The Pakistan electric truck market reflects a common South Asian story: strong interest in electric cargo and tractor units, but grid constraints and long utility queues. A Pakistan importer planning 20 KT5L-class trucks should file the 1.5-2.5 MVA application at least a year out, phase the chargers, and consider on-site storage to reduce the utility burden. The same playbook fits Bangladesh, Sri Lanka and parts of the Gulf where feeder capacity is tight and new lines take many months to commission.

A 12-month planning timeline you can follow

Work backward from your first delivery date:

  • Month 1: sign truck letter of intent, file utility load study.
  • Month 2-3: utility approves, order transformer and switchgear.
  • Month 4-6: civil works, trenching, cable trays.
  • Month 6-9: transformer installed and energized.
  • Month 9-11: chargers commissioned and tested.
  • Month 12: trucks arrive and charge on day one.

Coordinating the charger order with the grid timeline

The single most expensive mistake is ordering chargers before the transformer is approved, then watching the cabinets sit in a warehouse for a year waiting for power. Lead the project with the utility, not the hardware. File the load study, secure the transformer slot and only then place the charger order so delivery lands just before energization. A 360 kW charger has a 2-4 month build time, so it should be ordered around month 4-5 of a 6-18 month grid process, not month 1.

Also coordinate the civil works with the charger footprint. A wall-mounted row needs a reinforced wall and cable tray; an island cluster needs a pad and underground conduit. Settling this during the utility wait avoids a second trench later. Build a single Gantt with the transformer, the trench, the switchgear and the charger commissioning on one timeline, and assign one owner to chase the utility each week. Fleets that run the grid and the hardware as one project are the ones that charge on day one instead of month thirteen.

Document every approval in writing and keep the utility case number visible to the whole project team. When the substation is finally energized, run a witnessed 10-80 percent charge test at the stated kW and file the result; that closes the loop and proves the depot performs before the trucks are even unloaded.

Get the grid right and the electric trucks simply work. Get it wrong and you own the most expensive paperweights on the continent. Start the application now, size for 1.5-2.5 MVA per 20 trucks, and phase the chargers to match the fleet as it grows rather than paying for capacity you cannot yet use.
