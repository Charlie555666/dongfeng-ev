How Do EV Trucks Keep Torque at Altitude?

An electric truck motor is an electric machine with a flat torque curve: maximum twist is available the instant the wheels need it, regardless of rpm or thin mountain air. A diesel engine, by contrast, is an air pump, and at altitude the air is thinner, so it makes less power. The rule of thumb fleet engineers use is that diesel loses about 3 percent of its rated power for every 300 m of altitude gained. At 3,000 m a diesel tractor is roughly 30 percent weaker than at sea level, which is exactly where mountain roads are steepest and the power is needed most. The TZ5E electric dump truck keeps all 424 kW of rated output from the pit floor to the pass, so climb speed stays constant instead of collapsing near the top.

Energy Recovery and the Descent Bonus

The descent is where an EV truck turns a liability into a saving. Regenerative braking on an 8% down-ramp recovers 10-20 percent of the energy spent climbing, and on long Andean or Himalayan drops the figure can exceed 15 percent per run. Against a diesel that wastes that energy as heat in the brakes and exhaust brake, the electric truck arrives at the valley charger with more state-of-charge than a simple out-and-back math would predict. For a contractor paid by volume moved per shift, this recovered energy directly lowers the USD 0.12-0.32 per km energy cost that already undercuts diesel's USD 0.55-0.72 per km on the same grade.
