Dispatch Is Where the Electric Fleet's Economics Are Won

Fleet buyers often expect electrification's challenges to arrive as hardware problems - range, charging, batteries. Operators who have run electric fleets for a year report something different: the hardware behaves, and the real discipline is dispatch. An electric 6x4 dump truck has a known energy budget each morning, refills on a schedule measured in tens of minutes to hours rather than minutes, and rewards the dispatcher who plans energy the way diesel fleets learned to plan fuel stops. The TZ3Z electric 6x4 dump truck feeds dispatch the data that makes this manageable - state of charge, consumption rate, and range-at-current-conditions reported continuously. Range-Aware Scheduling: The Core Discipline

Range-aware dispatch sounds more dramatic than it is. The working rules fit on one screen: assign routes whose total distance plus reserve fits the truck's morning state of charge; hold the reserve honestly - 15-20% against traffic, weather and the day that goes wrong; and treat mid-shift charging as a scheduled event, not an emergency, where routes exceed single-charge range. The TZ3Z's 250 km of certified range covers the large majority of urban and regional routes in Astana outright, which means most trucks most days need no midday planning at all. For the longer routes, opportunity charging slots into the breaks the schedule already contains - loading, lunch, the dock queue. Dispatch software that ingests the truck's live state of charge automates the arithmetic; the dispatcher's judgment stays where it belongs, on the exceptions.

Charging-Aware Scheduling: The Fleet as One System

The second discipline looks at the fleet rather than the truck: charger capacity is shared infrastructure, and the schedule must sequence it. Overnight, managed charging handles this invisibly - trucks stagger their starts and finish by morning departure. The operational question is daytime: which trucks opportunity-charge, when, and for how long. The working answer comes from the duty data: trucks on long routes charge in their midday window, short-route trucks skip it, and the charging plan publishes with the shift plan so drivers see slots, not surprises. Energy consumption on the TZ3Z runs 1.0-1.6 kWh per kilometer in typical Kazakh duty, so a 30-minute slot on a 120 kW charger returns 50-60 km - often the difference that lets a truck take a second route. Fleets report that charging discipline becomes routine faster than expected - inside a month, it is simply how the board looks.

Telematics Integration: Data Flowing Both Ways

The dispatch stack works when data flows both directions. Truck to office: live state of charge, position, consumption rate and driver behavior from the TZ3Z's factory telematics, feeding the dispatch screen the energy picture alongside the familiar position map. Office to truck: route assignments and charging instructions through the driver app or terminal, closing the loop. The integration worth insisting on is API-level - the truck's data flowing into the fleet management system the operation already runs, rather than a separate screen watched separately. Exception alerts automate the supervision: projected range shortfall against remaining route, missed charging slot, consumption anomaly flagging a mechanical issue early. Fleets in Kazakhstan running integrated dispatch report the supervision overhead per truck falls below their diesel baseline - the data does the watching.

Utilization: The Number That Pays for Everything

Fleet economics run on utilization, and the honest question for electric trucks is whether charging time costs revenue hours. The deployment data answers clearly: on duty cycles matched to range - the fundamental specification discipline - utilization matches diesel within a few points, because charging lands in hours the truck was not earning anyway. Overnight charging consumes zero working time; opportunity charging consumes break time that regulation or rhythm already schedules. Where utilization genuinely differs, the cause is usually specification error - a truck assigned beyond its comfortable range, living on opportunity charge - and the fix is assignment, not technology.
