The cold chain is the natural first electrification win

Refrigerated transport is the ideal electric use case. The truck returns to the same depot every night, the route is predictable, and the refrigeration load is steady rather than random. In Colombia, where export fruit, meat, seafood and pharmaceuticals all depend on an unbroken cold chain, the KT5M configured as a refrigerated unit replaces both the diesel drivetrain and the separate reefer engine with a single 180-220 kWh pack, cutting energy cost, noise and emissions at the same time.

Reefer powered from the traction battery

In the KT5M configuration we build, the refrigeration unit draws from the chassis CATL LFP pack rather than a dedicated diesel burner. The reefer draw is typically 6-9 kW while holding setpoint, so the truck manages both propulsion and cooling from one energy source, one maintenance schedule and one bill. Because electric compressors hold temperature far more tightly than intermittent diesel units, product quality improves as well — a decisive point for Colombian exporters shipping to demanding buyers.

Pre-cooling, door discipline and holding time

The three habits that protect a cold chain are pre-cooling the box at the depot before loading, keeping door-open time to seconds, and never switching off the reefer while queuing. For a KT5M on a typical coffee and flower export cold chains route in Bogota, Medellin and Barranquilla, pre-cooling off cheap depot power while the truck is still plugged in cuts the energy drawn from the pack on the road. Drivers should treat the reefer as always-on from loading dock to delivery dock, exactly as a good diesel reefer operator would.

  • Zero tailpipe emissions: compliant with low-emission zones in Bogota, Medellin and Barranquilla.
  • Lower energy cost: electricity in Colombia is far cheaper per km than diesel.
  • Less maintenance: no engine oil, filters or aftertreatment service.
  • Regenerative braking: recovers energy on the urban distribution and cold chain cycle.
  • Predictable range: 250-300 km covers a typical coffee and flower export cold chains shift.
  • Warranty cover: 8 years / 4,500 cycles on the CATL LFP pack.

Range with the reefer running

The reefer load reduces range, so planning matters. A KT5M that achieves 250-300 km without cooling will do somewhat less with the reefer holding setpoint, depending on ambient temperature and door events. The practical answer for Colombia is a relay or mid-route top-up on the longest legs, and electrifying the shorter farm-to-packhouse and packhouse-to-port runs first. Most cold-chain duty cycles are well within range once the route is mapped honestly.

Cold-chain compliance and traceability

Modern export chains demand proof, not promises. An electric reefer unit with digital temperature logging produces a continuous, tamper-evident record that satisfies buyers and regulators in Colombia more cleanly than a paper chart. Combined with GPS and door-event data, the KT5M gives exporters a defensible record for every container. That traceability is increasingly a condition of contracts, especially for pharmaceutical and premium food cargo.

Comparing electric and diesel reefers

On paper a diesel reefer looks cheaper to buy; over its life the electric unit wins. The diesel burns fuel continuously, whether the truck is moving or parked, and needs separate engine servicing. The electric unit draws from a pack that is already paid for and maintained, and the energy is cheapest when charged at the depot from solar or off-peak power. For Colombian fleets adding KT5M units, the total cost per delivered pallet is consistently lower, and the ESG reporting is far simpler.

Why Colombia is ready for electric trucks now

The conditions that make an electric truck viable are all present in Colombia. high urban air-quality pressure and hydropower dominance. Freight demand concentrates in and around Bogota, Medellin and Barranquilla, where routes are short and predictable and the depot is never far away. Fuel logistics add cost and delay that a fleet charging its own vehicles simply avoids. For a KT5M electric cargo truck on a coffee and flower export cold chains duty cycle, this is not an experimental technology but a practical replacement for diesel, and the operators moving first are the ones who lock in the lowest cost per kilometre before their competitors do.

A step-by-step implementation plan

Electrifying a fleet in Colombia is a project, and running it in phases is what keeps it manageable. Phase one is a site and route audit: list every duty cycle, measure real daily distance and load, and map the depot power supply. Phase two is a pilot of two to five KT5M units on the most predictable routes, with chargers installed and drivers trained. Phase three is measurement — energy cost per kilometre, uptime, maintenance hours. Phase four is scaling what the data supports. Each phase de-risks the next and keeps the capital commitment matched to proven performance rather than optimism.

Choosing the right configuration

Within the KT5M range there are choices that matter more than cosmetics. The 4x2 layout suits the axle load and traction pattern of your duty cycle. Battery capacity of 180-220 kWh should be sized to the longest realistic daily route plus a safety margin, not to the biggest number on the brochure. Body and equipment specification — tipper, box, reefer, tank or compactor — should be matched precisely to the job in Colombia. Getting these four decisions right at order stage is far cheaper than modifying a truck after it lands.
