What Port Haulage Actually Demands

Port drayage is stop-start, low-speed, high-tonnage work: yard shuffles, quay-to-stacking, and short highway legs to the inland container depot. The duty profile — frequent acceleration, regen on every stop, short distances — is the single most favourable case for an electric truck. Energy use is dominated by mass and starts, not by cruising, so regen recovers a large share and pack sizing is about cycle continuity, not range anxiety. Terminals also value the zero-tailpipe operation near vessels and warehouses, and the quiet running at night shifts that many ports now run to spread road congestion.

  • Shift count. One shift favours simple DC charging; two or more shifts favour swap.
  • GCW. Yard-only loads stay under ~38 t; highway drayage with a loaded 40-foot box reaches ~45 t.
  • Charger vs swap bay. Swap needs a station and spare packs; charging needs only posts and idle time.
  • Terminal power. Solar + storage at the terminal cuts energy cost toward US$0.05–0.09/kWh.
  • Noise windows. Quiet electric tractors fit night-shift curfews better than diesel.

How Many Shifts Decide Swap vs Charge?

If you run a single shift and the tractor naturally idles at the depot for an hour at break or end-of-shift, DC charging the TE8L is simplest — no swap station capital. If you run two or three shifts, a 45-minute charge event removes a tractor from the haul road each cycle; the TE46’s 5–6 minute swap keeps every unit moving and is the lower-risk choice despite the higher infrastructure spend. Swap pays for itself once you operate four or more tractors across multiple shifts. The break-even is roughly four tractors: below that, the charger capex is lower; above it, the swap station pays back through avoided charger downtime.

What GCW Tips You to the TE8L?

Yard-only containers and chassis work sit inside the TE46’s ~38 t GCW envelope. When the duty includes highway drayage to an inland container depot with a loaded 40-foot box, GCW approaches ~45 t and the TE8L’s 282–350 kW motor and 350–420 kWh pack become necessary. The rule of thumb: if a loaded box leaves the terminal gate, spec the TE8L; if it stays in the yard, the TE46 wins on cost and uptime. We also see terminals run both — TE46 for the quay yard and TE8L for the gate-to-depot leg — which is the most cost-efficient fleet shape.

How Much Does a Port EV Truck Cost to Run?

At port diesel ~US$1.10/l, a diesel terminal tractor burns ~18 l/hour over ~5,000 hours — 90,000 l ≈ US$99,000. The TE46 at ~1.6 kWh/km equivalent draws ~0.9 GWh; at terminal solar US$0.09/kWh that is US$81,000, saving ~US$18,000/year, plus ~US$4,000 maintenance — ~US$22,000 annual advantage. The TE8L on longer legs saves ~US$25,000/year. Payback is 30–44 months depending on shift count. On a four-tractor swap fleet the avoided charger downtime alone often closes the business case.

Frequently Asked Questions

Which electric tractor truck is best for port container haulage?

For most terminals the Dongfeng TE46 terminal tractor is best: its 262–350 kWh pack with 5–6 minute battery swap sustains 24/7 drayage at 20–80% in 35–60 min equivalent, whereas the TE8L 350–420 kWh is the choice only when GCW exceeds 40 t or single-shift highway drayage to an inland depot is required.

How do I choose between battery swap and DC charging for port tractors?

Choose battery swap (TE46, 5–6 min swap) when you run two or more shifts and cannot afford charger downtime, because a swap matches diesel refuel speed; choose DC charging (TE8L, 20–80% in 35–60 min) when shifts are single and the tractor can charge during natural idle at the depot.

What battery size do I need for a port tractor?

You need 262–350 kWh for pure terminal yard shuffling (TE46) and 350–420 kWh when the duty includes highway drayage to an inland container depot (TE8L); both use CATL LFP cells rated 8 years / 4,500 cycles to 70% SOH.
