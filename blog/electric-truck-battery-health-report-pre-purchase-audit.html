Electric Truck Battery Health Report: Pre-Purchase Audit

Buying a used or demo electric truck without a battery health report is the fastest way to inherit someone else's worn pack. A proper EV truck battery audit reads state of health, cycle count and cell balance before money changes hands, and this guide shows a fleet buyer exactly what to check on a Dongfeng unit.

What State of Health Really Means

State of health, or SOH, is the percentage of the original capacity the pack still holds. A 282 kWh CATL LFP pack that reads 92 percent SOH still delivers about 259 kWh of usable energy, while one at 80 percent has lost nearly 56 kWh. Because the pack carries an 8-year or 4,500-cycle warranty, the SOH reading also tells you how much warranty life remains, which is a negotiable item on a demo or used unit.

SOH is not the same as the guess-o-meter range the dashboard shows. The dashboard range is a software estimate that can be optimistic; the SOH is a measured capacity from the battery management system. Always ask for the raw SOH export, not the driver-facing range number, because only the raw figure holds up in a commercial negotiation or a warranty claim.

Reading Cycle Count and Warranty

Cycle count is the number of full charge-discharge events the pack has logged. A truck used for two years in daily regional haul may show 700 to 1,000 cycles, well inside the 4,500-cycle limit. The audit must pull this number from the battery management system, not take the seller's word, because a pack near 3,000 cycles has far less remaining life even if SOH still looks acceptable.

Pair the cycle count with the depth of those cycles. A pack that spent its life on shallow 20 to 80 percent charges ages far better than one hammered with daily 0 to 100 percent deep cycles, even at the same count. The audit report should therefore show both the total cycles and the average depth, because together they predict the real remaining warranty runway.

Cell Balance and Thermal Checks

Check these items during the inspection:

  • Maximum cell voltage deviation across the module, ideally under 30 mV.
  • Liquid-cooling performance holding the 25 to 35 deg C window.
  • IP67 enclosure integrity with no ingress marks on the seal.
  • Isolation resistance on the high-voltage bus above the safe threshold.
  • Battery management system fault log with no repeated thermal events.

A TZ3Z that spent eighteen months hauling ore on short, heavy loops could show a deceptively healthy SOH but a high cycle count from those repeated shallow fills. Read both numbers together, and if the cycle count is high, price the earlier pack replacement into your offer rather than discovering it after a year of ownership.

Red Flags in a Used EV Truck

Walk away or renegotiate if you see any of these:

  • SOH below 80 percent with no price adjustment offered.
  • Cycle count deleted or marked unavailable by the seller.
  • Evidence of flood or dust ingress on the IP67 enclosure.
  • Cooling records showing operation outside 25 to 35 deg C.
  • A fault log with repeated contactor or isolation trips.

Using the Report to Negotiate

Armed with the audit, a buyer in Mexico or anywhere else can price the remaining pack life into the offer. A TZ3Z at 85 percent SOH with 1,200 cycles is worth close to new, while one at 82 percent with 2,800 cycles should drop in price to reflect the earlier replacement risk. Third-Party Versus Dealer Audit

You can ask the seller for the report, but a report you commission yourself carries more weight in negotiation. A third-party inspector with the right diagnostic tool reads the battery management system directly, photographs the seal and the fault log, and hands you a document the seller cannot quietly edit. The cost is a few hundred dollars and a half day at the truck, which is trivial against the risk of buying a pack with hidden cycle damage. For a single ex-demo unit the maths is obvious; for a batch of ten the audit pays for itself on the first price adjustment.

If the seller refuses an independent inspection, treat that refusal as the answer. A clean TZ3Z or KT5M with a genuine 90 percent-plus SOH and a low cycle count has nothing to hide, and a reputable dealer will welcome the check because it supports their price. Only a unit with something to conceal pushes back, and that is exactly the unit to walk away from before any money moves.

Audit Cost Versus Replacement Risk

Put the numbers side by side. A pre-purchase audit costs pocket change; a pack replacement outside warranty costs tens of thousands of dollars and weeks of downtime. Even a partial capacity loss that forces you to charge more often has a real productivity cost across a fleet. The audit is therefore not a luxury, it is the cheapest insurance in the whole used-EV transaction, and it belongs in your standard purchase process the same way a diesel pre-buy inspection does.

  • Budget the audit per unit before you negotiate, not after the fact.
  • Require the raw SOH and cycle export, not a smoothed summary.
  • Use the report to set a hard price linked to remaining pack life.
  • Keep the report for the vehicle file and for your own future resale.

In the Mexico market especially, where ex-mining demo trucks change hands quickly, the buyer who audits is the buyer who avoids the stranded asset.
