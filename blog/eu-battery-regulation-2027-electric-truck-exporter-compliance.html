EU Battery Regulation 2027: Compliance Guide for Electric Truck Exporters

The EU Battery Regulation (Regulation 2023/1542) becomes fully applicable in 2027 and fundamentally changes how any electric truck exporter must document and prove the environmental footprint of battery systems shipped into Europe. For fleet buyers and importers, the practical meaning is simple: every EV truck placed on the European market after the 2027 deadline must carry a digital battery passport and a verified carbon footprint declaration that traces the cell chemistry back to the mine, the refinery and the cell factory.

What the 2027 deadline actually changes for exporters

Until now, most electric truck exporters supplied batteries under general CE marking and UN R100 safety rules, with compliance documented in a single test report. From 2027, three new obligations stack on top of those rules. First, a carbon footprint declaration must accompany each battery model, calculated with the commission's defined methodology. Second, a digital battery passport must be readable by scanning a data carrier on the pack. Third, economic operators must run a battery due diligence system covering social and environmental risks in the raw material supply chain.

Carbon footprint declaration: the numbers that matter

The regulation sets a carbon footprint threshold expressed in kg CO2 equivalent per kWh of usable capacity. Exporters must report the declared value and compare it against the applicable threshold, and from 2028 a performance class label communicates the result to buyers. Exporters must report the exact variant shipped, because a single model line can span both passing and review-required bands depending on pack size.

Battery passport: data fields and access

The battery passport is a digital record, not a paper document. From 2027, every EV truck battery above 2 kWh must expose a unique identifier accessible by a data carrier such as a QR code or NFC tag. The minimum dataset includes the fields below, and the exporter must keep them updated for the full service life of the pack, not just at first sale.

  • Unique battery passport ID and batch serial number
  • Carbon footprint declaration and performance class
  • Material composition, including the share of recycled cobalt, lithium, nickel and lead
  • State of health and expected remaining cycle life
  • Information needed for removal, reuse, repurposing and recycling

Due diligence and supply chain obligations

The due diligence obligation targets the upstream chain: cobalt, natural graphite, lithium and nickel. Economic operators must identify and mitigate risks of child labor, forced labor, pollution and unsafe mining. A documented policy, a risk-management plan and third-party auditing are required, and the company must publish an annual report on its due diligence efforts. Fleet buyers importing through a compliant exporter inherit a cleaner audit trail, which simplifies their own ESG reporting to lenders and regulators.

Performance class labels and recycled content targets

Beyond the footprint, the regulation introduces performance classes and minimum recycled-content thresholds that phase in after 2028. LFP packs are exempt from the cobalt and nickel recycling targets but must still report lithium recovery rates. The practical checklist for fleets is short but must be completed before the truck is advertised for resale inside the EU:

  • Confirm the battery model number and declared capacity with the exporter before signing
  • Request the carbon footprint declaration and passport preview for the exact pack variant
  • Verify recycled-content reporting for lithium and lead where applicable
  • Map the service network so state-of-health data can be uploaded across the battery life

Cost of non-compliance and penalties

The regulation empowers member states to set penalties, and the expected range runs from fines proportional to shipment value to outright market exclusion for repeat offenders. For an exporter, a single non-compliant batch can mean recall, reputational damage and loss of the due-diligence registration that unlocks the entire EU channel. The cost of building the documentation system upfront is a fraction of one blocked shipment, so the business case for compliance is unambiguous.

Fleets should therefore treat the battery passport and carbon declaration as acceptance criteria in the purchase contract, not as a courtesy from the supplier. A contract clause that makes delivery conditional on a valid passport protects the buyer if the exporter's paperwork slips.
