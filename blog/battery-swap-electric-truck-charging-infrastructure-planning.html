Battery Swap vs Charging: Electric Truck Infrastructure Planning Guide

Every fleet electrifying heavy trucks faces the same fork in the road: build charging bays or build a battery swap station? For operators running a TZ3V electric dump truck on continuous shifts in Indonesia's nickel and construction corridors, the wrong choice strands capital and trucks alike. This guide compares the two models on capex, cycle time and operational fit so planners can size the right depot before a single truck arrives.

The temptation is to default to charging because it is familiar and cheap per bay. But familiarity is not fit. A swap station costs more and ties up packs, yet it can keep a 24-hour quarry running when grid power is weak and dwell time is zero. The correct answer is almost always a function of shift pattern, grid quality and truck count, not of which technology feels safer to finance. The sections below give planners the variables to model.

How the Two Models Work

Depot charging plugs the truck in and waits. Battery swap drives the truck over a bay where a crane or robot exchanges the depleted pack for a charged one in 5 to 6 minutes. Both use the same CATL LFP packs, so the trucks are identical; only the energy-delivery method differs. The swap model decouples vehicle downtime from grid power availability, which is decisive on sites with weak or intermittent supply and on operations where every minute of truck idle is lost production.

Capex and Throughput per Bay

For Indonesian operators the choice also interacts with local content and import rules, because a swap station is fixed infrastructure that must clear the same customs and permitting path as any plant. Planning the bay early, alongside the truck order, avoids the classic mistake of taking delivery of vehicles with nowhere compliant to energize them, which can idle a multimillion-dollar fleet on day one.

The capital story is the headline difference. A fast-charge bay is a fraction of the cost of a swap bay, but it serves a fraction of the trucks. The throughput multiple is what justifies the swap investment on continuous sites.

The swap bay costs far more up front and ties up capital in spare packs, but it serves an order of magnitude more trucks per station. For a 24-hour quarry or port, that throughput is the whole business case, because it lets the operator buy fewer trucks to move the same tonnage.

Swap Cycle Economics

The 5 to 6 minute swap only pays back when utilization is high. Cost drivers to model include the amortization of spare packs across swap cycles, the labor and maintenance of the crane, and the value of charging those spares during off-peak hours to flatten demand charges.

  • Spare pack amortization across swap cycles (8-year / 4500-cycle life shared).
  • Crane maintenance and operator labor per shift.
  • Off-peak charging of spare packs to flatten grid demand charges.
  • Reduced truck count needed because each unit spends less time idle.
  • Battery-as-a-service leasing to convert capex into opex.
  • Land and foundation cost for a swap bay, which is larger than a charge stall.

The financial model also benefits from the fact that swap decouples the truck from the charger entirely. Because a swapped truck is never waiting on a bay, the operator can run more shifts per pack-life than a charging-only fleet, and that utilization is where the depreciation is outrun. A truck that earns for twenty hours a day pays back its premium in months, not years, even after the spare-pack capital is counted, because the constraint is no longer how fast the pack charges but how fast the truck can cycle through its duty.

  • Model pack life in swap cycles, not just vehicle kilometers.
  • Spread spare-pack cost across all trucks served by the bay.
  • Charge spares during off-peak to capture the widest tariff spread.
  • Track per-bay throughput to know precisely when to add capacity.
  • Compare required truck count, not just bay cost, between options.

Indonesian operators who run the numbers this way frequently find that swap is cheaper per ton moved than charging on continuous sites, despite the higher bay cost, because the truck count required to hit a daily tonnage target falls. The saving in fewer chassis purchased and fewer drivers needed often dwarfs the infrastructure premium, which is why the throughput table earlier is the most important slide in the whole business case. Charging still wins on simple sites, but the moment a quarry runs three shifts the math flips decisively toward swap.

When to Choose Charging

Charging wins for lower-intensity fleets. If a dump truck runs a single 180 km shift and returns to a depot at night, a 240 kW DC charger plus overnight top-up is cheaper and simpler. Charging also suits sites with reliable, cheap power and ample dwell time. The TZ3V's 1.5 to 2 hour fast charge is acceptable when trucks naturally idle between loads, and there is no spare-pack capital to recover.

When to Choose Swap

Swap wins when trucks cannot afford to wait. The decision table below summarizes the triggers that should push a planner toward a battery swap bay rather than rows of chargers.

Hybrid Depot: Best of Both

For most Indonesian sites the right answer is not swap or charge but a hybrid that captures the throughput of swap where it matters and the low cost of charging everywhere else. A practical layout pairs two or three swap bays for the continuous mining and port trucks with a row of cheap overnight chargers for the rest of the fleet, so capital is spent only where dwell time is truly zero.

  • Start with chargers, add swap only after utilization justifies it.
  • Use spare packs from swap to buffer the whole site against outages.
  • Phase capacity as truck count grows to protect early cash flow.
  • Model the hybrid against pure-charge and pure-swap before committing.

The TZ3V's support for both energy-delivery methods is what makes the hybrid possible, because the same truck can use a swap bay on a busy shift and a wallbox on a quiet one. Operators who commit to a single rigid model too early often over-spend on swap or under-serve continuous lanes; the hybrid keeps options open and lets the duty cycle, not the vendor, dictate the final shape of the depot.

Planning Checklist

Before committing, Indonesian operators should survey grid capacity, shift pattern, truck count and land. A hybrid is often best: a few swap bays for continuous trucks plus cheap overnight chargers for the rest. Either way, the TZ3V platform supports both, so the infrastructure decision can follow the duty cycle rather than the other way around, and the fleet can scale one bay type at a time as utilization proves out.
