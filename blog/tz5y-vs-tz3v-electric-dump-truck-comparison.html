TZ5Y vs TZ3V Electric Dump Trucks: Spec-by-Spec Comparison

Nigerian operators weighing an electric dump truck - a heavy battery-electric truck built for tipper duty - usually narrow to two Dongfeng platforms: the TZ3V and the heavier TZ5Y. Both are battery-electric tippers, but they target different payloads, GVWs and price tiers. This TZ3V electric dump truck comparison with the TZ5Y helps contractors in Nigeria's construction and mining sectors pick the right electric truck for the job instead of over- or under-specifying the chassis.

The mistake most fleets make is buying on payload alone. A bigger truck is not automatically better if it spends half its day partially loaded, and a smaller truck is not cheaper if it needs two trips where one would do. The right call depends on daily tonnage, road grade, cycle distance and the price of diesel, which in Nigeria can swing the operating case more than any single spec. The breakdown below separates the two platforms on the variables that matter.

Payload and GVW Tiers

The TZ3V is the workhorse 8x4 for quarry and construction haul, while the TZ5Y is the ultra-heavy mining-class unit built for higher tonnage per trip. The trade-off is capital cost and charging demand versus tons moved per cycle, and that trade-off is exactly where the five-year economics are won or lost.

Battery and Energy Use

Heavier loads cost more energy per kilometer. Dump bodies sit in the 1.1 to 1.8 kWh per kilometer band, and the TZ5Y's larger pack offsets its heavier draw so that neither truck sacrifices usable shift range.

Both platforms share the CATL LFP pack and the same charging envelope, which simplifies depot design when a fleet runs a mix of the two. The 1.5 to 2 hour fast charge is acceptable on sites where trucks naturally queue between loads, and the optional 5 to 6 minute swap keeps continuous mining shifts moving without a charger bottleneck.

Grade and terrain also shape the comparison more than spec sheets admit. Nigerian quarry access roads are rarely flat, and the TZ5Y's higher peak torque of 3000 to 3200 Nm lets it hold speed up long climbs without the motor overheating, whereas a lighter truck may need to drop a gear it does not have. On the other hand, the TZ3V's lower mass means it recovers more energy on the descent through regen, which narrows the per-kilometer gap on hilly but shorter cycles where the heavier unit is never fully loaded.

  • Map the worst grade on the daily loop before sizing the battery.
  • Favor the TZ5Y where climbs are long and loads are maximal.
  • Favor the TZ3V where cycles are short and grades are moderate.
  • Use regen data to tune the pack size to actual duty, not brochure range.
  • Revisit the mix each year as tonnage and routes evolve.

Operators who measure rather than estimate consistently find the sweet spot between the two platforms, and many end up with a blended fleet: TZ3V units for construction and aggregate, TZ5Y units for the pit. That blend, supported by the shared CATL LFP pack and common charging, is the most capital-efficient way to electrify a Nigerian site without over-specifying any single truck. The mistake is committing to one class for every lane, which either strands capacity or wastes capital on payload that never gets sold.

Price Tiers and Total Cost

FOB pricing scales with battery and chassis class. The TZ3V sits in the USD 70k to 120k dump band, while the TZ5Y heavy mining unit reaches up to USD 180k. The decision hinges on tons-per-day, not sticker price, because the larger truck's value is realized only when its extra capacity is sold every shift.

  • TZ3V: lower capital, lower energy per ton on mid loads, ideal for urban and quarry construction.
  • TZ5Y: higher capital, fewer cycles for the same daily tonnage, ideal for high-volume mining haul.
  • Both avoid diesel at USD 0.75 to 1.10 per liter and cut maintenance by removing the engine.
  • Both share the CATL LFP pack, simplifying spare parts and swap logistics.
  • Both qualify for the 8-year / 4500-cycle warranty that protects residual value.

Financing also differs between the two classes. The TZ5Y-style heavy unit often qualifies for mining-equipment leasing with usage-based terms, while the TZ3V fits standard commercial vehicle finance. Nigerian buyers should compare the all-in monthly cost, including the battery warranty, against the diesel alternative they are replacing, because the lower running cost is what makes the higher electric premium affordable month to month rather than as a one-time shock.

Operator Training and Safety

Moving from diesel tippers to electric dump trucks changes the skills the crew needs. There is no engine noise to signal load, so drivers must learn to read state-of-charge and torque gauges, and to use regenerative braking to hold the truck on grade without riding the friction brakes. A short, structured training program pays back quickly in battery life and incident avoidance.

  • Train on single-pedal driving to maximize regen and cut brake wear.
  • Teach grade management so the LvKong motor holds torque efficiently.
  • Brief crews on high-voltage isolation and emergency disconnect procedure.
  • Set charging etiquette: plug in every return, never deep-discharge.
  • Run a weekly walk-around focused on tires, lights and coolant loop.

Safety is also simpler on the electric truck because there is no diesel fuel, no hot exhaust, and no engine bay fire risk in the cab. Nigerian sites with dust and heat benefit from the sealed, low-maintenance driveline, and the quiet operation lets spotters communicate by voice rather than hand signal alone. The 8-year / 4500-cycle warranty is easier to honor when the operator follows the training, because most pack stress comes from abusive charging and extreme discharge, both of which good habits remove.

Verdict

Pick the TZ3V for flexible construction and quarry work where capital efficiency rules. Pick the TZ5Y when daily tonnage is high enough that fewer, heavier trips beat more, lighter ones. Either way, Nigerian operators gain an electric truck with the torque, warranty and charging flexibility to replace diesel tippers at scale while cutting fuel exposure in a volatile diesel market.
