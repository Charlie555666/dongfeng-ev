Writing the RFP That Buys the Right EV Truck: Fleet Buyer's Tender Specification Guide

We answer hundreds of tenders a year from our side of the table, and the difference between a good and bad electric truck RFP is worth more than any negotiation: the good ones arrive with duty-cycle data and evaluation criteria that let the best truck win; the bad ones specify brochure numbers and end up buying the most confident bidder. This article is written for the buyer's side — the municipal procurement officer, the state logistics manager, the private fleet's category buyer — on how to structure a tender for electric trucks that produces comparable, evaluable bids. It uses a municipal sanitation fleet built around the KT3E electric garbage truck as the running example, with market context from our Philippines electric truck market guide, where public EV procurement is active.

Start With Duty Data, Not Vehicle Spec

The most valuable section of any fleet RFP is the one only the buyer can write: the operating profile. Bidders can read specs from catalogues; only the fleet knows its routes:

  • Route inventory: lengths, gradients, stop counts, surface quality — the inputs that determine battery sizing
  • Shift structure: hours per vehicle per day, one or two shifts, depot dwell windows (when charging can happen)
  • Climate envelope: the temperatures vehicles must start and operate in, from the record low to the summer peak
  • Payload reality: actual measured tonnage or volume per route, not the theoretical maximum
  • Infrastructure baseline: site electrical capacity, available space, grid reliability profile

An RFP that states "120 km routes, 8 hours, 2-shift, 38 °C peak, 14 t payload, 400 A site capacity" will receive battery, charger and pricing responses that are directly comparable. One that states "supply 20 electric garbage trucks" will receive a spread of interpretations that no evaluation matrix can untangle.

Evaluation Criteria: How to Weight the Decision

A weighting scheme we see work well for public EV truck tenders:

  • Technical compliance (30–40%): pass/fail gates on the hard clauses, scored on duty-cycle fit
  • Total cost of ownership (25–35%): evaluated over 8 years — purchase price, energy at the buyer's actual tariffs, maintenance, charger amortisation, residual value assumptions stated
  • Delivery and implementation (10–15%): lead time, commissioning plan, training programme
  • After-sales substance (10–15%): parts logistics, technician availability, warranty administration process
  • Reference operations (5–10%): comparable fleets running the offered platform in similar climates and duty

The critical discipline: evaluate energy cost at the buyer's own tariff, not the bidder's assumed one. The spread between bidders on realistic energy math is usually where the actual winner emerges.

Common RFP Mistakes, Seen From the Supply Side

  1. Specifying a diesel brochure with "electric" substituted. RFPs that demand engine horsepower equivalents, gear counts or tank sizes translated into kWh produce confused bids and wrong trucks.
  2. Over-specifying range. Doubled battery requirements double the capital cost and cut payload, usually to solve a charging problem that a charger placement solves more cheaply. Let bidders propose the charging architecture against your duty data instead.
  3. Ignoring the site electrical reality. The RFP should include the site's capacity numbers; otherwise every bidder assumes infinite power and the first grid study after award produces the tender's first variation order.
  4. One-year price comparison. Electric trucks lose bad tenders on purchase price and win good ones on eighth-year cost. The evaluation structure literally decides which truck is cheaper.
  5. No language, documentation and training requirements stated. For most of our export markets, the winning bid must include local-language manuals and certification-level training — but only if the RFP asks.

The Buyer's Due Diligence Pack

Beyond the tender document itself, buyers who get good outcomes ask finalists for: a reference fleet contact in a comparable climate and duty cycle; the battery supplier's warranty instrument itself; the diagnostic software demonstrated live on a vehicle; a commissioning plan with dates; and the parts price list for five years of scheduled consumables. Any bidder hesitant on those five items is telling the buyer something the evaluation matrix would otherwise conceal — and any bidder forthcoming on all of them, like our team with the KT3E-class sanitation platform's reference operations, is showing the buyer what a smooth fleet deployment actually looks like.

The Timeline and the Team: Executing the Tender Properly

A well-structured EV truck tender is a project with a team and a calendar, not a document with a deadline. The calendar that works: four to six weeks from kickoff to issue for the duty-cycle audit and specification drafting (the audit being the long pole — route telematics, shift schedules, site electrical data); a bidder window of four to eight weeks depending on fleet value and market depth, with a mandatory clarification round at the midpoint; a two-to-three-week technical evaluation including reference-fleet calls and a site visit or vehicle demonstration where volume justifies it; and commercial evaluation against the buyer's own tariffs and the TCO model the RFP mandated. From issue to award: three to five months, and the discipline that keeps it honest is refusing to let the schedule compress the audit — every week saved on the duty-cycle data reappears as an ambiguity dispute in evaluation.

The team that works: a procurement lead, an operations voice (the fleet manager who owns the routes), a technical evaluator (the workshop or engineering lead who reads the specification answers), and — the role most often missing — a finance member who runs the TCO model with the buyer's own tariffs and residual assumptions. Public-sector tenders add legal and publication requirements that vary by jurisdiction; the RFP structure in this article slots into them without distortion because its substance — duty data, evaluation criteria, thresholds — is jurisdiction-neutral.

And the closing observation from the supplier side of hundreds of these processes: the tenders that produce the best fleets are not the ones with the most pages but the ones with the clearest numbers. Buyers who know their routes, their tariffs and their thresholds get bids that compete on engineering; everyone else gets bids that compete on adjectives.
