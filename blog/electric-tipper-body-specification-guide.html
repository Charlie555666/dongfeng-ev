Specifying the Tipper Body on an Electric Chassis: The Complete Guide for EV Truck Buyers

On a diesel tipper, the body is a steel box bolted to a frame — specified by volume, steel grade and habit. On an electric tipper, the body specification becomes an energy and payload decision, because every kilogram of body steel is a kilogram not carried as payload, and every hoist cycle is a draw on the same battery that moves the truck. Getting the body right on an electric dump truck like the KTA1 or the 8x4 TZ3Z is where the electric advantage is either compounded or quietly squandered. This guide covers the five decisions in a tipper body specification — material, volume, hoist system, weight engineering and duty matching — with the electric-chassis physics that changes each one. Decision One: Body Material and the Payload Budget

The core electric-chassis arithmetic: the battery already spends 1.5–2.5 t of the payload budget that a diesel truck's fuel system never approached. The body must claw that weight back:

OptionWeight, 16 m³ classWhere it wins
Mild steel (St52 class)~4.2–4.6 tLight-duty, fine materials, lowest first cost
Hardox 450 floor + St52 walls~3.8–4.1 tThe balanced spec: abrasion where it matters, weight saved overall
Hardox 450 floor + Hardox 450 walls~3.5–3.9 tQuarry and demolition duty; higher initial cost, longer life
High-strength lightweight (Hardox HiAce-class thin plate)~3.0–3.4 tWeight-critical electric conversions; lighter materials duty

The rule we apply: on an electric tipper, a 500 kg lighter body is 500 kg of payload, every single load, every day — and at typical aggregate freight rates that weight saving pays for the premium steel inside the first operating year. Specify the abrasion grade by material, but specify the weight class by the payload budget.

Decision Two: Volume Against Legal Payload

Body volume and payload must be matched to the material density and the axle-law reality:

  • Aggregates (1.5–1.7 t/m³): a KTA1-class truck with ~18 t legal payload wants a 11–12 m³ body; the 8x4 TZ3Z at ~20–25 t payload wants 14–16 m³. Oversizing "for flexibility" just tempts overload fines and unbalances the axles.
  • Lighter materials — soil, mulch, recycled (1.0–1.3 t/m³): the payload law allows bigger volumes; 18–20 m³ on the 8x4 is legitimate and productive.
  • Wet excavation and sticky clay: specify polished floor plate and a steep tail angle (45°+ for full clean discharge) — sticky carryback is dead payload that accumulates load by load.

On electric duty the volume decision carries an extra rider: aerodynamic and cross-wind effects of tall bodies measurably affect consumption at highway speeds on corridor hauls — keep the body inside the legal envelope, not merely the physical one.

Decision Three: The Hoist System — Where Electric Changes the Physics

On a diesel tipper, the hoist runs off the engine PTO at idle rpm — slow, noisy and fuel-punishing. On the TZ3Z electric platform, the e-PTO drives the hydraulic pump from the traction battery at controlled speed, which changes the specification targets:

  1. Raise time: spec 18–30 s full raise depending on body size. Electric drive makes the fast end of that band routine — genuinely useful on high-cycle quarry duty where a 10-second-per-hoist saving across 30 cycles a day compounds.
  2. Energy per cycle: budget 0.6–1.2 kWh per hoist cycle depending on load and body. That is 8–18 km of driving energy across a 15-hoist day — real, and worth training dispatchers to include in route energy models.
  3. Power stability: electric hoisting holds pressure steady regardless of engine speed — gentler on the hydraulics, longer pump and seal life, smoother under a heaped sticky load.
  4. Lowering: specify power-lowering (metered descent) rather than free-fall gravity valves on any duty where precision discharge matters.

Decision Four: Body-Chassis Integration

The body is only as good as its marriage to the frame:

  • Sub-frame and pivot geometry: the hoist's pivot point and the body's centre of mass must sit within the chassis engineers' envelope — on battery-chassis frames this is engineered at the factory and must not be modified by local fabricators.
  • Rear overhang: longer overhang increases tipping stability risk and tail swing in city work; keep it inside the manufacturer's dimensioned limit.
  • Body lighting and covers: LED body lighting and roll-over covers/tarps are cheap adds at build time and expensive retrofits; covers particularly matter where night-haulage and environmental rules apply.
  • Wear parts as a kit: floor bolts, hinge bushes, tailgate seals — order a wear kit with the body, because floor bolts specifically loosen and must be re-torqued on schedule (part of the HV-adjacent maintenance regime, since a shifting body threatens the pack's frame-rail real estate).

The Specification Sheet, Assembled

What we ask buyers to nail down before a tipper order — and what our factory configurations deliver as standard on Dongfeng electric chassis: body volume and material by duty; steel grades by abrasion; hoist raise time and power-lowering; body weight within the payload budget; sub-frame to factory drawing; wear-parts kit; LED lighting and cover; and the e-PTO energy budget folded into the route model. Where a local fabricator builds the body on our chassis, we supply the mounting dimensions, weight limits and integration drawing — the one document whose absence causes every integration horror story we have been asked to repair.

The body is where an electric tipper's economics are finalised: a well-specified body makes the electric payload penalty disappear and adds a fast, smooth, silent hoist; a badly specified one gives back both advantages in steel and kWh. Specify it with the same rigour as the battery — your fleet's P&L cannot tell the difference between a kilogram of battery and a kilogram of over-thick floor plate, and neither should your tender document.

The Order Conversation, Condensed

When a buyer sits down with us to specify an electric tipper, the productive conversation is a checklist of the five decisions in this guide, taken in order and written into the order file. What material moves, in what density, at what legal payload — that fixes volume and steel. What the duty does to the body — quarry abrasion, sticky clays, night city work — that fixes the Hardox grade and the tail geometry. What the route's energy model says the hoist will cost — that fixes the e-PTO specification and folds into the range calculation the battery is sized against. What the axle-load sheet says — that fixes the sub-frame and overhang limits within which any fabricator must work. And what the wear-kit and integration drawing say — that decides whether year five of the fleet is routine maintenance or an escalating negotiation. Five decisions, one page each, and the body that arrives is an asset rather than a compromise.

Steel and kWh are both bought by the kilogram. The fleet that buys both well simply carries more of what pays.
