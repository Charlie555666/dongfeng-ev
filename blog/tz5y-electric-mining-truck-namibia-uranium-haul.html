The Uranium Haul Profile

Namibian uranium haulage runs ore from pit face to crusher or leach-pad stockpiles on private haul roads of 3-10 km, with grades of 6-10% and controlled speeds. The defining advantage for electrification: the loaded run descends from many of these pits, letting regenerative braking recover 20-30% of cycle energy, while the empty climb back consumes a fraction of loaded power. Measured cycle energy on comparable southern African operations runs 3.8-4.5 kWh/km loaded and 1.6-1.9 empty - a full double shift consumes roughly two 528 kWh pack equivalents, which is exactly what battery swap exists to serve.

Radiation-area discipline is standard at uranium operations, and an electric truck simplifies it: no diesel exhaust particulate mixing with ore dust in the pit air, no fuel handling in controlled zones, and far less heat rejection around processing areas.

The Battery Swap Model at Remote Mines

Diesel's hidden cost at Namibian mines is the fuel supply chain itself - tanker deliveries across the desert, on-site storage, spill management. Battery swap replaces it with electrons the mine often generates or wheels in anyway. The station model that works: one swap station per 4-6 trucks at the pit workshop, holding 2-3 charging packs per truck in rotation, grid-fed at night and able to integrate the solar arrays these mines are building. A swap takes 5-6 minutes - faster than a diesel refuel - and the charging packs replenish during the working shift. For pits without grid headroom, the TZ5E heavy electric dump truck offers a 65 t class fast-charge alternative on lighter duty.

  • Site the swap station at the pit exit weighbridge - every loaded truck passes it naturally.
  • Rotate three packs per two trucks; charging speed, not truck speed, sets fleet size.
  • Log kWh per tonne-hauled; uranium ore duty should hold 0.35-0.45 kWh/t-km on short loops.
  • Plan pack charging for off-peak or solar-peak hours; the swap model makes charging time flexible.

Desert Heat, Dust and Cold Nights

The Namib swings 25-30 C between day and night, with daytime peaks above 40 C and dust that finds every unsealed gap. The TZ5Y's liquid thermal management handles both directions - warming packs through cold pre-dawn starts and cooling them through afternoon haulage. IP67 sealing and high-capacity intake filtration address the dust; the maintenance schedule moves cooling-pack cleaning to weekly in the dry season. What the desert does not affect is motor power: no altitude or heat derating of the kind that cuts diesel output in these conditions.

Economics at the End of a Long Supply Chain

Diesel at remote Namibian mines carries delivered costs well above coastal prices, and every litre arrives by truck over the same desert roads. Electric energy - grid or wheeled solar - undercuts it by 60-70% per hour worked. Maintenance on the electric drivetrain halves the diesel figure, and the regen braking all but eliminates the brake wear that dominates haul-truck maintenance on descending pit roads. Swap Station Engineering Detail

The swap station is the system component buyers scrutinize hardest, and the engineering rewards it. The station's robotic frame exchanges a 528 kWh pack in 5-6 minutes, guided by the truck's alignment sensors - faster than a diesel refuel with documentation. The charging bank inside holds packs in rotation, charging at managed rates that extend pack life beyond fast-charge duty, with the thermal conditioning that desert service demands. Power supply designs for the site's reality: grid-fed where the mine's infrastructure allows, hybrid with the solar arrays these operations are building, with buffer storage smoothing both. Containerized construction ships complete and commissions in days; the station relocates by low-loader as the pit develops. Spare-pack inventory sizes at 1.5-2 packs per truck - the ratio that keeps trucks cycling while packs charge.
