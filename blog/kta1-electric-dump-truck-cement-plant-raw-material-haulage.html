KTA1 Electric Dump Truck in Cement Plants: Limestone, Clinker and Kiln Feed Haulage Economics

A cement works is a closed, continuous, high-tonnage material system, and it is one of the most compelling environments for electric dump trucks anywhere in industry. The hauls are short, the routes are fixed, the site already has heavy electrical infrastructure, and the material is abrasive enough that diesel fleets spend heavily on maintenance. The Dongfeng KTA1 electric dump truck — CATL 352 kWh LFP, LvKong 270 kW rated / 450 kW peak with a 4-speed AMT — is the mid-class tipper we most often deploy into cement and aggregates operations, and this article works through the three distinct haulage circuits inside a plant and how each one is specified.

Circuit 1: Quarry to Primary Crusher

This is the tonnage-defining haul. Characteristics that matter for an electric truck:

  • Loaded downhill is common. Many quarries sit above the crusher, meaning the loaded leg descends and the empty leg climbs. Inverter-duty regenerative braking recovers 15–25% of descent energy and, more importantly, removes the service-brake heat load that destroys diesel dump truck brakes on the same road.
  • Energy per shift on a 3 km one-way circuit at 20–28 cycles lands at 170–260 kWh — comfortably inside the 352 kWh pack with reserve, and typically allowing a second shift before recharge if the operation runs a midday window.
  • Rolling resistance dominates. A well-maintained, watered quarry road at 0.02 rolling resistance versus a rutted road at 0.06 is a 30–40% difference in kWh per tonne. Keeping a grader and water bowser on the road is an energy decision.

Circuit 2: Stockpile and Pre-Blend Shuttle

Short, continuous, high-cycle work is where the electric drivetrain is most clearly superior to diesel:

  1. Stop-start with no fuel penalty. A diesel tipper idling in a loader queue burns 2–4 litres per hour doing nothing. An electric tipper burns only what the auxiliaries draw.
  2. No clutch or gearbox wear. Twenty-five cycles per shift means twenty-five full stop-start events; that is the wear mode that puts diesel dump trucks in the workshop.
  3. Predictable energy. A 1.5 km shuttle at 30+ cycles uses 140–220 kWh per shift — one overnight charge, or one opportunity charge at the shift change for two-shift operation.

Circuit 3: Clinker and Hot Material

Clinker transfer is the hardest circuit and the one requiring explicit specification:

  • Material temperature. Clinker leaving the cooler can still be 100–200 °C. Body specification must account for it: heat-resistant floor plate and, in some designs, an insulating layer. Confirm the temperature profile before specifying.
  • Density. Clinker at roughly 1.3–1.5 t/m³ and gypsum at 1.2–1.6 t/m³ mean a body sized for volume may overload; size by mass, not volume.
  • Dust sealing. Cement kiln dust is fine, alkaline and pervasive. Sealed tailgates, sealed electrical connectors and enhanced filtration are not optional — they are the difference between a fleet that runs and one that spends its life in the workshop.

Charging at a Cement Plant

Cement plants are unusually easy to electrify from an infrastructure standpoint:

  • Existing HV/MV infrastructure. Most works have a substantial substation and in-house electrical engineers. Charger connection is an internal project, not a utility negotiation.
  • Waste heat and solar opportunities. Several operators we work with are evaluating solar canopies over parking and charging areas; see our solar-hybrid charging guide.
  • Continuous operation. Where trucks run 24 hours, battery swap at 5–6 minutes per exchange removes charging from the critical path entirely — covered in swap station deployment.
  • Dust-rated enclosures. Specify charger enclosures and connectors to the site's dust environment. This is the most commonly missed item in cement plant installations.
