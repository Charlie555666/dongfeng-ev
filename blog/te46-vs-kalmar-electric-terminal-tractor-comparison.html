Dongfeng TE46 vs Kalmar T2E: Electric Terminal Tractor Comparison for Port Container Yards

Port container yards are the most operationally demanding duty cycle an EV truck will ever face: 24/7 shifts, constant stop-start shuttle between quay and stacking blocks, trailers loaded to 40+ tonnes, and zero tolerance for a tractor that cannot turn a wheel because of a charging queue. Buyers comparing the Dongfeng TE46 against the Kalmar T2E electric terminal tractor are really asking one question — how much do you need to spend to keep a yard moving, and what does it cost to keep it moving for eight years? This comparison lays out the spec differences, the cost per container move, and the parts reality in Africa and the Middle East.

Why Port Operators Are Switching the Yard to Electric

The terminal tractor is the ideal electric-truck candidate. Routes are short and fixed, duty is repetitive, and the tractor never leaves the terminal fence — so range anxiety disappears and the truck can be recharged or battery-swapped on a predictable schedule. Diesel terminal tractors burn fuel idling at the blocks for most of a shift, while an electric tractor draws power only when it moves and recovers energy on every deceleration back to the stack. For a 50-tractor yard running three shifts, the energy bill alone is the difference between a line item and a footnote. The constraint is not the technology; it is the capital outlay and the spare-parts pipeline that keeps the fleet available.

Yard electrification also answers the emissions rules now appearing in port concessions. Many terminals face internal carbon targets and community noise limits that a diesel yard fleet cannot meet without expensive aftertreatment and curfew compromises. An electric tractor is silent at the block and produces no local exhaust where stevedores work. That compliance value is rarely on the diesel-versus-electric spreadsheet, but it is increasingly the reason the procurement is approved at all.

Battery Swap and Charging Reality

The TE46’s 5–6 minute battery swap is the decisive operational feature for a terminal that cannot afford a charging dwell:

  • Swap keeps every tractor on the floor; a depleted pack is exchanged, not waited on, so shift coverage stays at 100%.
  • Opportunity charging at 20–80% in 35–60 minutes covers lower-intensity blocks without a swap station.
  • Shared swap packs mean a yard buys fewer total kWh than one-pack-per-tractor ownership, lowering capital tied up in cells.
  • LFP chemistry tolerates the high state of charge and heat of a tropical port far better than nickel-rich packs.

The swap model also smooths the peak-power demand of the terminal. Instead of forty tractors arriving to fast-charge simultaneously at shift change, the energy is transferred to packs at the swap station on a managed schedule, which cuts the site’s transformer and demand-charge exposure. For a port already constrained on incoming power, that load-shaping alone can decide whether electrification is feasible without a substation upgrade.

Deployment Considerations for Yard Buyers

Before specifying the fleet, operators should settle four practical points. First, confirm the block spacing and gradient: both tractors handle standard terminal grades, but the TE46’s LvKong permanent-magnet motor delivers strong low-speed torque that suits the constant pull-away from the quay. Second, decide swap versus charge: yards above ~30 tractors on continuous duty benefit most from a swap station, while smaller fleets can live on depot charging alone. Third, train the maintenance team on high-voltage isolation rather than engine service — the skill set is smaller, not larger. Fourth, model the energy on the actual tariff and shift pattern, not a generic figure, because the per-move economics swing on local power cost.

Most large terminals combine both: swap for peak shifts, opportunity charging for the rest.
