Uzbekistan's Cotton Belt Goes Electric: KTH3 and KT5L Electric Truck Strategy from Gin to Mill

Uzbekistan is one of the world's largest cotton producers and, increasingly, an exporter of textile value rather than raw fibre. The government's industrial policy has pushed processing onshore: gins across the Ferghana Valley, the Zarafshan belt and Kashkadarya feed spinning and weaving clusters in Namangan, Andijan, Kokand, Bukhara and the Tashkent region, and the truck lanes that connect them run the same routes every season. This is a commodity corridor with the shape electric trucks love: fixed nodes, known distances, seasonal peaks over a year-round base load. We have covered Tashkent city logistics and the national cargo market; this piece is the cotton sector strategy, built on the KTH3 8x4 electric cargo truck for the fibre trunk legs and the KT5L electric cargo truck for the lighter seed, input and textile distribution work. The Cotton Truck Calendar

The sector's logistics run in three overlapping phases:

  • Haraship (September–November): raw seed cotton moving from field collection points to gins — the year's peak freight intensity, 30–80 km legs at full axle loads.
  • Ginning and fibre evacuation (October–February): baled lint moving from gins to textile mills, spinning clusters and export railheads — 100–400 km legs of high-density cargo.
  • Year-round textile and input logistics: yarn and fabric to domestic and export customers, seed cake to feed mills, fertilizer and chemicals out to the next season's fields.

A fleet specified for the harvest peak is underutilised in spring — unless it is electric, where the low cost of a quiet month and the flexibility to redeploy onto city distribution (as we described for Tashkent) make seasonal dips painless. Diesel trucks idle into corrosion and fuel-system problems; LFP packs hold charge for months with minimal loss.

Economics at Uzbek Tariffs

Uzbekistan's industrial electricity is cheap — USD 0.04–0.06/kWh — and diesel is market-priced. On cotton corridor duty the spread is dramatic:

  • Diesel 8x4 on gin-to-mill duty: roughly USD 0.36/km fuel + USD 0.10/km maintenance at 45,000 km/year.
  • KTH3 on the same duty: roughly USD 0.05–0.06/km energy + USD 0.05/km maintenance.
  • Annual saving: about USD 16,000–17,000 per truck — payback on the CAPEX premium inside 24 months.
  • KT5L on distribution duty at 35,000 km/year: saving of USD 11,000–12,000 per truck per year.

For a cotton cluster running twenty trucks, that is a USD 300,000-per-year operating advantage over a diesel competitor — on corridors where gin-to-mill rates are set by negotiation rather than public tender, the electric operator can price to win volume and still widen its margin.

Winter Ginning Season: Cold Weather Engineering

The fibre evacuation peak falls in the cold months, so the cold-climate specification is not optional. Our Uzbek-spec units carry liquid battery thermal management with heating, heat-pump cab heating (about a third of resistive heating's energy draw), and a depot protocol that keeps trucks plugged in overnight so grid energy — not battery energy — holds pack temperature. Real-world winter consumption on corridor duty runs 15–20% above rated, which the KTH3's range absorbs comfortably on legs under 250 km. Charging Along the Corridor

Uzbekistan's cotton geography clusters into a handful of corridors: the Ferghana Valley spine, the Zarafshan (Samarkand–Navoi–Bukhara) belt, and the Kashkadarya cluster around Karshi. A minimal national network electrifies all three:

  1. Gin-yard chargers: 120 kW DC points at participating gins — gins are industrial power customers, and their harvest-season demand profile (heavy October–December, light after) suits hosting charger capacity that trucks use hardest in exactly the same months.
  2. Mill-cluster chargers: 240 kW fast points at the spinning clusters of Namangan, Kokand and Bukhara, shared across operators.
  3. Depot base: overnight 120 kW charging at the operator's regional depot.

A corridor fleet of fifteen trucks needs roughly USD 200,000–260,000 of infrastructure, repaid inside 18 months by the fuel line alone at Uzbek tariffs.
