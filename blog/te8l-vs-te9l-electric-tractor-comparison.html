TE8L vs TE9L Electric Tractor Comparison: Port Drayage or Regional Haul

Choosing between the TE8L electric tractor and the TE9L electric tractor comes down to duty cycle. Both are 6x4 battery-electric prime movers built on the same Dongfeng EV platform, but they target different jobs: the TE8L is a 424 kWh port drayage and city distribution tractor, while the TE9L is a 600 kWh option engineered for longer regional haul and heavier gross combination weight. This comparison lays out the battery, motor, range, charging and price differences so fleet buyers can match the right EV truck to real routes rather than guessing from a spec sheet.

Platform and Cab: What the Two Tractors Share

Both tractors use a CATL lithium-iron-phosphate (LFP) pack that is liquid-cooled and rated for an 8-year or 4,500-cycle warranty, whichever comes first. Both draw on LvKong drive motors and a common 6x4 chassis with air suspension, disc brakes and a reinforced fifth wheel. The shared DNA means spare parts, service training and charging hardware carry across your fleet even if you run both models side by side. The headline difference is energy: the TE8L carries 424 kWh, the TE9L offers a 600 kWh option for longer legs and heavier loads.

  • Common LFP chemistry and liquid cooling loop
  • Shared 8-year or 4,500-cycle battery warranty
  • Identical DC fast charge connector and depot charger spec
  • Same cab, mirrors, instrument cluster and telematics gateway
  • Container-friendly dimensions for overseas shipment

Battery and Motor: 424 kWh versus 600 kWh

The TE8L pairs its 424 kWh pack with a 360 kW LvKong motor, enough for 250 to 300 km of real-world drayage work between charges. The TE9L steps up to a 510 kW motor and the larger 600 kWh pack, extending practical range to 300 to 350 km and raising available torque for grade climbs and full loads. For terminal-to-warehouse shuttles under 200 km per shift, the smaller pack saves capital and curb weight. For multi-drop regional runs, the extra 176 kWh is what keeps the EV truck from needing a midday charge stop.

Both packs use the same CATL cells and LvKong inverter family, so a charger rated for the TE8L also serves the TE9L, and a spare motor fits either truck. This commonality reduces the capital you tie up in spares and lets one technician service the mixed fleet. The 600 kWh option on the TE9L is built from the same module format as the 424 kWh pack, simply with more modules in series and parallel, so thermal management and warranty terms stay consistent across both models.

Range and Duty Cycle: Drayage versus Regional Haul

Port drayage is the TE8L's home turf. Containers move 20 to 80 km between terminal and inland depot, often with long idle periods that suit opportunity charging during breaks. The TE9L targets regional haul of 250 to 350 km per leg where a bigger buffer protects against traffic, payload and air-conditioning draw. Both trucks recover energy through regenerative braking, which is especially valuable on the stop-start drayage loop where every deceleration returns charge to the pack and extends effective range.

Charging Strategy for Each Model

Both tractors accept DC fast charging at 120, 240 or 360 kW, reaching 20 to 80 percent in roughly 45 to 90 minutes depending on charger power and pack temperature. The TE8L is ideal for depot overnight charging plus a 240 kW opportunity top-up at the port gate. The TE9L benefits from a 360 kW charger on regional corridors so a driver can add about 300 km of range during a regulated break. Plan charger capacity to the larger pack if you operate both, so the yard never bottlenecks the TE9L during a busy shift.

For fleets running mixed shifts, a smart charger that throttles by site limit keeps the depot under its demand cap while still preparing every truck. Pair the chargers with a buffer battery of 500 kWh to 1 MWh and you can charge both models off-peak, then discharge to the trucks during the expensive evening window. That combination is what turns an EV truck depot from a cost center into a controlled, predictable line item on the monthly bill.

Price and Total Cost of Ownership

FOB pricing puts the TE8L at $95,000 to $130,000 and the TE9L at $135,000 to $180,000. The $40,000 to $50,000 gap buys roughly 40 percent more energy and 42 percent more motor power. Over a five-year operating cycle, lower per-km electricity cost versus diesel, plus reduced brake wear from regen, usually closes the premium faster on high-utilization units. Maintenance, Brake Wear and Uptime

Electric tractors lower service cost because regenerative braking handles most deceleration, so friction brakes and tires outlast diesel equivalents by two to three times. The TE8L and TE9L share brake pads, suspension parts and cabin filters, which keeps the parts shelf small. The battery and motor need almost no routine service beyond coolant checks and over-the-air software updates. Fleets report brake life two to three times longer than diesel, a quiet saving that pays part of the purchase premium across the warranty window.

The liquid-cooled LFP pack is sealed and watched by the battery management system, which flags any cell drift before it becomes a roadside fault. Because both tractors use one architecture, a single diagnostic tool and one training course cover the whole fleet. Downtime falls to scheduled maintenance windows rather than breakdowns, exactly what drayage and regional operators need to protect on-time delivery rates.

Safety, Driver Comfort and Telematics

Both cabs give low-noise, low-vibration driving that cuts fatigue on long regional shifts, with full air suspension and disc brakes for stable stops under heavy trailers. The telematics gateway streams state of charge, position, energy use and fault codes to your depot, so dispatchers plan charges from real data instead of guesses. Drivers like instant torque for smooth merging and the lack of diesel fumes at the dock, which also helps terminals meet tightening air-quality rules.

Resale Value and Battery Second Life

After the 8-year or 4,500-cycle warranty, an LFP pack still holds 70 to 80 percent capacity and carries second-life value of roughly $20 to $40 per kWh in stationary storage. That residual softens the eventual replacement cost, which runs about $90 to $130 per kWh at the pack level. Trucks themselves hold value well where charging infrastructure has matured, because operators pay a premium for proven, low-mileage electric tractors. Factoring battery second life into your business case turns end-of-warranty from a liability into a managed line item.

Which Tractor Should Your Fleet Buy?

Buy the TE8L when your lanes are under 250 km, your depot can charge overnight, and you want the lowest capital entry into electric trucking. Buy the TE9L when regional haul exceeds 300 km per day, payload and grade demand the 510 kW motor, or you need the range buffer to absorb unpredictable schedules. Many fleets run both: TE8L units on drayage, TE9L units on line-haul, sharing one charging yard and one parts bin. Confirm local gross combination weight limits, charger plugs and paperwork before signing, then standardize on the tractor that fits your highest-mileage lane first.
