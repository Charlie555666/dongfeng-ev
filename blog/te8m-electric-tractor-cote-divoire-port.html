Abidjan Port Drayage Duty Cycle

Port drayage is the textbook electric-tractor application. A tractor picks a loaded container at the terminal, runs 10-40 km to the inland depot or warehouse, drops, and returns empty or with an export box - 10-20 cycles per day on fixed, repeatable loops. The loads are heavy, the distances are short, and the terminal is the natural energy node. Three facts make Abidjan ideal, and they line up with where global port policy is heading.

  • Terminal-anchored loops: the port and dry port already have heavy power; charging or swapping there costs little to provision and keeps the operation self-contained.
  • Continuous shifts: drayage runs dawn to late; a single charge rarely covers a full shift, so swap capability matters more than pack size alone.
  • Zero-emission zones: ports worldwide are moving toward low-emission terminal access, and the electric tractor is ahead of that regulation rather than scrambling to meet it.
  • Fixed routes: the predictable terminal-to-depot pattern lets the operator size the swap pool exactly to peak demand.

The Battery-Swap Operating Model

Abidjan drayage runs long shifts, and a 1.5-2 hour charge is dead time a port operator cannot afford. The TE8M's swap chassis turns energy replenishment into a 5-6 minute pack exchange at a terminal swap station, so the same tractor runs continuously across two shifts. The 8-year / 4500-cycle LFP warranty applies per pack, and a pooled-swap model lets a fleet right-size the number of packs to peak demand rather than buying a giant pack per tractor. This is the model that makes electric drayage economically obvious at port scale, because the asset utilization stays high instead of parking on a charger.

Charging and Swap Infrastructure

For operators who prefer plug charging, a 180-240 kW DC charger at the depot or terminal returns 20-80 percent in about two hours, covering a single-shift pattern. For continuous operation, a swap station with 4-8 packs serves a fleet of 10-20 tractors. Either way the energy node is the terminal, so no public infrastructure is required, and a charge scheduler prevents the site supply from being overloaded when several units draw together.

Maintenance and Terminal Uptime

The electric tractor removes the diesel engine, turbo, gearbox and exhaust aftertreatment from the most stop-start duty in trucking. Scheduled service focuses on brakes, tires, the fifth wheel and the high-voltage inspection. Regenerative braking on the empty return leg extends brake life, and the quiet cab improves yard safety around pedestrians and stacking equipment. Spare packs in a swap pool also mean a degraded pack is swapped out rather than taking a tractor offline.

Fleet Rollout and Total Cost of Ownership

The practical rollout for an Abidjan drayage operator is to start with a swap-enabled TE8M pair on the busiest terminal-to-depot lane, prove the 5-6 minute swap rhythm against the diesel baseline, then scale the swap pool as more tractors convert. Because the energy node is the terminal, there is no public-infrastructure dependency, and the visible benefit - zero terminal emissions and quieter night gates - aligns with where global port access rules are heading, protecting the operator's license to operate as zones tighten.

On total cost, the TE8M at 0.9-1.3 kWh/km is far cheaper than a diesel prime mover at roughly USD 0.28-0.38 per kilometer, and removing the engine, turbo, gearbox and exhaust aftertreatment cuts the yard workshop load. Even after amortizing the FOB premium of USD 90,000-150,000 and a swap station or 240 kW charger, most high-utilization drayage fleets recover the gap within two to three years of continuous shifts, and the per-pack 8-year / 4500-cycle warranty covers the swap pool's life. The pooled-swap model also means a degraded pack is exchanged rather than taking a tractor offline, which protects asset utilization that a plug-only fleet can lose to charging downtime.

Residual value holds because a swap-cabinet tractor is never stranded by a weak pack; the pack is a shared, managed asset. For fleet managers the metric is cost per container moved, and on the dense Abidjan loops the electric tractor wins once the swap pool is sized to peak demand rather than to one pack per tractor. Financing is helped by the immediate, measurable energy saving and by port sustainability mandates that increasingly favor low-emission terminal access for concession holders.

Drayage operators should log container moves and energy together, because the two define the real productivity of a terminal tractor. A telematics layer that tracks boxes moved, kWh drawn and swap counts lets a terminal manager show the concession holder a clean cost-per-container figure that justifies expanding the swap pool. The TE8M's zero terminal emissions also de-risk access as ports tighten low-emission rules, a strategic benefit beyond the per-kilometer energy saving. As the swap station serves more tractors, the per-unit pack overhead falls and the payback shortens further, making the electric fleet the default for new Abidjan drayage contracts.

Operators should also confirm terminal gate access for the swap chassis, which needs a short approach lane at the swap station; most Abidjan yards accommodate this within the existing trailer park, and the payoff is continuous two-shift running with no charger downtime. The swap cabinet is weather-sealed for the humid coastal climate and serviced on a simple scheduled interval.
