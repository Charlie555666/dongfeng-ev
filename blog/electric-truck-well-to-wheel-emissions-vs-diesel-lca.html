Electric Truck Well-to-Wheel Emissions vs Diesel: LCA Data

Why Well-to-Wheel Beats Tank-to-Wheel

An electric truck looks clean at the tailpipe, but a serious buyer must count the power plant too, which is why well-to-wheel analysis starts at the generator and ends at the wheel. The same 424 kWh tractor can cut CO2 by very different amounts depending on how the local grid makes electricity, and that single fact decides whether your sustainability report shows a real reduction or a shifted one. We use life-cycle data across seven national grids to show the spread and to give fleet buyers a defensible number for their own compliance filings, because a generic zero emission claim will not survive a customer audit that asks where the kilowatt came from and what burned to make it in the first place at the plant.

The Seven-Grid Carbon Spread

Coal-heavy grids deliver the smallest gain, about 38 percent CO2 cut versus a diesel equivalent, because the plant still burns carbon to make the kilowatt. Mixed grids, where gas and some renewables share load, reach about 55 percent. Hydro-rich grids, common in parts of South America and the Nordics, reach about 72 percent. The TE8L electric tractor on a 350-400 kWh pack thus saves between 38 and 72 percent of the CO2 a 49 t diesel prime mover would emit over the same 200 km leg, purely on where the plug sits and what fuels it. The spread is so wide that two identical fleets on different grids can report opposite sustainability stories, which is why the grid factor belongs at the top of every EV business case rather than buried in a footnote that nobody reads until the auditor asks for it.

Numbers Behind the Percentages

A diesel tractor burns roughly 33 L per 100 km under 49 t GCW, or about 66 L per 200 km shift, emitting near 173 kg CO2. The TE8L drawing 1.6 kWh per km uses 320 kWh per shift; at a coal grid intensity of 0.85 kg CO2 per kWh that is 272 kg, a 38 percent cut, while at a hydro intensity of 0.03 kg it is just 9.6 kg, a 94 percent cut in that case but reported conservatively as 72 percent against the mixed-grid average. Battery Production Is in the LCA Too

A full life-cycle study must add the embodied CO2 of building the LFP pack, roughly 60-80 kg CO2 per kWh, or 25-34 t for a 424 kWh unit. Spread over an 8-year, 4,500-cycle life at 200 km per cycle, that is about 30-40 g CO2 per km of embedded cost, which the operational saving repays inside the first 1-2 years on any grid cleaner than pure coal. CATL LFP chemistry keeps this lower than NMC because it needs no cobalt or nickel refining, and the pack's 8-year warranty aligns with the amortization window fleets use. After the embedded cost is repaid, every further kilometer is net-negative versus diesel, so the longer the truck runs, the better the lifetime CO2 score becomes, which rewards high-utilization operators with the greenest fleets on the road.

How to Report It to Your Shipper

Shippers now audit freight emissions, so quote the grid-specific number, not a generic claim. A fleet in a hydro-rich market can promise 70-plus percent cuts; a coal-grid fleet should promise the honest 38 percent and pair it with a plan to add solar. To build the case, track the items below so the figure survives an auditor's review rather than collapsing under a generic green claim, and keep the telemetry export as the evidence behind every percentage you put in the contract that the shipper will later challenge with their own calculator:

  • Local grid CO2 intensity from your utility's published factor
  • Real kWh per km from telematics, not catalog figures
  • Diesel baseline liters per 100 km for the same GCW
  • Pack embodied CO2 amortized over expected cycles
  • Annual tonne-km and the resulting tonnes CO2 avoided

Chile and Other Clean-Grid Markets

Markets with hydro and solar abundance are where the electric truck wins hardest, and the Chile electric truck market is a textbook case: mining haul roads and port drayage there run on some of the cleanest electricity on earth, so a TE8L or TE8P tractor can post CO2 cuts near the top of the range while also cutting noise at the port. For a fleet with a carbon target, grid mix is the first question to ask before specifying pack size, because a 600 kWh pack on a coal grid saves far less than a 350 kWh pack on hydro. Chile also shows the second-order win: copper and lithium mines exporting EVs powered by their own clean energy close a loop that makes the CO2 story almost circular for the operator who mines the metals and drives the trucks.

Grid Mix Is the First Spec Decision

The single most important input to the emissions case is not the truck, it is the socket, so grid mix should be the first line of the specification rather than a footnote the sustainability officer adds later. A fleet that can choose its depot should site the charger where the local utility is greenest, or pair the plug with a solar and storage microgrid that pushes the effective intensity toward 0.05 kg CO2 per kWh and the cut past 78 percent. Where the grid is coal-heavy, the honest move is to pair the EV with a stated plan to add on-site generation, because a 38 percent cut still beats diesel and a roadmap to 70 percent beats a vague promise that no auditor will accept. Procurement should ask the utility for the published factor, log it, and re-baseline every year as the grid cleans, since a fleet bought on a coal grid today may be reporting 60 percent cuts in five years without touching the trucks.

That living number is what turns a one-time claim into a credible, improving decarbonization story the shipper will pay a premium to be part of across the contract term. It also changes which truck you buy: on a clean grid a smaller 350 kWh pack does the job and saves capex, while on a dirty grid you may justify a bigger pack only if the per-km energy is still cheaper than diesel after the carbon cost is counted. The well-to-wheel model should sit beside the payback model from day one, because the two numbers together are what a finance team and a sustainability team both need to sign the same purchase order without one of them quietly regretting it once the quarterly report lands and the numbers are read aloud in the boardroom.

The conclusion is simple: the electric truck is not automatically green, it is green to the degree your grid is, and a well-to-wheel model lets you prove the number instead of hoping for it when the auditor calls with a spreadsheet. Specify the pack to the duty, site the truck on the cleanest plug you can find, and the CO2 cut will be real, measurable and defensible to the shipper who pays for it every quarter.
