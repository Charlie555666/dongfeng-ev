Why EV Fleets Need New Skills

An electric truck changes what a fleet's people need to know. Drivers manage energy instead of fuel, using regenerative braking and route planning to stretch every kilowatt-hour. Technicians work on high-voltage systems that demand certification and procedure, not just experience. Dispatchers plan around charging windows rather than fuel stops. None of this is difficult, but none of it is automatic either - fleets that treat training as a line item rather than an afterthought reach full electric productivity months faster than fleets that hand over keys and hope. For Vietnamese operators, structured training is the cheapest insurance on the entire electrification investment.

Driver Training Curriculum for the KT9X

A practical driver program for the KT9X electric concrete mixer fits into two days. Day one covers the vehicle: controls and displays, regenerative braking modes, range estimation and the effect of load, speed and climate on consumption, plus daily inspection routine. Day two is on-road coaching: energy-efficient driving technique, anticipation and regenerative braking use, hill strategy, and charging procedure at the depot. The payoff is immediate and measurable - trained drivers typically cut energy consumption 10-15% against untrained ones on identical routes, which flows straight into cost per kilometer. Technician High-Voltage Certification

Workshop staff need formal high-voltage qualification before touching anything beyond the 12V systems. The certification path runs from electrical safety fundamentals through isolation and lockout-tagout procedure, insulated tooling practice, and system-level diagnostics on the battery, motor and charging circuits. Level one authorizes supervised work; level two authorizes independent diagnosis and repair. Two certified technicians per shift is the sensible minimum for a working fleet. The training investment per technician is small against the risk it removes - and against the cost of trucking every fault to a third-party workshop. We organize factory-standard technician training for Vietnamese fleet customers, delivered on-site or through regional partners.

Charging Discipline and Energy Management

The least trained role in most fleets is the one that costs the most energy: whoever plugs the trucks in. Charging discipline means connecting on return to depot, following the planned charge window - off-peak where tariffs reward it - respecting the 20-80% fast-charging band for daily cycling, and logging exceptions. A simple charging roster prevents the peak-demand pileup that happens when every truck plugs in at shift change simultaneously. Fleet energy management software automates most of this once configured, but the human routine still decides whether the software's plan survives contact with a busy depot. One hour of training per driver, refreshed quarterly, holds the discipline.

Training Costs and Timeline in Vietnam

Budget training as a real project line, because it pays for itself. Driver programs run one to two days per driver in groups of eight to twelve; technician certification runs one to two weeks per person depending on level; dispatcher and energy-management training fits into a day. For a twenty-truck Vietnamese fleet, a complete initial program typically costs less than 1% of the fleet purchase value and completes inside the first month of operation. Driver Acceptance and Daily Operation

Driver acceptance decides whether an electric fleet reaches its modeled savings, and the KT9X earns acceptance quickly.
