How Much Does It Cost to Charge an Electric Truck? EV Truck Energy Cost Per km and Per kWh

Quick answer: At a typical commercial tariff of USD 0.10–0.14 per kWh, charging a Dongfeng electric truck costs USD 0.08–0.20 per km depending on duty cycle — about USD 0.09/km for a light box truck, USD 0.16/km for a 49 t tractor and USD 0.30/km for a loaded 6x4 mining dump truck. That is 55–75% below diesel on the same route.

Energy cost per kilometre is the single number that decides most fleet electrification business cases. Yet most published figures are passenger-car numbers scaled up, or manufacturer ideal values measured on a test track. This article uses measured kWh/km from our deployed export fleet, real commercial tariff structures, and a like-for-like diesel comparison so that a fleet CFO can put the figure straight into a model. It also covers the two costs that trip up first-time buyers — charging losses and demand charges — because ignoring either will make your post-audit numbers disagree with your business case.

The Core Formula: Cost Per km = kWh/km × Tariff

Only two variables matter at the first level of accuracy. Everything else is a refinement:

  • kWh/km — set by vehicle class, payload, route profile, terrain and climate.
  • Tariff (USD/kWh) — set by your market, your depot connection and, critically, the time of day you charge.

What a Full Charge Actually Costs

Take the most common export configuration — a 400 kWh pack:

  • 0–100% charge: 400 kWh ÷ ~92% charger efficiency ≈ 435 kWh drawn from the grid.
  • At USD 0.12/kWh: USD 52. At USD 0.14/kWh: USD 61. At an off-peak USD 0.07/kWh: USD 30.
  • That delivers 250–300 km of real range, so the cost per km lands at USD 0.17–0.24 at peak-day tariffs and USD 0.10–0.12 on night rates.

Most fleets never run 0–100%. A 20–90% daily window on 400 kWh is 280 kWh usable, roughly 200 km, and costs USD 34–40 at standard commercial rates. The reason we recommend that window is not just battery life — it is turnaround time, because the last 10% of charge is the slowest part of the curve.

In markets with subsidised electricity — much of the Gulf, hydro-backed grids in Central Asia, and industrial-zone tariffs in Southeast Asia — the saving widens. In markets with high commercial tariffs and heavy demand charges, it narrows, which is exactly why depot electrical design belongs in the business case and not in an appendix.

The Two Costs That Break Naive Models

Almost every first electrification budget is wrong for the same two reasons:

  • Charging losses. AC-to-DC conversion plus thermal management means you buy 8–12% more kWh than the pack stores. A fleet that budgets on nameplate kWh understates cost by roughly a tenth.
  • Demand charges. Many commercial tariffs bill a monthly peak-kW charge. Ten trucks charging simultaneously at 120 kW each create a 1,200 kW peak; the demand charge on that can add USD 0.03–0.06 per kWh to effective cost. Staggered start times and smart load management recover most of it — see our guides to demand charges and depot billing and smart charging load management.

How Tariffs Vary by Market

Indicative commercial electricity prices our customers actually pay, which is what determines your number:

  • Gulf (UAE, Saudi Arabia, Qatar). USD 0.05–0.09/kWh for industrial connections. Lowest operating cost of any market we serve; the constraint is grid connection lead time, not price.
  • Southeast Asia (Vietnam, Indonesia, Thailand, Philippines). USD 0.08–0.13/kWh, with industrial-zone tariffs at the lower end and seasonal peak premiums.
  • West and East Africa. USD 0.10–0.18/kWh where grid power is available; many operators pair solar with grid to cut daytime draw, as covered in our solar-hybrid charging guide.
  • Latin America (Chile, Peru, Colombia, Mexico). USD 0.11–0.17/kWh for commercial connections, with mining sites often running own-generation.
  • Central Asia (Kazakhstan, Uzbekistan). USD 0.04–0.07/kWh — among the lowest we see, offset by winter heating load on the pack.

Battery Swap: A Different Cost Structure

Where duty cycles are continuous — mining, port shuttle, 24-hour concrete — battery swap changes the cost model from energy-per-kWh to a per-swap or per-kWh service fee. A swap takes 5–6 minutes instead of 40, so the truck earns instead of waiting, but the swap provider prices in battery ownership, station capex and spare packs. Our rule of thumb: swap wins above roughly 18 operating hours per day or where charger capex at the depot is prohibitive; plug-in charging wins below that. The full trade-off is set out in charging vs battery swap fleet strategy, and swap economics for a specific truck are worked through in our Chile market page.

How to Build the Number for Your Own Fleet

Three steps, in this order:

  1. Measure the duty cycle. Distance per shift, payload, terrain, stops. Without this, any kWh/km figure is a guess — our battery sizing methodology shows how to do it properly.
  2. Get your real tariff. Not the published residential rate: your depot's commercial rate including demand charges and time-of-use windows.
  3. Add 10% for losses and model a night-charging scenario against a day-charging scenario before you size the electrical connection.

Do that and the answer to "how much does it cost to charge an EV truck" stops being a marketing claim and becomes a line item your finance team will sign off.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much does it cost to charge an electric truck?

At a typical commercial tariff of USD 0.10–0.14 per kWh, charging a Dongfeng electric truck costs USD 0.08–0.20 per km depending on duty cycle: about USD 0.08–0.10/km for a light box truck, USD 0.15–0.20/km for a 49 t tractor and USD 0.28–0.34/km for a loaded 6x4 mining dump truck.

Is charging an EV truck cheaper than diesel?

Yes, typically 55–75% cheaper per km. A diesel tractor burning 32 L/100 km at USD 1.05/L costs about USD 0.34/km; the equivalent electric tractor at 1.35 kWh/km and USD 0.12/kWh costs about USD 0.16/km including charging losses.

How many kWh does an electric truck use per kilometre?

Measured consumption: 0.55–0.75 kWh/km for light box trucks in city duty, 1.0–1.2 kWh/km for 18–25 t rigids, 1.25–1.45 kWh/km for 49–80 t tractors, and 1.6–2.4 kWh/km for loaded 6x4 dump trucks on haul roads.

Do demand charges change EV truck charging economics?

Yes, materially. Demand charges billed on peak kW can add USD 0.03–0.06 per kWh if a fleet charges simultaneously; shifting to night windows and staggering charger starts usually recovers most of that, and smart load management does it automatically.

How much does a full charge cost for a 400 kWh EV truck?

About USD 48–56 for a 0–100% charge at USD 0.12–0.14 per kWh including roughly 8% charging losses, giving 250–300 km of range. Most fleets charge 20–90% daily, which costs USD 38–45.
