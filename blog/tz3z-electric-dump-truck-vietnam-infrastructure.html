Vietnam's Infrastructure Boom Meets the Electric Dump Truck: Dongfeng TZ3Z Fleet Economics

Vietnam is executing one of the largest infrastructure programmes in Southeast Asia: the North-South Expressway completion drive, metro lines under construction in Hanoi and Ho Chi Minh City, Long Thanh International Airport, and a continuous wave of industrial park build-out feeding export manufacturing. Every one of those projects moves aggregate, fill and demolition material with dump trucks — and dump trucks run short fixed-radius cycles from borrow pit to worksite, returning to the same yard every night. That is the single best duty profile for battery-electric haulage in existence. With ACFTA preferential duties on Chinese-origin goods and Vietnamese electricity among the cheapest in the region, the Dongfeng TZ3Z — an 8x4 electric dump truck with CATL 400 kWh LFP, 55 t GCW and 510 kW peak drive — is the natural first unit for Vietnamese contractors going electric. This analysis lays out the market and the numbers.

The Project Pipeline That Creates Demand

  • Expressway construction. Haul cycles of 10–30 km from borrow pits to roadbed, 12–16 hour double shifts, high tonnage per day per truck. Diesel tippers in this duty burn 45–55 L/100 km fully loaded on rutted site roads.
  • Urban metro and tunnelling. Hanoi's metro extensions and HCMC's lines generate continuous muck-out and materials-in movements under city air and noise rules — where zero-tailpipe, low-noise electric dump trucks face less restriction and win night-work permits diesel cannot.
  • Long Thanh airport and industrial parks. Millions of cubic metres of earthworks in compact site radii, with ESG requirements increasingly written in by foreign anchor tenants who report Scope 3 emissions.

All three categories share the same geometry: a fixed site radius under 40 km, a controlled yard for charging, and punishing utilisation. The TZ3Z is engineered precisely for it.

Import Route and Homologation

Trucks ship RORO from Chinese ports to Hai Phong, Da Nang or Ho Chi Minh City (Cat Lai) in 5–12 days. Vietnam's registration process for imported trucks requires quality certification (VQ/QC registration through the vehicle registration authority) — we prepare the dossier, including UN 38.3 test summary and DG shipping documents for the 400 kWh pack, and our Vietnamese buyers clear through brokers experienced with electric vehicles. Form E issuance at our end is routine and takes days, not weeks.

Charging on the Construction Site

Site-yards are the charging network: a 20-truck fleet on double shifts is served by two 240 kW dual-gun chargers operating at shift change (midday and midnight) plus a 360 kW unit for opportunity charging, all fed from a dedicated 22 kV site transformer that civil contractors already install for batching plants. Total charging infrastructure: USD 120,000–180,000, often shared with site offices and equipment. Where grid capacity is tight — mountainous expressway sections — we design solar-hybrid arrays with battery buffering that cover 30–50% of charging energy from the site's own generation.

Monsoon and Heat Notes

Central Vietnam delivers both 38 °C summers and months of monsoon rain. The CATL pack's liquid cooling handles the heat; the IP68 HV architecture and high-mounted pack handle flooded site roads within stated wading limits. Our commissioning for Vietnamese fleets includes the wet-season inspection protocol and a mud-ingression check schedule for connectors — both standard practice across our Southeast Asian deployments.

Night Haulage: The Regulatory Advantage

Vietnam's two mega-cities regulate construction traffic the way every dense Asian city eventually does: heavy trucks are banned from inner districts during daytime hours, pushing aggregate and muck movements into 21:00-06:00 windows. This schedule is usually an operational burden — but it is structurally unfair in favour of electric dump trucks:

  • Noise permits: night haulage through urban districts carries noise limits that diesel tippers strain against and electric trucks clear easily. The TZ3Z runs at 69-72 dB(A) in operation — a working conversation two metres away — which keeps night permits continuous and avoids the community-complaint stoppages that plague diesel fleets on residential-adjacent routes.
  • Driver night-shift physiology: the electric cab is quiet, vibration-free and gearless; drivers finishing a night shift in a TZ3Z report fatigue levels closer to a car than a diesel 8x4. On double-shift projects this converts directly into lower absenteeism and better retention of the experienced operators Vietnamese contractors compete for.
  • Zero tailpipe at the gate: worksites increasingly specify no-idling rules at site entrances and loading points. An electric truck queuing at a weighbridge or waiting under the excavator consumes nothing — where a diesel burns 3-4 L/h and accumulates community complaints.
