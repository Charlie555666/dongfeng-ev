Two electric tractors in the same range, overlapping on paper, frequently shortlisted together: the Dongfeng TE46 at CATL 400 kWh with a LvKong 315 kW rated / 510 kW peak motor and 4-speed AMT, and the TE8L at CATL 400/466/600 kWh with the same 315/510 kW motor family. Buyers ask which one to order, and the answer turns out to be unusually clean: they are built for different combinations of distance and gross weight, and once those two numbers are known the choice is usually obvious. The Decision Rule: Distance First, Weight Second

Two questions resolve almost every case:

  1. What is the daily distance? Under roughly 220 km per shift and returning to the same depot each night, the TE46 with 400 kWh is sufficient and cheaper. Above 300 km, or with a mid-route leg, the TE8L's larger pack options are required.
  2. What is the gross combination weight and duty? Terminal shuttle with frequent coupling and uncoupling favours the TE46's terminal-oriented configuration. Sustained highway running at 49 t favours the TE8L.

Where both answers point the same way, the decision is straightforward. Where they conflict — long distance at low weight, or short distance at high weight — the answer is usually the larger pack, because energy margin is cheaper than a stranded schedule.

When the TE46 Is Clearly Right

  • Port and intermodal terminal shuttle. Short, repetitive moves with heavy idling: the TE46's design duty. See TE46 terminal operations and rail intermodal terminals.
  • Container yard and depot transfers within a port estate.
  • Industrial site shuttle — plant to warehouse, works to rail head, on fixed short corridors.
  • Capital-constrained first electrification. Lower entry price, and the 400 kWh pack is correctly sized for the duty rather than oversized.
  • Fleet standardisation on a single depot-charged shift with an overnight window.

When the TE8L Is Clearly Right

  • Line-haul corridor work at 49 t. This is its design duty, and the 600 kWh option exists specifically for it.
  • Multi-leg or long-distance days above roughly 300 km.
  • Reefer trailer duty, where continuous auxiliary load demands the larger pack — see TE8L cold chain analysis.
  • Routes with sustained grades, where energy per leg rises sharply.
  • Fleets that may redeploy vehicles to longer corridors later — the larger pack buys flexibility.

Operating Cost Comparison

Per-kilometre figures differ modestly, because both use the same motor family and similar drivetrains:

  • TE46 in shuttle duty: 1.05–1.25 kWh/km → USD 0.12–0.14/km at USD 0.11/kWh.
  • TE8L at 49 t line-haul: 1.25–1.40 kWh/km → USD 0.14–0.16/km.
  • Maintenance: comparable; terminal duty involves more coupling cycles and low-speed manoeuvring wear, line-haul involves more tyre and brake wear from distance.
  • Capital: TE46 lower, typically USD 10,000–20,000 per unit below a comparably equipped TE8L.

The real cost difference is capital and depot infrastructure rather than running cost — which reinforces the rule of buying the vehicle whose pack matches the duty.

Can One Fleet Run Both?

Yes, and many do. A port or logistics operator with both terminal shuttle work and longer regional legs benefits from running both, provided three things are standardised:

  1. One charging platform — same connectors, same backend, same load management.
  2. One telematics view — energy, availability and fault reporting across both classes.
  3. One spares and training programme — the shared motor family makes this practical.

Bottom Line

Choose the TE46 for terminal, port and industrial shuttle work under roughly 220 km per day, and for the lowest capital entry into heavy electric tractor operation. Choose the TE8L for line-haul at 49 t, for corridors above 300 km, and for reefer or graded routes where the larger pack options earn their cost. Both carry the same CATL warranty — 8 years or 4,500 cycles to 80% state of health — and both are supported by the same export service model.

Residual Value and Second-Life Considerations

Both tractors carry the same CATL pack warranty, so residual value is driven by condition, documented state of health, and the depth of the used market for the configuration.

  • Terminal tractors accumulate low annual mileage relative to line-haul units, so a ten-year-old TE46 may have a relatively low cycle count — which is favourable for resale, since cycle count is what buyers of used electric trucks scrutinise.
  • Line-haul tractors accumulate high mileage but predictable duty; a well-documented service history and a clean SOH certificate support value in the used market.
  • Both benefit from a formal battery health certificate at disposal, which converts an unverifiable claim into a measured figure. See SOH diagnostics and resale certification and used electric truck residual values.
  • Packs retired from traction at around 80% SOH retain stationary storage value, which is increasingly factored into end-of-life economics — covered in second-life battery value.

For fleets that replace on a fixed cycle, the practical advice is simple: instrument the pack from day one and keep the records. The difference between a documented and an undocumented battery history is worth more at disposal than most of the specification choices made at purchase.
