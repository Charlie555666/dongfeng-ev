Do Electric Trucks Have Enough Torque for Heavy Haulage?

Quick answer: yes - an electric truck produces more torque than any diesel in its class, delivered from zero rpm. The TE9L pairs a 510 kW motor with a 600 kWh CATL LFP pack for 60-90 tonne gross combinations over 350 km of certified range, with grade performance limited by energy, not by pulling power.

How Much Torque Does an Electric Truck Actually Produce?

More than any diesel in the same weight class, and it arrives differently. The TE9L runs a 510 kW motor that delivers roughly 2,400-3,400 Nm at the shaft from the first revolution, and the single-speed reduction plus axle ratio multiplies that to well over 25,000 Nm at the wheels. A comparable 13-litre diesel makes 2,300-2,600 Nm, but only between 1,000 and 1,400 rpm, and it has to be geared, clutched and spooled to get there. That is why an electric truck pulling away from a load-out bay feels nothing like a diesel of identical power: peak torque at zero rpm means no clutch slip, no turbo lag and no gear hunting on the ramp. For heavy haulage the practical translation is simple - the electric truck reaches its rated tractive effort instantly, and holds it up the grade instead of fading as rpm drops.

Why Does Torque at Zero RPM Change Grade Performance?

Grade performance is a torque problem before it is a power problem. A loaded combination climbing an 8% ramp at 15 km/h needs continuous tractive effort, and a diesel must sit at high rpm with the torque converter slipping to deliver it - which is where fuel consumption and transmission heat peak. The TE9L delivers the same effort at 20-30 km/h with the motor spinning comfortably inside its efficient band, and regenerative braking on the way down recovers energy instead of burning it through a retarder. On Emirati haul roads where the loaded leg is uphill and the empty leg is downhill, that symmetry compounds: the truck spends less energy climbing and recovers part of it descending. Operators running both drivelines on the same ramp consistently report the electric unit holding higher average speed loaded, with no gear changes at all.

Can an Electric Truck Pull 60 to 90 Tonnes Gross?

Yes, within defined limits that buyers should understand before ordering. The TE9L is rated for 60-90 tonne gross combination weight depending on axle configuration, tyre specification and the grade profile of the route - with a 600 kWh CATL LFP pack supplying the 510 kW driveline over 350 km of certified range. The constraint on heavy haulage is rarely torque and almost always energy: a 90-tonne combination on a long climb can consume 3.0-3.5 kWh per km, which turns a 600 kWh pack into roughly 170-200 km of working range. That is ample for port shuttles, quarry loops and mine ramp cycles, and short for 500 km line-haul. Buyers should size the battery against the daily loaded distance, not the headline range, and plan a mid-shift top-up where the duty demands it.

Does Instant Torque Wear Out Drivelines Faster?

Not when the driveline is specified for it - and the failure mode is different from what diesel experience suggests. Electric drivelines lose the clutch, the torque converter and the multi-speed gearbox, which removes three of the highest-wear assemblies in a heavy truck, and the motor's torque ramp is electronically limited at launch to protect shafts and joints. What replaces those wear items is software: torque limiting in first motion, overspeed protection on descents and thermal derating that trims output before anything is damaged. In practice the components that need watching on heavy electric haulage are tyres, suspension bushes and the final drive, all of which see higher continuous load. Which Electric Truck Specs Matter for Heavy Haulage Most?

Four numbers decide it, and motor kW is the least important. Gross combination weight rating and the axle ratings behind it define what the truck may legally pull in the UAE. Usable battery kWh comes second, because consumption at 60-90 tonnes runs 2.2-3.5 kWh per km and range follows directly. Continuous motor power comes third - a long climb is a thermal event, and continuous rating is what the truck holds for an hour. Charging power accepted by the pack comes fourth.
