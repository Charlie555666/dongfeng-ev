The TE46 Duty Cycle Baseline

Port shuttle work is the friendliest duty cycle in heavy trucking for electrification: short fixed loops (typically 1–4 km between quay and yard), average speeds of 15–25 km/h, constant stop-start, and heavy loads at low speed. That means regenerative braking recovers a large share of traction energy, and consumption lands at roughly 1.1–1.4 kWh per shuttle cycle depending on laden ratio and gradient. A TE46 running 350–450 cycles per shift consumes 450–630 kWh per day — more than one full 400 kWh pack.

Option 1: Overnight + Break-Shift Depot Charging

The simplest model uses 120–240 kW chargers in the tractor parking area. Drivers plug in at shift change; the truck soaks 60–90 minutes during handover plus a longer window overnight for the graveyard-to-day transition. The infrastructure cost is modest: a 12-gun 120 kW depot with transformer and civil works lands around USD 480,000–700,000, and grid connection is usually straightforward because ports already hold firm power contracts.

The constraint is fleet availability math. If a TE46 needs 90 minutes of charging per 8-hour shift to stay in rotation, and you only get two natural break windows per shift, every tractor spends 15–18% of its calendar time on a plug. Terminals solve this the classic way: buy 15–20% more tractors than the diesel fleet they replaced. Even with the extra units, the energy cost per box moved drops so sharply that the business case holds — but the CAPEX line grows, and yard space for charging positions must be carved out.

  • Best fit: terminals with predictable shift structure, existing substation headroom, and space near the tractor pool.
  • CAPEX: USD 40,000–58,000 per charging position installed, plus transformer upgrade where needed.
  • Fleet effect: plan 15–20% more tractors than diesel baseline for the same box moves.
  • Energy cost: off-peak port power at USD 0.06–0.11/kWh translates to roughly USD 55–80 per tractor-shift.

Option 2: Battery Swap for Maximum Utilisation

A swap station exchanges a depleted pack for a charged one in 5–6 minutes — faster than a diesel refuel, and fully automated. The tractor never leaves rotation for more than a coffee break. With swap, the terminal runs the same tractor count as diesel, and utilisation climbs above 90%. The trade is infrastructure: a heavy-duty swap station with an inventory of 8–12 spare packs, crane rig, battery racking with liquid-conditioned bays, and a higher-power grid connection typically runs USD 1.6–2.8 million installed, plus roughly USD 45,000–70,000 per spare pack inventory unit.

Ports actually fit swap unusually well compared with highway fleets, for three reasons:

  1. Fixed geography. The entire duty cycle happens within 3 km of one point, so one station serves 100% of energy events — no corridor problem.
  2. Peak-shift energy arbitrage. Swap stations charge packs at controlled rates around the clock and can preferentially absorb cheap off-peak power, then release that energy as 5-minute swaps during the shift.
  3. Asset separation. Packs can be owned or leased by the station operator, which takes battery residual-value risk off the terminal's balance sheet and turns energy into a per-swap service fee.

The pattern that emerges across real projects: depot charging wins on lifetime cost when the terminal can absorb the availability haircut; swap wins when throughput is contractually fixed (berth productivity penalties make a 15% availability loss expensive), when land near the plug positions is scarce, or when a third-party energy operator is willing to carry the station CAPEX and sell per-swap.

Strategy 3: The Hybrid Most Ports Actually Choose

The most common landing point in our project pipeline is hybrid: dual-gun fast chargers (240–350 kW) installed at the chassis-lane and workshop areas for opportunistic charging during routine dwell, plus a single swap station serving the core shuttle pool. The fast chargers cover the 20–30% of tractors whose schedules show natural 30–40 minute gaps, while the swap station guarantees the deadline-driven core never queues. Terminals that start pure-depot frequently add a swap station in year two when berth productivity data justifies it; terminals that start pure-swap add plug positions once they see how many trucks have idle windows anyway.

One detail that matters at specification time: order the TE46 fleet with the swap-compatible battery module and dual-gun charge port from day one. Retrofitting swap compatibility onto a fixed-pack tractor is not economic, but a swap-ready truck plugs in happily for years until a station arrives. The option costs little upfront and keeps both doors open.

Grid, Safety and Certification Notes

Port electrification interacts with two bodies most truck buyers meet for the first time: the port authority's electrical safety regime, and dangerous-goods rules for lithium battery storage. Keep these on the checklist early:

  • Grid studies first. A 40-unit depot at 240 kW per gun is a 6–9 MW peak load; port substations often have headroom, but the interconnection study takes 3–6 months in most jurisdictions. Start it before the trucks ship.
  • Battery storage licensing. Swap station pack inventories above roughly 1 MWh stored energy trigger battery-storage permitting in many ports. Budget the paperwork, not just the steel.
  • UN 38.3 and IMDG. All TE46 shipments move with UN 38.3 test summaries and IMDG-compliant dangerous-goods documentation — a routine matter for us, but terminal customs brokers should be briefed before the vessel arrives.
  • Isolation discipline. Port environments are wet, salty and wash-down heavy. The TE46's battery enclosure is IP-rated for washdown, and charging areas need the standard clearance and drainage details we supply in the terminal layout drawings.

What We Recommend, By Terminal Type

  • Under 15 tractors: depot charging, dual-gun 240 kW. Swap CAPEX never pays back at this scale.
  • 15–40 tractors, fixed berth commitments: hybrid — plug positions at natural dwell points plus one swap station for the deadline pool.
  • Over 40 tractors or multi-terminal operator: swap-led with a shared station, pack leasing, and plugs as secondary. Fleet-wide availability above 92% changes berth planning math permanently.
  • Greenfield terminals: design swap into the master plan from day one; the civil works cost of adding it later is what kills projects.
