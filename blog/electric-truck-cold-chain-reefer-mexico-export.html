Mexico's export cold chain, moving avocados, berries, and vegetables from Michoacan and Jalisco to border crossings and ports, is one of the most demanding duties an electric truck can take on, because the refrigeration unit draws power continuously on top of traction. Why the Cold Chain Is Electrifying

Export produce logistics runs fixed loops between packhouses, cold stores, and border or port terminals, so routes are predictable and depots are known, the structure electrification needs. The cold chain also cares about temperature integrity, and an electric reefer holds setpoint silently at loading bays and border queues without a diesel engine idling. Specifying the Reefer: Power Budget First

The critical calculation is the refrigeration unit's draw on top of traction. A diesel-driven reefer unit burns 3 to 5 liters per hour; an electric reefer running from the truck's pack or a dedicated auxiliary battery draws 6 to 10 kW continuously. On a platform with a 262 kWh pack, the practical approach is a small dedicated auxiliary battery for the reefer, so refrigeration never competes with traction for range.

Range and Charging for Produce Loops

With the reefer on its own auxiliary pack, traction range stays at the chassis figure of 240 to 280 kilometers, covering the packhouse-to-border or packhouse-to-port loops with margin. Overnight depot charging covers daily operation, and cold stores along the corridor can host opportunity chargers so the truck tops up while loading, with the reefer running on shore power during the stop.

  • Spec a dedicated auxiliary pack so the reefer never cuts traction range.
  • Plug the reefer into shore power at every loading stop.
  • Pre-cool the box on grid power before loading produce.
  • Charge overnight at the packhouse depot.
  • Log temperature and energy for export compliance records.
