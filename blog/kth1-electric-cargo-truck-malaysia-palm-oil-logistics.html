KTH1 Electric Cargo Truck in Malaysia Palm Oil Logistics

Moving Fresh Fruit Bunches Without the Diesel Bill

Malaysia's palm oil supply chain depends on a relentless daily movement of fresh fruit bunches, or FFB, from outlying plantations to the nearest mill before the fruit degrades. The electric truck is a natural fit for this loop. The KTH1 electric cargo truck, and its closely related heavier variant the KTH3 electric cargo truck, are purpose suited to plantation-to-mill haulage in Johor, Sabah, and across Peninsular Malaysia, where routes are predictable, distances are short, and return loads are common.

Because FFB must reach the mill within 24 hours of harvest, mill catchments are tight and trucks run multiple back-to-back trips per day. That duty profile, frequent starts, moderate speeds, and constant stop-go on estate roads, is exactly where an EV truck beats diesel on both energy and maintenance. Regenerative braking on the loaded return and the absence of engine idling in long loading queues quietly erase a large slice of operating cost.

For estate managers, the appeal is also operational simplicity. A diesel cargo truck on a plantation accumulates hours idling in load queues, in river crossings, and at weighbridges, all of which burn fuel and wear the engine without moving freight. The KTH1 simply draws a trickle when stationary and delivers full torque the instant the light turns green. Over a month of FFB peaks, that difference shows up directly on the running-cost ledger.

KTH1 and KTH3 Cargo Specifications

The KTH platform uses a LvKong drive motor in the 282-350 kW range with 2200-2800 Nm of torque, ample for fully loaded FFB trailers on estate gradients. CATL LFP battery options from 262 to 384 kWh let fleet managers match pack size to route length and avoid paying for unused range. The KTH3 adds payload and a longer wheelbase for Sabah's longer plantation corridors.

For the humid, tropical conditions of Johor and Sabah, the sealed electric drivetrain resists the corrosion and electrical faults that plague diesel systems in high-moisture estates. IP-rated components and a simplified cooling loop keep the KTH dependable through the monsoon months. The absence of a diesel exhaust system also removes a common failure point on muddy, waterlogged laterite roads where a raised intake is the only defence against hydrolock.

Plantation-to-Mill Route Planning

A typical FFB loop runs 15-45 km one way on a mix of sealed and laterite estate roads, with the loaded leg outbound and a partial return. Because the route is fixed, depot charging is straightforward: charge overnight at the mill using off-peak tariff, and top up during the midday lull.

  • Map each estate's daily FFB volume to truck count and battery size.
  • Locate the depot charger at the mill to use existing three-phase supply.
  • Train drivers on single-pedal driving to maximize regeneration.
  • Monitor state-of-charge and route completion through fleet telematics.
  • Reserve a spare truck per 8-10 units to cover maintenance windows.

Mill operators who convert a catchment to electric haulage often find the quietest benefit is reliability: fewer breakdown recoveries from deep inside the estate, and a predictable energy bill instead of volatile diesel pricing. Because the electric truck has roughly half the moving parts of a diesel powertrain, the scheduled service visits shrink from weekly checks to monthly inspections, freeing the estate workshop for other equipment.

A useful planning rule is to size the battery for 1.4 times the longest single-day loop distance. This buffer absorbs rainy-season slowdowns, detours around felled trees, and the extra kilometres from a relocated collection point, without stranding a truck mid-route. The KTH3's larger pack is the right call for Sabah estates where the mill may be 60 km or more from the furthest smallholder scheme.

Humidity, Heat, and Mountain Grades

Malaysian estates combine coastal humidity with inland grades as plantations climb toward the interior. The CATL LFP pack is tolerant of high ambient temperatures, and the KTH's thermal management holds cell temperature in a safe band even on slow climbs with a full load. On the steeper Sabah corridors, the KTH3's higher torque rating preserves cycle times that a smaller motor would lose to heat derating.

The electronics enclosure is sealed to IP67 and the high-voltage connectors are corrosion-resistant, which matters on coastal Johor routes where salt-laden air accelerates rust on a conventional chassis. Operators report that after two monsoon seasons the electric trucks show fewer electrical gremlins than the diesel fleet they replaced, a finding that has driven repeat orders across several Peninsular estates.

Total Cost of Ownership for Mill Catchments

The financial case for the KTH1 rests on three pillars: lower energy cost per tonne, lower maintenance cost per kilometre, and avoided diesel logistics. On a 30 km FFB loop run 10 times per day, the electric truck typically consumes 30-45 kWh per round trip, against 18-22 litres of diesel for a comparable laden diesel. At Malaysian industrial electricity and diesel prices, the energy saving alone often pays a meaningful share of the financing.

Lay the maintenance saving on top, add the resale stability of an 8-year / 4500-cycle battery warranty, and the KTH1 frequently beats diesel on a five-year total-cost basis even before any green-premium palm oil contract is counted. For mills pursuing certified sustainable palm oil, documenting electric haulage also strengthens the audit trail and supports premium market access.

Smallholder Schemes and Shared Charging

A distinctive feature of Malaysian palm logistics is the smallholder scheme, where many independent growers deliver FFB to a central mill along the same estate roads the plantation trucks use. The KTH1 fits this model because its moderate size and predictable range make it easy to share a single depot charger among several vehicles owned by different cooperatives. A mill can offer charging as a service, billing each truck by the kWh, which lowers the capital barrier for smallholders who cannot justify a dedicated charger.

This shared model also smooths the grid load. Instead of every truck charging at once, the telematics system sequences them through the available DC guns overnight, keeping the mill's peak demand within its connection limit. During the harvest peak, when FFB volumes surge, the KTH3's larger pack absorbs longer days without extra charging infrastructure, and the KTH1 covers the shorter, high-frequency loops near the mill.

We recommend mills pilot the electric fleet on their own captive transport first, prove the energy and maintenance saving for 90 days, and then extend the model to participating smallholders under a simple charging agreement. The result is a catchment where the majority of FFB moves on clean electricity, the mill controls its energy cost, and smallholders gain access to modern trucks without taking on diesel price risk. That combination is why estate managers describe the KTH platform as a logistics upgrade rather than merely a green one.
