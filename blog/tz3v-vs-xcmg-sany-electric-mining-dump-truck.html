Three Brands, Three Traditions

XCMG and SANY come from the construction-machinery world — cranes, excavators, concrete machinery — and their electric mining trucks inherit that DNA: purpose-built rigid frames, mining-specific styling, and an engineering culture optimised for heavy plant. Dongfeng comes from the trucking world: millions of commercial vehicles on the road, a drivetrain and chassis tradition built around duty-cycle economics, serviceability and the CATL battery partnership that defines the Chinese heavy-EV industry. None of these traditions is superior in the abstract; they resolve differently depending on what your operation actually does with the truck.

Five Details That Actually Differentiate

  • Warranty structure on the pack. The 8-year / 4,500-cycle CATL warranty backing the TZ3V is the benchmark term in the industry, and it is transferable to the export buyer through our documentation. Compare terms line by line: cycle definitions (full-equivalent vs absolute), SoH floor at warranty end (typically 70–80%), and who stands behind the pack in-market — the truck brand, the battery maker, or the exporter. Fleet financiers care intensely about this line; it drives residual-value assumptions.
  • Road-legality of the configuration. The TZ3V is a truck-class 8x4 tipper — road-legal, which matters for mines hauling on public corridors between pit, crusher and railhead, and for contractors who redeploy trucks between sites. Wide-body pure mining rigs from any brand trade that flexibility for pit-only duty. Match the configuration to your haul geography before comparing anything else.
  • Conventional-axle drivetrain economics. The TZ3V drives through a conventional central-motor-to-axle path. Distributed motor systems can offer elegant traction control, but they multiply the number of high-voltage rotating machines and power electronics on a dust-blasted mine site, and every additional HV node is a maintenance and spares item. In our experience of African and Central Asian mine support, fewer, bigger, standard components win the availability argument.
  • Export support reality. All three brands sell internationally, but the buyer's experience depends on the channel: which entity holds your parts pipeline, who flies a technician when a fault code stops a truck at 02:00, and whether HV training for your team was part of the deal. We build commissioning, technician training and an air-freight critical-spares lane into every TZ3V fleet order — ask any brand's representative to state their equivalent commitment specifically, in the contract, before comparing prices.
  • Charging and swap interoperability. The TZ3V's swap-capable variant follows the CAS swap standard with deployable station hardware, and its plug-in variant charges on open-standard CCS2/GB/T dual-gun DC. Proprietary charging or swapping stacks tie your infrastructure investment to one vendor's ecosystem — expensive to discover in year four.

The Cost Ground Every Brand Shares

One point of convergence deserves emphasis because it is the real reason all three brands are winning diesel conversions globally: an 80 t-class electric tipper with 600 kWh LFP hauls at roughly USD 0.020 per tonne-kilometre against a diesel equivalent's USD 0.045–0.055 on typical mine power prices — a saving of 55–60% that no drivetrain philosophy dispute touches. Whichever badge ends up on your fleet, the decade's biggest mining cost decision is diesel-to-electric; the brand comparison is the second-order question. But second-order questions decide real money too — over an eight-year, 20-truck fleet life, the spares, downtime and warranty differences between brands can swing several hundred thousand dollars. That is why the four documents matter more than the spec sheet.

Swap Interoperability: The Quiet Lock-In Risk

Battery swapping deserves its own diligence section because it is where brand ecosystems can quietly lock in. A mine that builds a swap station buys not just hardware but an interface standard — pack form factor, locking mechanism, communication protocol, rack geometry. The TZ-series swap-capable variants follow the CAS-standard architecture with station hardware we export and deploy alongside the fleet, and the station's charger inventory charges packs on open-standard DC hardware. When evaluating any brand's swap offering, ask three questions: (1) Can packs from more than one truck model use the station, or is it single-model? (2) Who owns the pack inventory and under what terms — purchased, leased, or BaaS? (3) If the fleet later adds a second brand's swap trucks, can the station serve both, or does the mine buy a second station? The answers determine whether the station is infrastructure or a leash. Our preference — stated plainly because we benefit from buyers who ask it of everyone, including us — is that mines treat swap stations the way they treat fuel farms: multi-user infrastructure whose ownership and control sit with the mine, not the truck brand.
