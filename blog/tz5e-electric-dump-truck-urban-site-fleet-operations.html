TZ5E Electric Dump Truck Fleet Operations: Cycle Time, Queue Management and Charger Allocation on Urban Sites

Urban construction is a different operating problem from mining. The haul is shorter, the roads are public, the neighbours are close, and the constraint is rarely the truck — it is the loader queue, the gate, the traffic and the site's electrical supply. Cycle-Time Discipline: Where the Tonnes Go

An urban tipper cycle decomposes into six elements, and in our experience two of them account for most of the avoidable loss:

  1. Load — 4–8 minutes. Wheel loader or excavator fill, often on a street with traffic management.
  2. Haul loaded — 8–20 minutes depending on distance and traffic.
  3. Tip and turn — 4–7 minutes; includes sheeting/unsheeting, positioning, body raise and wash-down.
  4. Return empty — 6–15 minutes.
  5. Queue at the loader — 3–12 minutes. This is the largest controllable loss on most sites.
  6. Gate, wheel wash and traffic control — 2–6 minutes.

Total cycle: 27–68 minutes. That is a wide band, and the spread is almost entirely queue and traffic, not vehicle performance. The practical implication for an electric fleet: adding trucks to a site with a single loader produces diminishing returns fast. Balance the loader-to-truck ratio — typically one loader per 4–6 tippers in urban work — before you add vehicles.

Charger Allocation: Sizing for a Compressed Window

Urban sites typically have a 10–12 hour working day and a temporary electrical supply. That makes charger sizing the critical calculation:

  • Per-truck daily energy: a TZ5E on urban duty runs 1.5–1.9 kWh/km loaded; at 120–180 km per shift that is 200–330 kWh, plus 10% charging losses.
  • Overnight window: 10–12 hours between shifts. A fleet of eight trucks needing 2,400 kWh total requires roughly 220 kW average site load — comfortably served by two 180 kW DC units with staggered scheduling, or by distributed 44 kW AC sockets per bay.
  • Night-only operations: where the council permits night earthmoving only, trucks work 22:00–06:00 and charge during the day. Daytime charging raises thermal load on the pack in hot climates — plan shade or covered bays where ambient exceeds 38 °C.
  • Temporary supply reality: site generators or temporary grid connections are often limited to 100–250 kVA. Confirm capacity before ordering chargers; a smart load-management controller lets you charge a larger fleet inside a smaller supply ceiling.

Queue Management: The Cheapest Productivity Gain on Site

Three measures that consistently recover 8–15% of fleet productivity without spending on vehicles:

  • Staggered shift starts so eight trucks do not arrive at the loader simultaneously at 07:00.
  • A designated passing and holding area near the load point, so trucks circulate rather than idle in a queue.
  • Loading-point scheduling tied to the tipper count — the excavator's output, not the truck count, sets site tonnage.

Night Operation: The Urban Advantage of an Electric Truck

This is where electrification changes what a project can do rather than just how much it costs. Many city authorities now permit — or actively prefer — night earthmoving when the equipment is quiet, because daytime works conflict with traffic, schools and retail. An electric tipper operating at 65–70 dB(A) versus 85–90 dB(A) for a diesel equivalent can win working windows that a diesel fleet cannot. Two operational requirements follow:

  • Lighting — specify site lighting and cab work lights for night loading; it is the most commonly forgotten line item.
  • Charging alignment — night working means daytime charging, which in hot climates needs thermal planning and in all climates needs a secure, supervised yard.

Compliance and Community: Sheeting, Wheel Wash, Spillage

Urban sites are judged on what leaves them. Three items belong in the operating plan:

  1. Sheeting or covered bodies — mandatory in most jurisdictions for public-road haulage; automatic sheeting systems reduce cycle time versus manual.
  2. Wheel wash at the site exit — pair with a KT7A electric washing truck for road cleaning, keeping the whole site diesel-free.
  3. Dust suppression — a KT3F electric sprinkler truck on a watering schedule, particularly on temporary site roads.

Operating Checklist for the First 30 Days

  • Log cycle times by element for two weeks before changing anything — you cannot fix what you have not measured.
  • Verify charger capacity against actual daily energy, not the design estimate.
  • Set a minimum arrival state of charge of 15% and enforce it in dispatch.
  • Track tonnes per truck per shift and cost per tonne weekly; both are the numbers that fund the next order.
