CATL LFP Battery Second Life: What Happens After 4,500 Cycles in an EV truck

What 4,500 cycles does to a CATL LFP pack

The Dongfeng TZ3V 6x4 dump truck runs a 350 kWh CATL LFP pack warranted for 8 years or 4,500 cycles, whichever comes first. An electric truck pack does not die at cycle 4,501; it fades to about 70-80 percent of original capacity and keeps working. Understanding that fade curve matters because the pack is the single most valuable component on the truck, and its second life changes both TCO and resale. This guide covers degradation, stationary reuse, buyback programs and the effect on fleet economics.

The degradation curve

LFP chemistry fades slowly and predictably. A well-managed 350 kWh pack typically holds 90 percent state of health at 1,500 cycles, 80 percent near 3,000 cycles and 70-80 percent at the 4,500-cycle warranty end. Unlike NMC, LFP resists thermal runaway and calendar aging, so a truck working hot Chilean copper roads loses capacity from cycling, not from sitting in the sun. Most operators see 8-12 percent range loss over the warranty, not the 30 percent a diesel buyer might fear.

Residual capacity is still useful

A pack at 70 percent of 350 kWh still stores 245 kWh, enough for a second career in stationary storage. At 220 km original range, the truck may show 150-160 km at end of warranty, which is still fine for depot shuttles, port equipment and short mining loops. Fleets often keep the truck and simply accept the shorter range, or move it to a lighter duty cycle rather than scrapping the asset.

Stationary storage reuse

Second-life packs feed solar farms, microgrids, telecom sites and depot peak-shaving. A 245 kWh module can shave a depot demand charge or store midday solar for night charging, exactly the buffer described in solar-hybrid depot designs. Stationary use is gentle: shallow cycles, stable temperature and no vibration, so a pack can serve five to eight more years off the truck, earning $3,000-$8,000 a year in avoided energy cost depending on the market.

Effect on TCO and resale

Because the pack keeps value, a TZ3V sold at year five retains more of its FOB than a diesel, and the second-life pack adds a salvage line the diesel simply lacks. Energy and maintenance savings of $15,000-$20,000 a year already beat diesel; add $4,000-$9,000 of pack buyback and the TCO gap widens further. Resale buyers pay a premium for a pack with documented cycle history, so keep the battery log.

Second-life checklist

  • Track cycles and state of health from day one.
  • Decide buyback versus in-house repurpose before purchase.
  • Ask about pack lease to lower upfront FOB.
  • Plan stationary use where you already have solar or peak charges.
  • Keep documentation to protect used-truck resale value.

Cycle logging best practice

The warranty and the resale price both ride on the cycle log, so meter every pack from first charge. Record date, state of charge in and out, kWh delivered, ambient temperature and depth of discharge; most Dongfeng packs log this automatically through the telematics port, but you still need to export it monthly. A clean log proves the pack hit only 3,000 of its 4,500 cycles at sale, which supports a higher price than a pack with no history. Treat the log like the diesel service book, because buyers now ask for it first.

Second-life case study

A Chilean copper contractor ran TZ3V dumps on a 6 km pit loop for four years, reaching 3,200 cycles at 78 percent state of health. Instead of scrapping, the operator pulled eight packs and built a 1.96 MWh stationary bank at the camp, charging it from a 120 kWp solar array and shaving the site demand charge. The bank saved about $26,000 a year in diesel generator fuel and demand charges, paying back the second-life balance in three years. The trucks then took fresh packs on a lease, so the fleet never lost a truck to the swap.

Recycling floor value

Even at end of life the TZ3V pack is not scrap. The LFP cells hold about $120-$180 per kWh of material value for recycling or rebuild, so a 350 kWh pack at 70 percent state of health is worth $25,000-$40,000 in cells before it leaves the truck. A recycler pays $1,000-$3,000 to take it, but a rebuild shop can regrade the good modules into a 200 kWh stationary pack for a fraction of new cost. Either way the pack closes the loop with positive residual, which is why the TCO above never shows a disposal cost. Some Chilean miners already run a closed loop where the truck pack becomes the camp battery and the camp battery backs the charger, so the same cells serve two careers on one site, and the end-of-life path should be booked at purchase so the value is captured, not binned.
