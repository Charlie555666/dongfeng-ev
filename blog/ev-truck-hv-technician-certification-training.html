Training High-Voltage Technicians: Building an EV Truck Service Workforce

The trucks arrive on a vessel; the competence has to arrive some other way. Every EV truck fleet that stays operational long-term shares one quiet asset: two or more technicians who genuinely understand high-voltage systems — not afraid of them, not casual with them. The good news for fleet operators is that HV competence is a much smaller training problem than most expect: a capable diesel mechanic reaches first-line EV qualification in days, not years, because the safety discipline is procedural and the diagnostics are largely software-guided. This article sets out the curriculum we deliver at fleet commissioning, the staffing model that scales, and the certification ladder that turns a workshop from a liability into a fleet's competitive moat — illustrated with deployments like the TZ3V electric dump truck fleets operating in Ghana's mining belt.

What Technicians Actually Need to Know

The curriculum divides cleanly into four tiers, and — importantly — the tier that covers 80% of workshop days is the shortest one:

The staffing arithmetic follows the tiering: a fleet of 15 trucks wants 4–6 tier-1 technicians and 2 tier-2. Tier-3 work is deliberately rare and handled with our engineers via the diagnostic link — the design philosophy is that the fleet's own people handle everything routine, and nothing routine should require an international phone call.

The Safety Culture That Makes It Work

High-voltage safety is not knowledge; it is ritual. The practices we drill until they are boring:

  • Two-person rule — no HV work alone, ever; the second person is rescue, not assistance.
  • Isolation, verification, lockout — disconnect, then prove zero potential with a tested meter, then lock and tag. The verification step is the one that saves lives when a disconnect fails silently.
  • Gloves dated and tested — HV gloves have a service life and a dielectric test schedule; a cracked Class-0 glove is worse than none because it invites confidence.
  • No exceptions for "quick jobs" — the injury statistics of HV work are dominated by exactly the tasks someone was sure would take two minutes.
  • Rescue drill — the non-contact rescue protocol for an HV casualty, drilled twice a year; you cannot grab a person still in contact with a live conductor.

Fleets sometimes ask whether this culture makes EV service slower than diesel. The answer is that it makes EV service calmer: the tier-1 workload (the overwhelming majority of workshop events) is routine mechanical and software-guided electrical work performed on a de-energised truck, and the discipline surrounding genuine HV work replaces the improvised risk that diesel workshops accept every day with hot components, fuel lines, and rotating machinery.

How the Training Actually Gets Delivered

At fleet commissioning, our commissioning engineers run tiers 0 and 1 on site — the tier-0 session for the whole yard including drivers, and the tier-1 course for the workshop team, delivered on the customer's own trucks so every disconnect, connector, and diagnostic menu is practised on the vehicles they will maintain. Tier-2 candidates either complete a supervised period at the first fleet or attend the regional service partner's programme; certification is documented per technician with refresher requirements, which matters for insurance (carriers increasingly ask for named HV-qualified staff) and for labour compliance. The teaching material — and this is underappreciated — must be in the working language of the workshop; our training materials ship in English, French, Spanish, Russian, and Arabic editions for exactly this reason, and the vocabulary test is simple: if a technician cannot explain the lockout procedure back in their own words, the training has not happened yet.

Retention: The Part Nobody Budgets

A trained HV technician is a market asset, and competitors know it. Fleets that keep their people do three things: they pay a certification allowance (the honest market rate for HV qualification is a modest, well-defined premium); they rotate the interesting work so tier-2 skills are used rather than rusted; and they tie certification to a development path — tier-2 leads to regional advanced roles, sometimes within the manufacturer's service network itself, which turns a retention risk into a career story. The alternative — training two people and hoping — is the single most common self-inflicted EV fleet failure we see, and it is entirely avoidable for the cost of a policy and a small allowance.

The Payoff, Measured

The fleets that invest in the full ladder show it in availability: first-line resolution rates above 85% (no external callout), scheduled maintenance executed in-shift, and battery SOH trajectories that track the engineering model because thermal and connector care happened on schedule. In Ghana's mining belt, the TZ3V operators with trained in-house teams post availability within a point of their old diesel fleets — from a technology two years old to them — while operators who outsourced everything are still flying technicians in. The workforce is the truck fleet's other battery: charge it on schedule and it holds capacity for years.

Curriculum in Practice: What Week One Looks Like

Fleet managers approve training budgets faster when they can see the week, so here is the tier-1 week as it actually runs on the customer's own yard. Day one is theory and fear-removal: what the HV system is, what the orange cabling means, how isolation monitoring works, and the moment that changes every class — the demonstration that the service disconnect renders the drivetrain bus dead, verified live on a truck with the meter, in front of the whole group. Day two is procedure: lockout-tagout until it is muscle memory, the two-person rule, glove inspection and storage, the rescue drill. Days three and four are truck-work: each technician performs the isolation verification, the 12V service items, the coolant and connector inspections, and the guided-diagnostic workflow on every model in the fleet — not on a training rig, on the vehicles they will maintain. Day five is assessment: each candidate runs the full lockout-verify-service-lockup sequence alone while the trainer scores it, then explains the day's fault case — a planted connector fault with its BMS signature — back to the class in their own words. Candidates who can do that pass; the ones who cannot get a second week, and fleets that fund the second week never regret it.

The week's quiet deliverable is cultural rather than technical. Technicians arrive expecting a demotion — the electric trucks "needing specialists" — and leave having discovered the opposite: the diagnostic software walks them through most events, the drivetrain has a fraction of the diesel engine's failure modes, and their mechanical skills transfer wholesale to everything the electric truck still is: a truck. That reframing is what makes the retention problem tractable, because nobody leaves a job they have just been made better at, and the certification allowance plus a development path completes the picture. The mining-belt fleets that crack availability parity with diesel in year one all share the same origin story: a commissioning week when the workshop stopped being afraid and started being the fleet's advantage.

The refresher rhythm that follows — a half-day annually, the rescue drill quarterly, the glove test on schedule — costs a few workshop days a year across a fleet and keeps the certification meaningful for insurers, auditors, and the technicians' own confidence. It is the cheapest insurance a fleet buys, and unlike most insurance, it improves the thing it covers.
