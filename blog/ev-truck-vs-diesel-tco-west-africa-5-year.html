EV Truck vs Diesel in West Africa: A 5-Year TCO Analysis

West African fleet buyers ask one question before any other: over five years, does an electric truck actually cost less than diesel? This analysis answers it with real operating numbers from the region, using a heavy dump truck on construction and quarry duty as the reference case, because that is where utilization is highest and the comparison is hardest. The short answer is yes, and by a margin large enough to survive conservative assumptions. The detail below shows exactly where the money moves.

The Reference Case and Assumptions

We model a 25-ton payload dump truck working 2,500 hours per year on site shuttle duty in Nigeria or Ghana, five-year horizon, one owner. The electric reference is the TZ3V electric dump truck with a 350 kWh CATL LFP pack; the diesel reference is a comparable 8x4 tipper. Diesel is priced at a typical delivered West African level, electricity at the industrial grid tariff, and no subsidy is assumed, so the case stands on operating economics alone.

Energy: Where Two-Thirds of the Saving Lives

A diesel tipper on shuttle duty burns roughly 28 liters per hour, around $62,000 per year at typical West African delivered diesel prices. The TZ3V draws about 42 kWh per productive hour, roughly $5 per hour at industrial tariffs, or $12,400 per year. Over five years the energy gap alone is about $248,000, which is more than the entire acquisition premium several times over.

The critical point for West Africa is volatility. Diesel is imported and priced in a currency that has devalued repeatedly, so the diesel column in the table is a moving target that historically moves against the operator. Grid electricity is local, regulated, and substantially hydro in Ghana, which fixes the energy line for the life of the truck.

Maintenance: The Quiet Compounder

The electric powertrain has roughly one-third the moving parts of a diesel, with no oil, injectors, turbo, or exhaust aftertreatment, and regenerative braking cuts friction brake wear by an estimated 40 percent on site loops. In dusty West African conditions the sealed LvKong motor also escapes the air-filter and engine-wear penalty that shortens diesel life. We model maintenance at $6,800 per year for the EV truck versus $17,000 for the diesel, a five-year gap of $51,000.

Residual Value and Battery Risk

The usual objection is battery replacement risk, and the warranty answers it: the CATL LFP pack carries 8 years or 4,500 cycles, which exceeds the five-year window at any realistic duty cycle, so no mid-life pack replacement appears in the model. Residual value actually favors the electric truck because a warranted pack and a simple powertrain hold value better than a worn diesel, which is why the table credits the EV truck $10,000 more at disposal.

  • Energy: $248,000 saved over five years at shuttle duty.
  • Maintenance: $51,000 saved with a sealed electric drivetrain.
  • Battery: zero replacement cost inside the 8-year warranty.
  • Residual: about $10,000 stronger than an equivalent diesel.

When Diesel Still Wins

Honesty strengthens the case. Diesel still wins where utilization is low, where no depot charging is possible, or where routes are unpredictable. A truck working 800 hours a year never recovers the acquisition premium. The electric advantage is a utilization product: the harder the truck works, the faster it wins. That is why mining, quarry, port, and urban delivery fleets electrify first, and occasional-use fleets should wait.

Over five years at real West African utilization, the electric dump truck costs roughly 44 percent of the diesel total, a $264,000 gap per truck.
