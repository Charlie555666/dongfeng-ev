EV Truck Import Masterclass: Payment Terms, Shipping, Inspection and Delivery From China

Importing an electric truck from China is not difficult — but it is different from importing a diesel truck in a handful of specific, knowable ways, and the buyers who get burned are almost always the ones treated those differences as afterthoughts. Having shipped electric tractors, dump trucks, cargo rigids and special vehicles to fleets across Africa, the Middle East, Southeast Asia, Central Asia and Latin America, we have written this masterclass as the document we wish every client read before their first order: how payment structures really work, why a lithium-battery truck is dangerous-goods cargo, what inspection actually protects you from, and how a delivery becomes a working fleet instead of a yard ornament.

Three principles govern all of them. First, the advance payment buys a production slot against a specified configuration — make sure the configuration sheet (battery size, cab, body, charging standard) is attached to the contract, because that sheet, not the brochure, is what the factory builds. Second, the document release point is your protection: insist on the complete set (commercial invoice, packing list, B/L, certificate of origin, UN 38.3 battery test summary, DG declaration, technical file) before balance payment or LC negotiation. Third, larger first orders should be staged — a client who commissions six trucks before the remaining fourteen ship sleeps better, and so do we; a fleet programme that survives its first month is a fleet programme that scales.

Part 2: Shipping — Why Your Electric Truck Is Dangerous Goods

A truck with an installed lithium traction battery ships under UN 3171, Class 9 (miscellaneous dangerous goods). This changes three things about the freight:

  1. The paperwork set expands. Beyond the standard bill of lading, the shipment carries an IMDG-compliant dangerous goods declaration, the UN 38.3 test summary for the battery pack, and state-of-charge documentation (packs ship at a reduced, transport-approved SOC — typically around 30%). We prepare all of it routinely; the failure mode is a destination broker who has never handled vehicle Class 9 cargo — so brief your clearing agent before the vessel sails, not after it anchors.
  2. Routing is mainstream, not exotic. Every major RORO line (and all breakbulk handlers of experience) move battery-electric vehicles daily on the China lanes — Shanghai, Qingdao, Tianjin, Lianyungang to Mombasa, Durban, Jebel Ali, Jakarta, Manila, Callao, Kolwezi-via-Dar, Poti and beyond. Transit runs 18–45 days by destination. The only routing constraint is choosing a discharge port whose customs office handles DG vehicle declarations smoothly — something we flag per destination from experience.
  3. RORO vs flat-rack is a real choice. RORO is standard and cheaper (roughly USD 6,500–16,000 per heavy unit by destination); flat-rack containerisation costs 10–25% more but consolidates mixed fleets and serves ports without RORO service. Heavies above 3.5 m height or 40 t ride flat-rack or breakbulk by rule.

Part 3: Inspection — What It Actually Protects You From

Pre-shipment inspection (PSI) exists in three forms, and understanding the difference matters:

  • Mandatory government PSI (required for import in several African and Latin American markets — the SGS/Bureau Veritas/Cotecna regimes): a compliance-and-valuation inspection that your customs authority requires; book it during production, not at the port.
  • Voluntary third-party PSI: the same agencies, hired by you, inspecting build conformity to contract, quantity, cosmetic condition and documentation. Costs USD 350–800 for a truck shipment — the cheapest insurance in the transaction.
  • Supplier verification protocol (ours): for every order we provide dated photo/video documentation at build milestones — frame number, battery installation, rolling test, charging test, loading — so the buyer watches their truck move through the factory in real time, and receives the pack as a standing record for registration and insurance.

What PSI cannot do: inspect what is not specified. The most common import disappointment is not defect — it is configuration surprise (the wrong charging standard for the destination, a body variant that doesn't fit local rules). The cure is contractual, not optical: lock the configuration sheet, including the charging standard (GB/T vs CCS2 by market), body dimensions against national road rules, and left/right-hand drive, before the advance payment. Our quotation process forces exactly this closure — it prevents more problems than any inspection.

Part 5: After Arrival — Turning Delivery Into a Fleet

The gap between a truck that landed and a truck that earns lives in five commissioning steps, all standard in our handover package:

  1. Post-transit battery inspection. After weeks at sea at reduced SOC, verify isolation resistance, thermal system function and charge the pack on a controlled first cycle. One disciplined day that protects a six-figure asset.
  2. High-voltage safety induction for the maintenance crew (half-day, certified): PPE, lockout, emergency procedures — the foundation everything else builds on.
  3. Driver familiarisation: regenerative braking behaviour loaded, charging discipline, silent-operation awareness around pedestrians.
  4. Charger commissioning: the depot charge posts (ordered with the trucks, installed in parallel) energised, tested and integrated with the site's electrical protection scheme.
  5. Telemetry activation: fleet dashboards live from day one — ours in Xi'an monitoring pack health alongside yours, which is how remote support actually works across an ocean.

Fleets that skip steps one and four pay for it in year one; fleets that run all five rarely call us with anything harder than a parts request.

Part 6: The Seven Mistakes That Recur

  • Ordering trucks before the charging plan — grid studies take months; start both clocks together.
  • Misclassification at customs — confirm the EV tariff line in writing before ordering; battery-electric trucks do not always share diesel tariff codes.
  • An unbriefed broker meeting a DG declaration for the first time — the number-one cause of port delay on EV imports.
  • Skipping PSI to save USD 400 — reliably regretted.
  • No opening spares package — a 2–3%-of-fleet-value kit of wear items and critical electronics is cheap uptime in remote markets.
  • Configuration ambiguity — charging standard and body spec locked by contract, not by assumption.
  • Paying 100% before seeing anything — staged shipments protect serious buyers on both sides of the ocean.

The Timeline, End to End

For a typical 10-unit fleet order: contract and configuration (week 0) → production (weeks 1–6) → inspection and DG documentation (week 6) → balance payment / LC draw (week 7) → vessel and transit (weeks 7–12, destination-dependent) → customs, clearance, inland haul (weeks 12–15) → commissioning and training (weeks 15–16). Sixteen weeks from signature to working fleet is the honest planning number for most destinations — buyers who are told four weeks are being told stories.
