Ports are the perfect electric truck environment

A container port is a closed loop: fixed routes, short distances, heavy loads, constant stop-start and a depot within metres of the action. That is exactly the duty cycle where a TE9L electric tractor outperforms diesel economically and environmentally. At Hai Phong and Cat Lai in Vietnam, the combination of high utilisation, cheap depot charging and growing pressure to cut port-area emissions makes electric drayage one of the clearest business cases in freight.

Docket to gate: the daily duty cycle

A typical port tractor shuttles between the terminal, the yard and the customer or railhead, often covering 150-250 km in a shift with many stops. The TE9L with 420-466 kWh and a real-world range of 300-350 km fits this pattern well, because the truck returns to base frequently enough to top up. The heavy loads and constant acceleration that punish a diesel’s fuel economy and brakes are precisely where the electric drivetrain’s torque and regenerative braking shine.

Charging at the port depot

Port operators control their land, which makes charging straightforward. A TE9L fleet can be charged overnight on AC and topped up between shifts on DC fast chargers sited at the yard. Because the trucks are never far from base, range anxiety is a non-issue and the depot can run entirely on its own solar or off-peak power. At Hai Phong and Cat Lai, this turns the fuel line of the operating budget into a fixed electricity cost.

  • Zero tailpipe emissions: compliant with low-emission zones in Hanoi, Ho Chi Minh City and Hai Phong.
  • Lower energy cost: electricity in Vietnam is far cheaper per km than diesel.

  • Regenerative braking: recovers energy on the long-haul and heavy corridor work cycle.

Emissions rules in and around ports

Ports sit at the centre of urban air-quality regulation, and many now impose their own emission requirements on drayage fleets. In Vietnam, the direction is toward zero-emission port vehicles over the coming decade. A TE9L is compliant with rules that are still being written, which means Vietnamese drayage operators who electrify early avoid both future restrictions and future retrofit costs.

Uptime, reliability and shift patterns

Port work runs around the clock, so availability is everything. An electric tractor has fewer wear parts than a diesel, which supports higher availability, but the fleet must plan charging around shift patterns so that no truck is caught short. With TE9L units on a two- or three-shift rotation, we design a charge plan that guarantees each truck leaves the yard full, using staggered overnight charging and a fast-charger buffer.

The economics of electric drayage

Drayage is high-mileage, high-idle work, which magnifies the electric advantage. The TE9L eliminates idling fuel burn at the gate, cuts energy cost per kilometre sharply, and reduces brake maintenance thanks to regenerative braking. For Vietnamese operators at Hai Phong and Cat Lai, the total cost per container moved is lower than diesel and predictable quarter to quarter. As port emission rules tighten, the electric tractor also protects the operator’s licence to work.

Why Vietnam is ready for electric trucks now

The conditions that make an electric truck viable are all present in Vietnam. Decision 876 EV targets and a low-emission-zone push in Hanoi. Freight demand concentrates in and around Hanoi, Ho Chi Minh City and Hai Phong, where routes are short and predictable and the depot is never far away. Fuel logistics add cost and delay that a fleet charging its own vehicles simply avoids. For a TE9L electric tractor on a garment, seafood and electronics logistics duty cycle, this is not an experimental technology but a practical replacement for diesel, and the operators moving first are the ones who lock in the lowest cost per kilometre before their competitors do.

A step-by-step implementation plan

Electrifying a fleet in Vietnam is a project, and running it in phases is what keeps it manageable. Phase one is a site and route audit: list every duty cycle, measure real daily distance and load, and map the depot power supply. Phase two is a pilot of two to five TE9L units on the most predictable routes, with chargers installed and drivers trained. Phase three is measurement — energy cost per kilometre, uptime, maintenance hours. Phase four is scaling what the data supports. Each phase de-risks the next and keeps the capital commitment matched to proven performance rather than optimism.

Choosing the right configuration

Within the TE9L range there are choices that matter more than cosmetics. The 6x4 layout suits the axle load and traction pattern of your duty cycle. Battery capacity of 420-466 kWh should be sized to the longest realistic daily route plus a safety margin, not to the biggest number on the brochure. Body and equipment specification — tipper, box, reefer, tank or compactor — should be matched precisely to the job in Vietnam. Getting these four decisions right at order stage is far cheaper than modifying a truck after it lands.
