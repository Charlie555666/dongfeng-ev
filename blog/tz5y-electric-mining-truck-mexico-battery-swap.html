Why Swapping Beats Plugging in a Mexican Pit

  • Utilisation is everything. A pit truck earning revenue per tonne hauled cannot sit on a charger for 40–60 minutes. A swap station exchanges the empty pack for a full one in 5–6 minutes — comparable to a diesel refuelling stop, preserving 23+ operating hours per truck per day.
  • Grid capacity at remote mines is limited. Sonoran and Sierra Madre mine sites often have modest interconnections. A swap station charges its pack inventory continuously at a controlled rate (typically 9 packs cycling on 600–900 kW average draw), smoothing demand instead of imposing 1 MW+ fast-charge spikes. One station serves a 10–20 truck fleet without a grid upgrade.
  • Solar synergy is exceptional. Northern Mexican mines sit under some of the best solar irradiance in the world. A 2–4 MW solar array feeding the swap station's charger inventory can supply 40–60% of haul energy at a mining-class LCOE — energy cost per kWh far below diesel equivalent, with the mine's own generation.
  • Descent regen is free money. Loaded trucks descending from pit benches regenerate significant energy back into the pack. On routes with 150–300 m of descent per cycle, regen returns 10–18% of consumed energy — diesel trucks turn the same elevation into brake heat and wear.

Import Route, Duties and the USMCA Question

Mexico applies its normal commercial-truck import regime to Chinese-origin heavy trucks — duty plus possible compensatory measures depending on classification, and NOM (Norma Oficial Mexicana) emissions/technical compliance for registration. Because mining trucks operate on private concession roads, many units register under off-road/mining-equipment categories, which materially changes the compliance path versus public-road trucks — your customs broker in Veracruz, Manzanillo or Lázaro Cárdenas should confirm the classification strategy before ordering. We supply the complete document set: UN 38.3 test summary, IMDG Class 9 declaration for the 600 kWh pack, technical file for NOM review, and certificate of origin. Note that for mining use, buyers occasionally structure purchases through the mine's operating subsidiary with equipment-list treatment — a decision for the buyer's tax and customs advisors, but one that has repeatedly improved landed economics for our mining clients.

Altitude and Heat Notes for Mexican Belts

Sonoran pits run hot (40 °C+ summer working conditions) and Sierra Madre operations climb past 2,500 m. Electric drivetrains hold full power at altitude — no turbocharger derating — which is a genuine productivity advantage over diesel at high sites; the CATL pack's liquid cooling manages Sonoran heat, the same architecture we ship into GCC and African operations. Dust is the third adversary: we fit the sealed HV architecture with IP68 connectors and a reinforced filtration regime, and the swap station's enclosed pack racks protect battery inventory from blast-fines accumulation.

Station Siting and Pack Inventory: The Engineering Detail That Decides Success

A swap deployment succeeds or fails on station geometry. The rules we apply when siting a CAS station for a Mexican pit:

  • Place the station on the loaded-path origin, not the midpoint. Trucks swap at the loader or crusher end of the cycle, where they already queue — the 5-6 minute exchange absorbs into dwell time that exists anyway. A mid-route station creates a dedicated stop that costs pure schedule.
  • Size the pack inventory to cycle time, not truck count. The arithmetic: each truck consumes one pack per (cycle time / pack endurance) interval; the station needs enough packs cycling that a full pack is always ready. For a 15-truck fleet on 40-minute cycles with two-cycle pack endurance, an 9-pack station with 600-900 kW average charging keeps every truck swapping without waiting.
  • Orient for dust and blast direction. The station's enclosed pack racks face away from the active face; blast-hour protocols move racks to charging positions in advance. Mexican blast schedules are disciplined and predictable, which makes this a calendar routine rather than an operational scramble.
  • Plan the solar interleave. Northern Mexican solar peaks midday; pit haul peaks are typically dawn-to-mid-morning and mid-afternoon-to-dusk. The station charges its inventory fastest exactly when the array produces hardest — a coincidence of profiles that lifts the solar share of haul energy above what naive capacity math suggests.

The Pilot Structure We Recommend to Mexican Operators

Mining procurement is conservative everywhere, and Mexican mine management is no exception — appropriately so, since haulage availability is revenue. The pilot structure that clears internal approvals efficiently:

  1. Three trucks plus one station on the highest-tonnage short cycle (the route where electric duty is easiest and savings per day are largest). Thirty-day instrumented baseline on the diesel trucks running that route first, so the comparison is against real numbers, not budget assumptions.
  2. Ninety-day validation with weekly reporting: tonnage per truck-day, availability, kWh and cost per tonne-km, swap-station uptime, driver feedback. The weekly cadence matters — mining management moves on data rhythm, and a pilot that reports weekly builds the institutional confidence a full conversion requires.
  3. A written scale trigger: agree before the pilot what thresholds trigger fleet conversion (typically: availability at or above diesel, cost per tonne-km below 50% of diesel, station uptime above 95%). When the thresholds clear — and on short-cycle Mexican routes they clear early — the conversion decision is already made; only the schedule needs approving.
