Why the Beira Corridor Demands a Purpose-Built EV Truck

Port drayage is unlike long-haul freight. A terminal tractor typically travels 8 to 25 kilometers per shift, moving between quay, stacking yard, Customs-bonded warehouse and rail siding. It idles heavily, starts and stops constantly, and rarely exceeds 40 km/h inside the gate. These duty cycles are exactly where a battery-electric truck wins, because regenerative braking recovers energy on every stop and the electric drivetrain delivers full torque from zero rpm, which matters when pulling a loaded chassis from a standstill.

For a Mozambican operator, diesel terminal tractors burn 18 to 24 liters per 100 km of yard running, and in 2025 that fuel cost landed near USD 1.15 per liter at the port gate. A TE46 running on grid or solar power at USD 0.18 per kWh cuts that energy line by more than 70 percent. The corridor also faces strict engine-idle limits inside Beira port, and an electric truck simply has no idle emissions to police.

Port Drayage Duty Cycle and Real Range

On the Beira quay a typical TE46 shift looks like this: 22 km of loaded moves, 14 km of empty returns, 9 km of spotting at the rail head, and roughly 45 minutes of creep speed at the gate. Total energy draw lands near 150 kWh across a 10-hour shift, leaving comfortable reserve inside the 282 kWh pack. Operators report 180 to 240 km of effective range per charge, which covers two to three full shifts on a single overnight charge if depot charging is used.

  • Shift 1 (06:00 to 16:00): container moves from berth to yard, ~90 kWh

  • Opportunity charging: 60-minute DC top-up during meal break adds ~120 km
  • Battery swap: full 282 kWh pack replaced in under 6 minutes for 24/7 yards

Charging Infrastructure Along the Corridor

Beira already has 33 kV substation capacity inside the port free zone, and most yards can host a 120 kW DC charger for under USD 35,000 installed. For fleets running the inland leg toward Chimoio and Tete, a solar-plus-storage microgrid at the depot turns a 60-minute charge into a near-zero-cost event. The TE46 accepts CCS2 DC up to 150 kW and AC up to 22 kW, so it slots into almost any existing truck-charging cabinet already deployed for Dongfeng TE46 electric tractor units operating across the region.

Operating Cost and Payback for Mozambique Fleets

Consider a yard running 6 tractors across two shifts, 300 days per year. Diesel units consume about 54,000 liters annually at USD 1.15, equal to USD 62,100 in fuel. The same electric truck fleet draws about 320,000 kWh at USD 0.18, equal to USD 57,600, but with on-site solar that falls to roughly USD 22,000. Maintenance savings add another USD 9,000 per year because an electric drivetrain has no oil, no turbo, no exhaust aftertreatment and fewer brake wears from regen. Net annual saving per tractor is USD 11,000 to 16,000, giving a payback of 4 to 6 years before the battery-warranty horizon even ends.

Planning depot charging for this EV truck

Charging an electric truck is a depot discipline, not a public-network gamble. A 262 kWh pack working a 200 km day typically returns with 25 to 35 percent in reserve, so overnight demand is about 157 kWh per truck. On a 120 kW DC charger the pack climbs from 20 to 80 percent in roughly 79 minutes; on 240 kW that falls to about 39 minutes, which lets two trucks share one charger on staggered shifts.

Size the site transformer for your busiest shift plus 30 percent headroom, keep cable runs under 30 metres to limit voltage drop, and meter each charger separately so energy cost per truck stays visible. In South Africa, grid connection lead times of three to nine months are common, so apply for the connection the day you sign the truck order, not the day the ship arrives. Energy cost per kilometre: EV truck versus diesel

A comparable diesel terminal tractor burns about 0.38 litres per kilometre in mixed duty; at $0.95 per litre that is roughly $0.36 per km. The electric equivalent consumes about 1.15 kWh per km fully loaded; at an industrial tariff of $0.10 per kWh the cost is near $0.12 per km, and overnight or solar-coupled charging can push it below $0.08. Over 70,000 km a year the saving is about $17,000 per truck, before any carbon or low-emission-zone benefit.

The gap widens in stop-start duty, where regenerative braking returns 15 to 25 percent of traction energy instead of burning it as brake dust. In South Africa, where diesel quality and price both fluctuate, locking in an electricity tariff also removes a major budgeting variable from the fleet P&L.
