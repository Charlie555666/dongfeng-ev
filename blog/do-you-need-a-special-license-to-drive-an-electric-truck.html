Do You Need a Special License to Drive an Electric Truck?

Quick answer: no special license is required - electric trucks are licensed by gross vehicle weight exactly like diesel equivalents, so your drivers' existing classes carry over. What fleets should add is a one-day high-voltage awareness and regen-driving orientation, which insurers and public tenders increasingly expect to see documented.

Which License Class Covers an Electric Truck?

The same class that covers the diesel equivalent, in nearly every market we ship to - because licensing follows gross vehicle weight and vehicle category, not drivetrain. A 140 kWh box truck such as the KT5M electric box truck sits in the same weight class as its diesel counterpart, so the license your drivers already hold covers the electric version. This is the rule across Vietnam, the Gulf states, most of Africa and Latin America: the registration category is by GVW, the endorsement is by vehicle class, and none of it references the fuel. The exceptions buyers should verify are niche: a few jurisdictions require a brief orientation certificate for zero-emission vehicles, and some municipal fleets impose internal policies stricter than the law requires - policy, not regulation.

Do Any Markets Require an EV-Specific Endorsement?

Driver-side, almost none; workshop-side, yes in practice. What markets increasingly regulate is who may service the high-voltage system: certified technicians for HV work, with inspection regimes referencing the vehicle's safety approval. For drivers, the practical requirement that does appear is employer or insurer policy - some underwriters writing policies on electric fleets ask for documented high-voltage awareness training, and some public tenders score it. None of this is a license category: it is a training record, completed in a day. The KT5M ships with the isolation-point documentation and the training deck that satisfies both, so fleets in Vietnam can put the certificate in the driver file the week the truck arrives.

What Driving Differences Should Training Cover?

The license is unchanged; the technique is worth a day. Four differences matter on duty: one-pedal familiarity - regenerative braking changes pedal feel, and drivers should learn the lift-and-coast technique that recovers energy; creep behavior - the electric driveline moves the truck the moment the brake releases, unlike an idling diesel; instant torque - loaded launches are effortless, and new drivers need a ramp limit understanding for wet-weather traction; and the silence - dock and yard workers accustomed to hearing engines need visual protocols instead. None of these requires a licensing authority; all of them are covered in the commissioning driver orientation we include with every fleet delivery, typically four to six hours of range and yard time.

How Does Importing Change Licensing and Compliance?

Import compliance and driver licensing are separate tracks that buyers sometimes conflate. The import track is the vehicle's: CR type approval or the equivalent conformity route for Vietnam, the customs file, and registration - none of which touches the driver. The licensing track is the person's: the GVW-based class already held. Where fleets add days rather than months is when they assume the vehicle approval includes a driver requirement and delay dispatch to chase a nonexistent endorsement. The faster path: confirm the license class against the truck's gross weight at order, book the HV-awareness briefing into commissioning week, and put both documents in the driver file. What About Insurance and Fleet Policy Requirements?

Insurers price the truck, and the terms ride on documentation. Hull and liability premiums on electric trucks rate the battery - replacement cost and thermal-management certification - and some underwriters attach a condition requiring that drivers complete an EV-familiarization briefing and that only certified technicians open the HV system. Both conditions are satisfied by the standard commissioning package: the driver orientation record and the technician certification from our training program. Fleet-internal policies are the third layer, and they should be written deliberately: charging procedure authorization, key control for silent vehicles in yards, and the emergency response card for first responders. None of these changes a license; all of them close the gaps insurers and safety auditors actually inspect.

What Should a Fleet Do in the First Week?

A five-step onboarding closes the licensing question permanently. First, verify each driver's license class against the truck's gross weight - a formality, but document it. Second, run the driver orientation: regen technique, charging procedure, silence protocols, four to six hours. Third, file the high-voltage awareness certificates for drivers and the technician certification for workshop staff. Fourth, brief yard and dock staff on the vehicle-silent protocol. Fifth, register the training records with the insurer if the policy carries the condition. From week two, the electric truck is administered exactly like the diesel it replaced - same license renewals, same medical checks, same compliance calendar.
