KT5L Electric Cargo Truck Fleet in Colombia: Depot Power Upgrade Guide

Electrifying a cargo fleet in Colombia starts at the depot, not the road, and an electric truck lives or dies by its charging infrastructure. The KT5L electric cargo truck runs a 350 kWh CATL LFP pack, a 300 kW motor and 260-320 km of range, priced at $78,000-$98,000 FOB. This guide sizes the transformer, plans the load and controls charging cost for a Bogota depot running a KT5L fleet.

Depot Transformer Sizing Basics

The simplest rule is to size for simultaneous charging, not total fleet energy. Six KT5L trucks each drawing 120 kW equals 720 kW of continuous demand. Adding headroom for other depot loads and future growth points to an 800 kVA transformer as the practical minimum. Undersizing causes voltage drop and tripped breakers; oversizing wastes connection capacity. The Colombia electric truck market sees many first-time operators underestimate this step and then pay for a costly upgrade within a year of launch.

Smart Charging to Flatten the Peak

A depot rarely needs all trucks charging at full power at once. Staggered charging and a management system that caps total draw keep the peak near the transformer limit. Practical steps include:

  • Set a depot cap at 700 kW so the 800 kVA transformer stays comfortable.
  • Sequence charging by departure time, filling the early trucks first.
  • Use overnight off-peak windows when Colombian grid rates are lowest.
  • Pre-cool cabs while plugged in to avoid draining the pack on site.

Charging Cost Control in Bogota

Colombian commercial electricity runs roughly $0.10-$0.18 per kWh depending on the tariff and time of use. Charging a 350 kWh pack overnight costs about $35-$63, covering 260-320 km. At 2.0 kWh per km that is $0.13-$0.20 per km, against $0.30-$0.40 per km for diesel in Bogota traffic. The KT5L electric cargo truck therefore cuts energy cost by roughly half on a city route, and the saving compounds across a multi-truck depot over the year.

Power Connection and Permitting

A new 800 kVA connection in Colombia requires a utility study, a transformer enclosure and protection gear. Budget 8-16 weeks for the utility process and $20,000-$50,000 for the substation depending on distance from the nearest feeder. The Colombia electric truck market rewards operators who start the connection application before the trucks arrive, because the depot, not the truck, is the long lead item and a delayed transformer can ground an entire fleet on delivery day.

Battery Health and Depot Design

Liquid-cooled CATL LFP packs prefer a steady charge rhythm. A depot designed with weather protection, cable management and a 360 kW DC option for fast turns keeps the 8-year or 4,500-cycle warranty intact. The KT5L's 300 kW motor and 260-320 km range mean most Bogota routes need only one charge per shift, so a modest charger count serves a surprisingly large fleet without crowding the yard or exceeding the transformer limit during peak demand.

Solar and Renewable Charging at the Depot

Colombian depots with roof space can cut charging cost further by adding solar and a small battery buffer that charges the KT5L fleet during the day. A 100 kW rooftop array offsets a meaningful share of the 700 kW depot peak and shelters the operator from tariff rises. The KT5L electric cargo truck charges happily from mixed solar and grid supply, and the liquid-cooled pack tolerates the variable input. In the Colombia electric truck market, pairing the right transformer with on-site generation is the lowest-risk path to a sub-$0.15 per km energy cost.

Scaling the Depot With the Fleet

As the fleet grows from 6 to 20 trucks, the transformer steps up to 2,500-3,000 kVA and the charge bays multiply. Planning the trenching and switchgear for the final size at the start avoids costly rework.

Load Management and Demand Charges

Beyond the transformer size, Colombian operators must watch demand charges, which bill the peak 15-minute draw each month. A depot that lets all six KT5L trucks charge at once spikes the demand and raises the bill even if total energy is modest. A charge management system that caps the simultaneous draw near 700 kW keeps the demand charge controlled and protects the 800 kVA transformer. The KT5L electric cargo truck tolerates a slower overnight charge, so the system can shift load into the cheapest windows without hurting the morning dispatch. The Colombia electric truck market has time-of-use tariffs that reward this discipline, and a well-tuned depot can cut its monthly bill by 15-25% versus an unmanaged one that peaks every evening.

Phasing the Depot Build With Truck Deliveries

The depot need not be finished before the first trucks arrive, but the power connection must be, because the utility lead time of 8-16 weeks dwarfs the truck transit. A practical phasing starts with the 800 kVA transformer and two chargers, then adds bays as the fleet grows from 6 to 10 to 20 trucks. The KT5L electric cargo truck arrives ready to charge on the first day if the enclosure and cable are staged ahead of delivery. The Colombia electric truck market first movers who file the utility study early and build the trenching for the final size avoid the costly mid-project upgrade that strands a half-built depot when the fleet expands ahead of plan.

Maintenance of the Charging Equipment

The depot is only as reliable as its chargers, so a simple maintenance plan protects the investment. Filter the cooling intake, inspect cables for wear and run a monthly insulation test on the 800 kVA transformer to catch issues before they strand a KT5L. The KT5L electric cargo truck depends on a working bay each morning, and a failed charger is a missed dispatch. The Colombia electric truck market operators who contract a local electrician for quarterly service avoid the costly downtime that an unmanaged depot eventually suffers once the warranty on the charger expires.

Grants and Incentives for Colombian EV Depots

Colombia and several city governments offer incentives for commercial electrification, and a depot project should capture them rather than leaving money on the table. The support ranges from accelerated depreciation on the chargers to local rebates on the transformers and, in some programmes, a per-vehicle credit that lowers the effective $78,000-$98,000 FOB of the KT5L. Importers should check the current Bogota and national schemes at the planning stage, because the paperwork lead time can be as long as the utility connection. The KT5L electric cargo truck qualifies under most clean-freight definitions, and pairing the truck purchase with a documented depot plan strengthens the application. The Colombia electric truck market first movers who stack the utility saving, the time-of-use arbitrage and the incentive together reach the lowest total cost of ownership in the region for an electric cargo fleet.

Depot Safety and Ventilation

An EV depot needs basic but disciplined safety: clear high-voltage signage, a fire-suppression plan suited to lithium packs and good ventilation in the charge bay. The KT5L's liquid-cooled CATL LFP chemistry is among the safest cell types, and the 8-year warranty assumes a dry, monitored charge environment. The KT5L electric cargo truck charges happily in a standard covered bay, so the cost is procedure and training rather than special construction. The Colombia electric truck market insurers look favourably on a documented depot safety plan, which can also soften the premium on the fleet policy and protect the $78,000-$98,000 FOB asset from avoidable incidents during the long Bogota service life.
