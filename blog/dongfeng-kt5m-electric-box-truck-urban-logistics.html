City distribution is the highest-stakes logistics game in emerging markets: thin margins, dense traffic, low-speed duty with constant stopping, and delivery windows that start before dawn. It is also where an electric truck is most decisively superior to diesel — stop-start traffic is poison for a diesel engine and free efficiency for an electric one. Why Stop-Start City Duty Loves Electric Drivetrains

  • Zero idle loss. A diesel box truck in dense city delivery spends 30–40% of engine time idling at kerbs and in queues, burning fuel and clogging its DPF. An electric drivetrain consumes nothing at standstill and never needs a forced DPF regeneration.
  • Regenerative braking. Every one of a city driver's hundreds of daily decelerations feeds energy back — we typically log 15–20% recovery in distribution duty, extending range and halving brake wear.
  • Silence at delivery windows. Early-morning and residential-zone deliveries without noise complaints are a genuine operational capability — more legal operating hours per vehicle per day.
  • Clean air access. Low-emission zones and city-centre access restrictions increasingly exclude older diesels; an electric truck is permanently compliant.

Daily Duty Economics

A representative city distribution fleet: 120 km/day of dense delivery duty, 320 days a year — 38,400 km annually. The diesel comparison burns 28 L/100 km in this profile: 10,750 litres, USD 11,000–13,000 at typical prices. The KT5M consumes roughly 0.9–1.1 kWh/km: about 38,000 kWh, USD 3,400–4,600 at commercial tariffs. Maintenance on the diesel (city duty is its worst enemy: EGR coking, DPF, stop-start clutch and turbo stress) runs USD 3,500–5,000 annually; the KT5M driveline needs roughly a third of that. Net annual advantage: USD 9,000–13,000 per truck. On the 310 kWh specification at FOB USD 95,000–105,000, the premium over an 18 t diesel unit pays back in 2.5–4 years depending on local fuel pricing — the fastest payback of any duty cycle in our product range.

Fleet Workflow: How a Day Actually Runs

  1. 04:30 — Pre-dawn departure. Truck leaves the depot at 100% SOC from overnight 180 kW charging; silence keeps residential neighbours indifferent.
  2. 06:00–14:00 — Delivery waves. Regional distribution centre runs, then multi-drop city routes. Each stop regenerates a little energy; kWh/km stays under 1.1.
  3. 14:00 — Depot return. SOC typically 45–60% at end of shift; 90-minute top-up on the DC charger during the afternoon trough.
  4. 16:00 — Optional second wave. Same-day retail replenishment using the regained range.
  5. 22:00 — Full overnight charge at low tariff, ready for the next 04:30 departure.
