How Much Does It Cost to Charge an Electric Truck?

Quick answer: Charging an electric truck typically costs $18 to $60 for a full 282 to 600 kWh pack, or $0.03 to $0.12 per km, at a commercial tariff of $0.05 to $0.18 per kWh. That is 60 to 75 percent cheaper than a diesel truck at $0.25 to $0.45 per km.

How much does a full charge actually cost?

The simplest way to price a charge is pack size times your tariff. A light urban KT5M electric cargo truck with a 282 kWh pack costs $14 to $51 for a full charge, while a 600 kWh long-haul tractor costs $30 to $108. Most fleet depots negotiate a commercial rate near $0.12 per kWh, so a 350 kWh mid-size truck lands at about $42 per full charge. The spread exists because tariffs vary from $0.05 per kWh on Nigerian off-peak industrial supply to $0.18 per kWh on South African daytime commercial rates, and public charger networks add another $0.10 to $0.20 per kWh on top.

What is the cost per kilometre for an EV truck?

Per-km cost depends on consumption and tariff. A 282 to 424 kWh EV truck drawing 1.2 to 1.8 kWh per km costs $0.06 to $0.18 per km at $0.05 to $0.12 per kWh, against $0.25 to $0.45 per km for a diesel truck burning 1.0 to 1.4 L per km at $0.9 to $1.4 per litre. The crossover is clear: even at the most expensive grid tariff, electric stays under diesel on energy alone, before you count the 35 percent lower maintenance of an EV powertrain.

For a distribution fleet running 200 km a day, the KT5M at 1.5 kWh per km and $0.12 per kWh costs $36 per day in electricity versus $70 to $90 in diesel, a $1.2 million saving per 30-truck fleet over five years.

Which tariff should a fleet depot negotiate?

Most commercial tariffs split into peak, standard and off-peak bands, and the off-peak window of 22:00 to 06:00 is where electric trucks should charge. Booking a demand-charge managed supply and shifting bulk charging to off-peak can cut the effective rate from $0.18 to $0.08 per kWh. Fleets should also ask the utility for an EV or green tariff, which several African and Middle Eastern grids now offer at a 10 to 20 percent discount in exchange for managed load.

  • Off-peak depot charging: $0.05 to $0.10 per kWh.
  • Standard daytime commercial: $0.12 to $0.18 per kWh.
  • Public DC network: $0.20 to $0.35 per kWh plus idle fees.
  • Solar plus storage: $0.02 to $0.06 per kWh amortised.

Are home or solar chargers worth it for fleets?

For a single owner-operator, a 7 to 22 kW wallbox at $0.10 per kWh is enough overnight, but a fleet needs 60 to 240 kW DC units. Solar plus battery is where the real saving appears: a 100 kW array with 200 kWh storage offsets 30 to 50 percent of a 10-truck depot's daytime top-up and can cut the annual bill by $15,000 to $30,000. The payback on the solar system is typically 4 to 6 years, after which the energy is nearly free for the life of the panels.

Can battery swapping reduce charging downtime and cost?

Battery swapping trades a depleted pack for a charged one in 5 to 6 minutes, eliminating the 45 to 90 minute charge wait for terminal tractors, port shuttles and quarry trucks that run 24-hour shifts. The TE46 terminal tractor swaps in 6 minutes, so a Mombasa drayage fleet keeps moving without a charger queue. Swapping shifts the cost from on-truck charging time to a centralised swap station that charges packs slowly off-peak, often at the cheapest tariff, which can lower per-kWh cost by another 15 to 25 percent.

What hidden costs should fleets budget for?

Beyond energy, fleets should budget three items: the depot connection upgrade, typically $5,000 to $25,000 for a 200 to 700 kW supply; the charger hardware, $15,000 to $60,000 per 120 to 240 kW DC unit; and a load-management controller, $2,000 to $5,000, that caps demand charges. These are one-time capital items amortised over the truck life, and they are far smaller than the diesel fuel they replace. Most fleets recover the charging infrastructure inside 18 to 30 months through the per-km saving alone.

Driver training and a high-voltage safety course add about $300 to $600 per operator, a negligible line against the five-figure annual fuel saving per truck. Telematics, usually included, shows kWh per km so the finance team can verify the business case monthly.

How do regional tariffs compare across export markets?

Tariffs vary widely by market, which is why the same truck shows different cost per km by country. Nigeria off-peak industrial supply reaches $0.05 per kWh, South Africa daytime commercial peaks at $0.18 per kWh, and Kenya and Ghana sit in the $0.09 to $0.16 per kWh band. A fleet operating across borders should negotiate a green or EV tariff everywhere and shift bulk charging to off-peak, which typically cuts the effective rate by 30 to 50 percent versus standard commercial pricing.

Should you charge or swap for your duty cycle?

Charge suits fleets with predictable overnight or break windows, while swap suits 24-hour terminal, port and quarry duty where a 45 to 90 minute charge loses moves. The TE46 terminal tractor swaps in 6 minutes, keeping drayage moving, whereas a distribution fleet charging at the depot overnight rarely needs swap. Match the model to the shift: charge for routed delivery, swap for continuous shuttle, and the cost per productive hour drops accordingly.
