TE9L Electric Tractor on South Africa's N3 Durban Corridor

The TE9L electric tractor is a 510 kW long-haul EV truck built for the busiest freight lane in South Africa, the N3 between Durban and Johannesburg. This brief gives fleet operators the specifications, a corridor charging plan, the per-km cost and the import steps for running the TE9L long-haul electric tractor on one of Africa's highest-volume routes.

Why the N3 corridor suits an electric prime mover

The N3 carries more than 7 million tonnes of freight a year between the Port of Durban and the Gauteng industrial heartland, almost all of it in 49-tonne combinations. The 570 km corridor is a steady highway profile with two long climbs around the Drakensberg that actually suit an electric truck, because regenerative braking recovers energy on the descent that a diesel unit wastes as heat. At South Africa's diesel price near $1.20 per litre, a diesel prime mover burns 32 to 38 litres per 100 km at 49 t, costing $0.38 to $0.46 per km. The TE9L cuts that energy line by 60 to 70 percent using grid electricity at $0.09 to $0.18 per kWh.

The corridor also has the grid and the freight density to support a charging network, which is why it is the first route where South African fleets can run electric tractors without range anxiety.

Corridor charging plan: Durban to Johannesburg

For the 570 km run, the 600 kWh pack covers the distance with margin, but most fleets prefer a single top-up at the Harrismith midpoint to keep reserve for the return load. A 240 kW DC charger at a Harrismith truck stop restores 20 to 80 percent in about 60 minutes, which fits the legally required driver rest break. Planning the charge into the mandatory rest turns downtime into compliance rather than lost hours.

Telematics from pilot runs show the TE9L arrives in Gauteng with 12 to 18 percent reserve even after the Drakensberg climb, and the descent alone recovers 30 to 45 kWh back into the pack. This makes the corridor one of the few long-haul lanes where electric already works on today's battery sizes.

Cost per kilometre versus a diesel prime mover

The metric that decides a corridor fleet is total cost per km at 49 t:

  • Energy: TE9L at 1.4 to 1.8 kWh per km and $0.12 per kWh = $0.17 to $0.22 per km.
  • Diesel equivalent: 35 L per 100 km at $1.20 per litre = $0.42 per km, roughly double.
  • Maintenance: no AdBlue, no engine rebuilds, regen brakes — about 35 percent lower per km.
  • Tolls: the N3 toll of about $0.05 per km applies equally, so it is neutral between fuels.

Over a 1,000,000-km five-year life a single TE9L avoids roughly $200,000 in fuel and $60,000 in maintenance against a diesel unit, comfortably covering the FOB premium of $125,000 to $150,000 inside 30 to 40 months of two-shift corridor operation.

Charging infrastructure and grid connection

A corridor depot at each end needs four 240 kW DC chargers on a managed 1 MW supply, with a load balancer to avoid peak demand penalties. South African daytime commercial tariffs reach $0.18 per kWh, so scheduling bulk charging overnight at $0.09 per kWh halves the energy bill. A 250 kW solar and battery system at the Durban depot offsets midday top-ups for local distribution units that share the site.

Community and emissions benefits

Residents near the Harrismith and Van Reenen passes report less truck noise at night once electric units replace idling diesels at the rest stops, and the port-to-Gauteng corridor becomes a showcase for South African freight decarbonisation that financiers increasingly reward with green terms.
