Inside the LFP battery that powers your truck

The heart of any electric truck is its battery, and the TZ5E uses a lithium-iron-phosphate (LFP) pack of 350 kWh supplied by CATL. LFP chemistry was chosen deliberately: it is more thermally stable, tolerates deep cycling better and lasts longer than many alternatives. For Peru fleets, that translates into a pack designed for heavy daily duty, a warranty of eight years or 4,500 cycles, and a markedly lower fire risk than chemistries such as NMC.

Why LFP lasts: the chemistry explained

LFP’s long life comes from the stability of its cathode material. Where other chemistries lose capacity as their crystal structure degrades, LFP resists that decay, which is why it can be charged and discharged thousands of times while retaining most of its capacity. The TZ5E pack is warranted to retain 70% state of health across 4,500 cycles — in practical Lima, Callao and Arequipa duty, that is well beyond the first life of the vehicle.

Managing depth of discharge and temperature

Pack life in the real world depends on how it is used. Two rules dominate: keep daily state of charge between 20% and 80% rather than routinely hitting the extremes, and keep the pack within its thermal comfort zone. The TZ5E manages temperature with liquid cooling, but operators in hot Peru climates should shade parked trucks and pre-condition while plugged in. Following these habits protects the warranty and the pack’s second-life value.

Fast charging: useful but not free

DC fast charging is convenient and the TZ5E supports it, but frequent fast charging at high temperatures accelerates degradation. The right strategy is to make depot charging the backbone and reserve fast charging for genuinely long days. For Peruvian fleets, we recommend a routine of slow overnight charging with fast charge used as an exception, which maximises pack life while still giving the flexibility to cover longer routes.

Reading a battery health report

Every TZ5E produces a state-of-health figure through its battery-management system, and understanding it is straightforward. A new pack sits near 100% SOH; the warranty threshold is 70%. A gentle, well-managed pack may lose only a few percent a year, while an abused one can fall much faster. For Lima, Callao and Arequipa fleets, downloading the BMS log monthly makes degradation visible early, when it can still be managed rather than discovered.

The second life and end-of-life of a pack

When a traction pack eventually falls below the warranty threshold, it is not scrap. A retired 350 kWh pack from a TZ5E retains most of its capacity for gentler duty, making it ideal for stationary storage or depot solar buffering. This second life, before responsible recycling, is what makes the whole lifecycle narrative of an electric truck genuinely circular, and it preserves value for the operator at the end of the vehicle’s first service life.

Why Peru is ready for electric trucks now

The conditions that make an electric truck viable are all present in Peru. cheap hydro power in the south and growing mining electrification. Freight demand concentrates in and around Lima, Callao and Arequipa, where routes are short and predictable and the depot is never far away. Fuel logistics add cost and delay that a fleet charging its own vehicles simply avoids. For a TZ5E electric dump truck on a mining supply and cold-chain agro-export duty cycle, this is not an experimental technology but a practical replacement for diesel, and the operators moving first are the ones who lock in the lowest cost per kilometre before their competitors do.

A step-by-step implementation plan

Electrifying a fleet in Peru is a project, and running it in phases is what keeps it manageable. Phase one is a site and route audit: list every duty cycle, measure real daily distance and load, and map the depot power supply. Phase two is a pilot of two to five TZ5E units on the most predictable routes, with chargers installed and drivers trained. Phase three is measurement — energy cost per kilometre, uptime, maintenance hours. Phase four is scaling what the data supports. Each phase de-risks the next and keeps the capital commitment matched to proven performance rather than optimism.

Choosing the right configuration

Within the TZ5E range there are choices that matter more than cosmetics. The 6x4 layout suits the axle load and traction pattern of your duty cycle. Battery capacity of 350 kWh should be sized to the longest realistic daily route plus a safety margin, not to the biggest number on the brochure. Body and equipment specification — tipper, box, reefer, tank or compactor — should be matched precisely to the job in Peru. Getting these four decisions right at order stage is far cheaper than modifying a truck after it lands.
