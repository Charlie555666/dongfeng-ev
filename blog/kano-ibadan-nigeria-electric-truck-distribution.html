Nigeria Beyond Lagos: KT5J and KT5M Electric Truck Strategy for Kano and Ibadan Distribution

Most conversations about electric trucks in Nigeria start and end in Lagos. That is a mistake. Nigeria's two most promising second-city markets — Kano and Ibadan — offer distribution duty cycles that are in some ways better suited to battery-electric drivetrains than Lagos's congested, infrastructure-strapped corridors. Kano is the commercial hub of the entire Sahel, distributing consumer goods across northern Nigeria and into Niger; Ibadan is the load centre for Nigeria's southwest, sitting between Lagos's ports and the biggest consumer belt in West Africa outside Lagos itself.

In both cities, the workhorse vehicle is the same: a light-to-medium rigid running fixed daily routes from a warehouse or wholesale hub to retail clusters. That is precisely the mission of the KT5J electric delivery truck and the larger KT5M electric box truck. Nigeria-specific import, duty and financing context is covered in our Nigeria market guide — this piece focuses on the city-level operating strategy.

Kano: Sahel Distribution Capital

Kano's economy runs on trade. The Sabon Gari and Dawanau markets move staggering volumes of consumer goods, and the city's distributor fleets run a distinctive pattern: dense morning sorties from warehouse to market cluster inside the city, then longer secondary runs to Katsina, Kaduna and the northern state capitals. The duty profile is 120–250 km per day with long stationary periods at loading bays and market drops.

Why this favours electric:

  • Idle-free economics. A diesel truck queuing at Dawanau's gates burns fuel for two hours a day doing zero productive work. A KT5J in the same queue consumes almost nothing.
  • Flat terrain. Kano sits on the flat Kano Plain — no gradients punishing the loaded vehicle, so real-world consumption stays at 0.85–1.0 kWh/km for the KT5J class.
  • Predictable routes. Fixed distributor routes mean charging needs are known exactly, not estimated.

The KT5J, on CATL 262 kWh LFP with an 18 t GVW chassis, delivers a rated 300 km-plus range — a full Kano distribution day with margin. Ten trucks charging overnight at a 120 kW depot point each need nothing more exotic than a commercial grid connection and a disciplined parking plan.

Ibadan: The Southwest Load Centre

Ibadan's strategic value is its position. Goods that clear at Apapa and Tin Can ports move to Ibadan for break-bulk and onward distribution to Oyo, Ogun, Osun, Ondo and Ekiti states — a consumer catchment of well over 30 million people. The city's own congestion is materially lighter than Lagos's, and the Lagos–Ibadan expressway, now substantially rehabilitated, has made the 130 km trunk leg reliable for the first time in a decade.

The Ibadan pattern differs from Kano: fewer, longer legs. A typical distributor day is one trunk run (Ibadan to Lagos or a southwestern state capital) plus a city cluster run. For this we specify the KT5M — CATL 310 kWh in long-range trim, 18 t GVW, and a box body of up to 45 m³. Rated range of about 300 km covers the trunk and the city leg with a single overnight charge, and a 240 kW DC charger at the Ibadan depot enables a midday splash for double-shift units.

The Power Question, Answered Honestly

No article about electric fleets in Nigeria can dodge the grid. Distribution companies supply Kano and Ibadan industrial estates with variable reliability, and any fleet plan must account for outages. The honest answer is that the diesel alternative faces the same problem — every Nigerian depot already runs a generator, and diesel gensets are the most expensive electricity in the country at USD 0.35–0.45 per kWh in fuel alone. Compare the options:

Energy sourceEffective cost/kWhRole in a Kano/Ibadan depot
Grid (Band A industrial)USD 0.08–0.12Primary — overnight base charging
Diesel gensetUSD 0.35–0.45Outage bridge — sized to one charger, not the fleet
Rooftop solar + small BESSUSD 0.06–0.09 amortisedDaytime shave + outage ride-through

The design principle: never size the generator for the whole fleet. Charge the fleet overnight on grid, keep a 100–150 kVA genset able to feed one charger during daytime outages, and add solar where roof space allows. Even in a bad month where a third of charging energy comes from the genset, the blended cost per kilometre still undercuts diesel distribution trucks by 45–55% at Nigerian pump prices.

Per-Kilometre Economics: Kano Case

Modelling a 12-truck KT5J fleet on Kano distributor duty — 180 km/day, 26 days/month, 40,000 km/year blended energy cost of USD 0.13/kWh:

  • Diesel baseline: USD 0.34/km fuel + USD 0.11/km maintenance = USD 0.45/km.
  • KT5J electric: USD 0.10/km energy + USD 0.05/km maintenance = USD 0.15/km.
  • Annual saving per truck: about USD 12,000 on 40,000 km — the USD 25,000–30,000 CAPEX premium repays in 26–30 months.
  • Fleet level: twelve trucks save roughly USD 144,000 per year versus diesel.

These figures deliberately ignore carbon credits, which Kano and Ibadan distribution fleets can pursue through verified displacement methodologies — treating any issuance as upside rather than baseline.

Receiving Trucks: Apapa, Tin Can or Onne

Northern and southwestern fleets typically receive through Lagos's Apapa and Tin Can ports or, increasingly, Onne in the east for clients with Niger Delta catchments. Nigeria applies the ECOWAS CET; electric trucks currently enter at a reduced duty band versus diesel equivalents, and the naira-denominated duty assessment rewards the lower CIF value of a KT5J-class vehicle versus a heavy tractor. We handle SONCAP conformity certification in China before shipment, ship RHD as standard, and for Kano-bound consignments we can arrange through-transport or handover at the Lagos yard — the 1,000 km Lagos–Kano run is done on a flatbed, not under battery power.

Sequence the Deployment

Our recommended sequence for a Nigeria second-city deployment: start with eight to ten KT5J units on pure city distribution in Kano or Ibadan where routes are fixed; add the KT5M box trucks for trunk legs once driver teams and depot charging are proven; and only then consider the Lagos corridor. The second cities are where Nigerian electric distribution economics are cleanest — and they are where your competitors are not yet looking.
