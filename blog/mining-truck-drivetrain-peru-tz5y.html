How Electric Truck Drivetrains Differ from Diesel

A diesel truck needs ten to sixteen gears because its engine produces usable torque only in a narrow band. An electric motor produces maximum torque from zero rpm and holds a broad, flat torque curve to highway speed - which is why most electric trucks, including the TZ5Y electric 80t mining truck, drive through a single-speed reduction gear with no clutch and no shifting. The drivetrain shrinks from hundreds of moving parts to a handful: motor, reduction gear, drive shafts. What the driver notices is seamless, uninterrupted acceleration. What the workshop notices is the disappearance of the transmission service line - no clutch replacements, no gearbox overhauls, no shift-system faults, historically among the costliest diesel truck repairs.

Single-Speed vs Multi-Speed: The Real Trade-Off

Single-speed drivetrains win on simplicity, efficiency and reliability for the majority of duty cycles. Multi-speed units - typically two or three ratios - earn their complexity where operating envelopes are extreme: sustained high-speed highway running combined with severe gradeability demands, or very heavy gross weights. A second ratio lets the motor stay in its efficiency sweet spot at both ends. The honest engineering summary: for distribution, port, municipal and regional work, single-speed is the right answer full stop. For long-haul mountain corridors or maximum-GCW applications, model both. The TZ5Y, as an electric 80t mining truck with a 510 kW motor, is specified for its intended duty - and we advise buyers route by route rather than by specification-sheet anxiety.

Gradeability and Motor Torque in Practice

Specification sheets quote maximum gradeability - typically 20-30% for heavy electric trucks - but fleet buyers should ask the better question: what grade can the truck climb continuously, at what speed, at what weight? The TZ5Y's 510 kW LvKong motor delivers full torque from standstill, which means loaded starts on grades where diesel trucks need run-ups or crawler gears. Sustained grade climbing is a thermal question, not a torque question - motor and inverter cooling sets the continuous limit, and liquid-cooled systems sustain high output far longer than air-cooled designs. On Peruvian routes with significant elevation, the regenerative descent matters as much as the climb: energy recovered downhill measurably extends daily range.

Efficiency Maps: Why Gearing Choice Costs Energy

Electric motors have efficiency maps - zones of rpm and torque where conversion efficiency peaks above 95% and edges where it falls toward 85%. Single-speed drivetrains spend more time at the map's edges during extreme low-speed heavy work or high-speed cruising; multi-speed units keep the motor centered. The real-world difference on typical 80t mining truck duty is a few percent of energy consumption - meaningful but second-order compared with driving style, speed discipline and payload. This is why the transmission decision should follow route analysis: flat urban and regional work gains nothing from extra ratios; mixed highway-and-grade work may gain a few percent; severe mountain mining duty gains enough to matter. Maintenance: What Disappears from the Schedule

The transmission change rewrites the maintenance book. Deleted from the service schedule: clutch wear measurement and replacement, gearbox oil services, synchronizer and shift-fork wear, transmission cooler service, and the driveline vibration analysis that chases gear wear. What remains: reduction-gear oil changes at extended intervals, drive-shaft joint inspection, and software updates that occasionally refine motor control maps. Workshop hours on driveline work typically fall 70-80% versus diesel equivalents. For Peruvian fleets, the reliability effect compounds: transmission failures strand trucks for days awaiting parts; the TZ5Y's simplified drivetrain removes the category almost entirely, and the parts that remain are stocked in our recommended fleet starter kits.
