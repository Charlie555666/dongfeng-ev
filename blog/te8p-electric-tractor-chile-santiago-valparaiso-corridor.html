TE8P Electric Tractor on Chile's Santiago-Valparaiso Corridor

The electric truck case for Chile's primary export corridor is built on a short, repeatable loop that exposes every weakness of diesel fuel. The EV truck running loaded containers between Santiago and Valparaiso completes roughly 115 km each way, returns empty or lightly loaded, and repeats up to four times per day, which is exactly the duty cycle that rewards electrification. For a fleet operator this means predictable distance, a fixed depot at each end, and a return trip that lets regenerative braking recover meaningful energy on the descent toward the coast.

Corridor Profile and Duty Cycle

The route climbs from the Valparaiso port at sea level to the Santiago metropolitan distribution zone at about 500 m elevation, crossing the Cuesta La Dormida and nearby grades that reach 6 to 8 percent on the approach roads. A 6x4 prime mover in this service typically hauls a 40 ft container with gross combination weight near 38 to 42 tonnes. Diesel tractors in Chile burn roughly 0.45 to 0.55 L/km under this loaded profile, which at current diesel prices of $0.90 to $1.30 per liter translates to $0.40 to $0.72 per kilometer in fuel alone before any maintenance is counted.

An electric tractor inverts that math in a way that compounds across a fleet. The TE8P heavy electric tractor draws about 1.1 to 1.6 kWh/km when loaded on this corridor. At Chilean commercial electricity rates of $0.08 to $0.20 per kWh, the energy cost drops to roughly $0.09 to $0.32 per kilometer. Even at the high end of grid pricing the EV truck beats diesel on energy by a wide margin, and most depot charging contracts in Chile sit well below the retail ceiling because they are negotiated on industrial time-of-use tariffs.

The repeatability of the loop matters more than the absolute distance. Because the truck returns to the same two depots every day, an operator can install fixed charging at both ends and never rely on public infrastructure. The empty or partially loaded return leg also means the second half of each trip is cheaper than the first, improving real-world efficiency versus the quoted loaded figure.

Range and Charging Plan

Loaded range on the TE8P sits between 200 and 350 km depending on grade, payload, and climate load. For the 115 km Santiago-Valparaiso leg this leaves a comfortable margin for a round trip plus terminal queuing without a mid-route charge. Most operators run a simple depot model that keeps the truck moving during normal shift windows.

Charge overnight at the Santiago distribution yard using off-peak electricity near $0.08 to $0.12 per kWh, then top up at the Valparaiso terminal during loading and unloading windows. A 360 kW DC charger moves the pack from 10 to 80 percent in about 45 to 90 minutes, which fits naturally inside a container handling slot. Where a fleet can install MCS hardware, a 1 MW session refills the 600 kWh pack in well under an hour, effectively matching the turnaround of a diesel refuel across a busy shift and removing charging as a bottleneck.

Battery swap is not required on this corridor because the duty cycle allows depot charging, but the 5 to 6 minute swap option remains available for fleets that want to eliminate charging downtime entirely during peak export season. The swap pack shares the same CATL LFP chemistry and 8-year warranty as the fixed pack.

Why the TE8P Fits Chile's Grid and Ports

Chile's grid already runs on a high share of renewables, which means the TE8P electric tractor delivers some of the lowest well-to-wheel emissions of any market in Latin America. Port operators in Valparaiso face tightening emissions rules for yard and drayage equipment, and a zero-tailpipe prime mover removes the diesel particulate and NOx problem entirely from the terminal gate. The 510 kW motor also holds full power at altitude; while a diesel unit loses 30 to 35 percent of its output at 4,000 m, the electric drivetrain loses none, an advantage that matters for any fleet extending operations into the Andean interior or up to mining supply routes.

Operator Deployment Checklist

Fleets that have moved first on this corridor followed a consistent sequence, and we recommend the same order to keep risk low:

  • Audit your current diesel per-kilometer cost including idle and queuing fuel, not just road fuel.
  • Size the depot charger at 360 kW minimum, or 1 MW if you run more than six tractors on the loop.
  • Negotiate a time-of-use industrial tariff with the Santiago and Valparaiso sites before delivery.
  • Train drivers on regen braking and the single-speed drivetrain to recover the 8 to 15 percent descent energy.
  • Start with two TE8P units against two diesel tractors and measure real TCO for 90 days.
  • Scale the order once the measured saving matches the model within 10 percent.
