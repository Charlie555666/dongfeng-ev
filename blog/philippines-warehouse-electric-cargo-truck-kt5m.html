The Warehouse Shuttle Duty Cycle

Warehouse shuttle work - distribution center to satellite warehouse, DC to store, cross-dock transfers - is one of the most electric-friendly duty cycles in road transport. Distances are short and known, typically 30-150 km per round trip; vehicles return to a fixed base daily; schedules repeat week after week. This predictability removes the two classic objections to electric trucks: range uncertainty and charging access. The KT5M, with 280 km of certified range from a 140 kWh CATL LFP pack, covers multiple shuttle cycles per day with margin to spare, then charges overnight at the depot it already returns to. For Philippine logistics operators, shuttle routes are the obvious first electrification target.

Why Electric Trucks Fit DC-to-Store Work

Beyond range fit, shuttle duty exploits every electric strength. Stop-start urban running between warehouses is where diesel engines waste the most fuel and where regenerative braking recovers the most energy - the 160 kW motor on the KT5M electric box truck harvests braking energy at every traffic light. Noise matters too: early-morning and night deliveries to urban stores face tightening restrictions across Manila, and a near-silent electric truck turns restricted hours into usable delivery windows. Payload is preserved because shuttle loads are typically volumetric rather than weight-limited. And the cost per kilometer drops by 60-75% on energy alone, which compounds across the dozens of weekly cycles a shuttle fleet accumulates.

Charging Strategy for Shuttle Fleets

Shuttle fleets rarely need public charging at all. The baseline strategy is overnight depot charging at the lowest tariff, sized so every truck starts the day at full charge. Where daily distance approaches the 280 km range limit, a midday opportunity charge of 30-60 minutes on a DC fast charger bridges the gap without changing the route plan. Charger count follows shift patterns, not truck count: a single-shift fleet of ten trucks typically needs two to three DC chargers plus overnight points, while a two-shift operation staggers charging across the changeover. In the Philippines, depot power upgrades should be confirmed with the utility early - transformer lead times, not charger prices, are the usual schedule risk.

Route and Shift Planning in Manila

Shuttle electrification succeeds or fails in route planning, and the data needed already exists in any fleet's dispatch records. Map each shuttle lane by distance, payload, traffic window and return time; rank them by total daily kilometers; electrify the longest, most regular lanes first because fuel savings scale with utilization. In Manila, congestion patterns matter: electric trucks lose almost nothing idling in traffic, which flips the usual diesel logic - the worst-congested routes often deliver the biggest savings. Driver shifts should align charging breaks with natural pauses - loading, paperwork, meal windows - so charging time never sits on the critical path. Telematics on the KT5M reports actual energy per lane, letting dispatchers refine assignments monthly.

Charging Planning for KT5M Fleets

Charging infrastructure decisions made at order time shape fleet economics for a decade. The KT5M supports DC fast charging through a CCS2 inlet, recovering 20-80% of its 140 kWh pack in roughly an hour on a 120 kW unit, while overnight AC or lower-power DC charging covers base load at the cheapest tariff. A sound depot plan matches charger count to shift patterns rather than truck count - fleets running single shifts rarely need more than one fast charger per four to six trucks plus overnight points per bay. Fleets in the Philippines should also model tariff structures: time-of-use pricing rewards overnight charging discipline, and the savings fund the infrastructure faster than most buyers expect.
