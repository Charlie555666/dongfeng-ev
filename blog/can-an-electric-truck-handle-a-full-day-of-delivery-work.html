What Decides an Electric Truck's Daily Distance?

Four factors set the real daily figure, and only one of them is the battery. Certified range: the KT5J electric delivery truck carries 220 km on a full charge of its 100 kWh pack - the single-charge ceiling. Charging opportunity: the hours the duty cycle offers for top-ups - a truck with a 45-minute midday break on a 120 kW charger adds 100-130 km to its day. Duty intensity: consumption runs 1.0-1.6 kWh per kilometer depending on load, terrain and traffic, moving the daily total proportionally. And shift structure: the binding constraint is often hours, not energy - a single-shift truck cannot use more range than its working window allows regardless of pack size. Multiply the factors honestly and heavy electric trucks on structured duty cover 300-500 km per day comfortably, with multi-shift operations reaching 600 km and beyond - the figures this guide breaks down.

How Far on a Single Charge in Real Operations?

The single-charge day is the baseline, and the certified range survives contact with real duty better than skeptics expect. The KT5J's 220 km rating is a homologation figure; real Vietnamese duty with full loads, air conditioning and Ho Chi Minh City traffic typically returns 80-90% of it - 180-315 km depending on model and conditions. Urban and regional distribution, port shuttles, municipal rounds, site supply - the large majority of commercial truck duty worldwide runs under 250 km daily, which is why a single overnight charge covers most fleets' operations outright, with reserve held back for the day that goes wrong. The planning discipline: meter the first month's actual consumption per route from the truck's telematics, and set reserves from your own data rather than the brochure. Fleets report the anxiety about range typically survives about three weeks of operation - then the dashboard replaces it.

How Do Multi-Shift Operations Reach 500-600 km?

High-utilization electric trucking is an operational design, and the pattern is proven. Two-shift trunking: truck runs a 200-280 km first shift, charges during the driver change - 40-60 minutes on a 120-240 kW DC charger restores 60-80% of the pack - and runs the second shift on the same legs, totaling 450-550 km per day. Hub-and-spoke relay: routes structured so trucks return through the depot or a charging point mid-duty, adding a 30-45 minute top-up to the schedule the way diesel fleets schedule fuel. Battery-swap variants: where swap infrastructure exists, a five-to-six-minute exchange resets range entirely, and daily distance becomes a shift-hours question only. The KT5J at an FOB range of $48,000-$65,000 supports both fast-charge patterns; the constraint on daily distance in practice is driver hours regulation, not the battery - which is the same constraint diesel fleets manage.

Does Heavy Duty or Hot Climate Shrink the Daily Figure?

Yes, measurably, and honest planning uses the shrunken numbers. Full gross-weight duty raises consumption to the 1.4-1.8 kWh per kilometer band; mountain terrain adds more on the climb, though regenerative braking returns a meaningful share on the descent; and Vietnamese heat at 40 degrees and above costs the pack thermal-management energy plus air-conditioning load - typically 5-12% of range combined. Stacked honestly, a heavy-duty day in a hot climate plans on 70-80% of certified range: still 150-260 km per charge across the model range, still covering the duty these trucks are specified for, because mining loops, port work and urban vocational duty are short-haul by nature. The fleets that struggle are the ones that specified against brochure numbers; the fleets that thrive specified against their own worst-day data, with the margin built in before the order.

How Should You Plan Daily Distance for Your Fleet?

The planning method is four steps, and it converts the question from anxiety into arithmetic. Map the duty: actual daily kilometers per route from existing fleet records, including the seasonal peak week, not the average week. Apply the real-world factor: 80-90% of certified range for the KT5J in Vietnamese conditions, less on mountain or extreme-heat routes. Design the charging windows around the schedule's natural breaks - loading, meal breaks, shift changes - before assuming any dedicated charging downtime. And hold reserve: 15-20% state of charge at day end as policy. Run that arithmetic and most delivery truck duty fits single-charge operation; what does not fit gets an opportunity-charging slot designed in from day one. Frequently Asked Questions

How many kilometers can an electric truck drive per day?

300-500 km on typical commercial duty: certified single-charge range of 200-350 km plus one 40-60 minute DC fast-charge during shift breaks. Multi-shift operations with planned charging reach 600 km, where driver-hour rules - not the battery - set the limit.

How far does an electric truck go on one charge in real use?

Expect 80-90% of the certified figure: a 600 kWh heavy truck rated at 330-350 km delivers 260-315 km fully loaded in hot-weather operation; smaller 262-400 kWh models deliver 200-300 km - enough for most urban and regional routes outright.

Does hot weather reduce electric truck daily range?

Yes, by 5-12%: battery thermal management plus air conditioning at 40C costs measurable energy. Plan on 70-80% of certified range for heavy-duty hot-climate work and the daily figure stays reliable - a 350 km truck plans as a 250-280 km truck.

How long does the midday charge take on a 500 km schedule?

40-60 minutes on a 120-240 kW DC charger restores 60-80% of a 400-600 kWh pack - timed to the driver change or meal break it costs zero working time. Battery-swap variants exchange packs in 5-6 minutes where swap stations exist.

Is 200 km of range enough for commercial truck work?

For the majority of vocational duty, yes: urban delivery, port shuttles, municipal rounds and construction supply typically run 80-200 km daily. Meter your own routes first - most fleets discover their longest regular day fits a single charge with 15-20% reserve.
