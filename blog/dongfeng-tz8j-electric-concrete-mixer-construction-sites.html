The Mixer Duty Cycle, Quantified

A city mixer's day is a repeated loop: load at the batching plant, haul 15–40 km to site, queue at the pump, discharge, return. What the schedule conceals is how much of a diesel mixer's fuel is burned standing still:

  • Drum rotation at idle: the diesel engine runs the drum hydraulics at the plant, in the site queue, and during the pour — 30–50% of engine hours in a mixer's life are stationary drum-turning.
  • Plant and site dwell: 20–40 minutes per cycle waiting at the loading bay or for the pump to be positioned.
  • Short loaded legs: the haul itself is 15–40 km each way — comfortably inside electric range with the drum running.

The TZ8J replaces all of it with one battery. The electric drum drive draws a fraction of what a diesel PTO system wastes through hydraulic conversion, and every idle minute costs nothing. In measured deployments, electric mixers cut energy cost per delivered cubic metre by 50–65% against diesel.

Economics per Cubic Metre

Take a typical plant serving metro construction: 12 loads per day, 6 m³ average load, 50 km round trip including plant and site dwell — most mixer operations run 250–350 km/day with drum time included. Diesel mixer consumption in this duty: 40–45 L/100 km effective plus idle — call it 130 L/day, USD 120–140. The TZ8J covering the same day, drum included, draws about 230–260 kWh: USD 20–29 at USD 0.09–0.11/kWh. Daily saving: USD 95–115, roughly USD 29,000–35,000 per year at 300 operating days. Maintenance on the hydraulic-diesel PTO chain (a chronic mixer cost centre) largely disappears with the electric drum drive. On a USD 120,000-class FOB price, the mixer premium over diesel pays back in 3–4 years on energy alone.

Silence as a Pour Licence

Urban construction is under pressure to extend working hours — but noise rules written for diesel machinery cap how early pours can begin and how late they can run. An electric mixer is dramatically quieter: no engine at the site, no hydraulic whine at the plant, drum drive hum only. Readymix operators report the practical consequence is earlier pour windows in residential and hospital zones, which is scheduling capacity the competition literally cannot buy. Several city authorities now explicitly favour low-noise, zero-emission equipment in night-work permits.
