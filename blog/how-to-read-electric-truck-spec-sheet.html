How to Read an EV Truck Spec Sheet: The Twelve Numbers That Actually Predict Fleet Performance

An electric truck spec sheet can carry forty lines of data, and most buyers negotiate over two of them — price and range — while the numbers that actually decide fleet economics sit unread in the middle. Diesel spec literacy took fleet managers decades to build; electric spec literacy can be compressed into twelve numbers, because a battery-electric drivetrain is a simpler machine with cleaner cause-and-effect. This is the reading guide our engineering team wishes every procurement officer arrived with: what each number means, which ones predict what, and the specific traps we see tender comparisons fall into. Use it against our own model pages — the TE8L electric tractor, KTA1 electric dump truck or the cargo line — or against any competitor's sheet.

Group One: The Range Numbers (Read Together, Never Alone)

  • 1. Nominal range (km) — the headline figure, measured under a fixed test cycle (usually China's GB or a UN-equivalent at partial load). Treat it as an upper bound under mild conditions, not a planning number.
  • 2. Usable pack capacity (kWh) — nominal capacity times the usable window the BMS allows (typically 90-95%). This is the real energy tank. A "600 kWh" pack with a 90% window holds 540 usable kWh.
  • 3. Consumption at your load (kWh/km) — the number that converts capacity into range, and the one spec sheets advertise most misleadingly. Demand the consumption figure at your GCW and terrain, not the flat-road partial-load figure. Loaded quarry duty runs 1.5-1.9 kWh/km on an 8x4; the brochure's 1.2 is a different mission.

The planning arithmetic: usable capacity ÷ loaded consumption = honest range. 540 kWh ÷ 1.7 kWh/km = 318 km on the mission that matters. That single division, done per mission profile, is worth more than any test-cycle range figure on the sheet.

Group Two: The Duty Numbers (What the Chassis Can Actually Do)

  • 4. GCW / GVW rating (t) — gross combination or vehicle weight. On a tractor this is the whole story of revenue per trip: a 49 t GCW 4x2 and a 120 t GCW heavy-haul unit are different businesses.
  • 5. Peak vs continuous motor power (kW) — peak power (282-510 kW across the Dongfeng line) sells brochures; continuous power sustains gradients. A truck rated 410 kW peak but 240 kW continuous will slow on a 6% grade at GCW that its "410 kW" implied it could hold.
  • 6. Peak torque (Nm) and at what speed — electric motors deliver peak torque from ~0 rpm, which is the spec advantage diesel never matches: it is why an electric tipper pulls away from a standstill at full GCW without clutch drama. Compare torque curves, not just the peak figure.
  • 7. Gradeability at GCW (%) — the direct answer to "can it climb my route". A quarry truck that cannot hold your haul-road ramp at legal speed is a write-off of a purchase regardless of price.

Group Four: The Charging Numbers (The Hidden Infrastructure Cost)

  • 11. Max DC charge rate and charge curve (kW, 10-80% time) — the charge rate decides your depot capex class (120/240/360 kW hardware) and the 10-80% time decides your mid-shift opportunity windows. Ask for the curve, not the peak: a truck advertising "360 kW" that tapers to 180 kW above 60% charge has a different depot math than the headline implies.
  • 12. Charging standard & swap compatibility — GB/T, CCS or MCS-track hardware, and whether the pack is swap-capable (the option that changes both capex structure and utilisation ceiling later). A fleet that buys swap-ready trucks keeps an option open that non-swap trucks cannot retrofit cheaply.

Reading a Comparison Like an Engineer

When we sit in tender evaluations, the fastest way to expose a weak sheet is the two-line test: (1) divide usable capacity by the loaded consumption at the tender's stated mission — if the honest range is below the mission's daily distance plus a 20% reserve, the truck is mispriced for the job no matter its brochure; (2) check that the continuous power and gradeability numbers survive the route's worst kilometre — a single unclimbable ramp voids the truck's economics on that corridor. Then read the warranty group against the finance term: a five-year lease against an eight-year/4,500-cycle CATL warranty leaves residual value on the table; the same lease against an anonymous two-year pack warranty embeds an unpriced risk. Bringing the Twelve Numbers to Your Next Purchase

The buyer who arrives at a comparison with the twelve numbers structured — whether against our own line-up like the KT5J electric cargo truck or any competitor's sheet — controls the meeting. The compression we recommend:

  • Demand the mission-profiled numbers: consumption at your load and gradient, charge curve to 80%, continuous power at your worst ramp — the three requests that separate engineered sheets from marketing ones.
  • Run the honest-range division: usable capacity ÷ loaded consumption, per route — the single calculation that exposes most range claims.
  • Read the warranty against the finance term: years, cycles, SOH floor and the claim procedure — the three lines that determine whether the pack's value survives the loan.
  • Name the cell supplier: a CATL-branded LFP pack with serialised data is a bankable asset; an anonymous "lithium-ion" line is an unpriced risk — the second-life economics in our battery piece depend on exactly this documentation.
  • Check market-specific duty context: the same truck runs different numbers in Jakarta's humidity than in Almaty's winter — our country pages, such as the Indonesia electric truck market, carry the local adjustments that make the twelve numbers locally honest.
