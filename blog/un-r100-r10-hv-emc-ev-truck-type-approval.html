UN R100 and R10: High-Voltage Safety and EMC Type Approval for EV Truck Imports

Hidden inside every clean EV truck import are two unglamorous documents that decide whether the truck clears customs, gets registered, and holds its resale value: the UN Regulation R100 certificate (electric powertrain safety) and the R10 certificate (electromagnetic compatibility). Most buyers discover these regulations at the worst moment — when a broker asks for them and the seller cannot produce them. This guide explains what each regulation actually certifies, why customs authorities and registries from Vietnam to the GCC increasingly demand them, and how to verify the paperwork before you wire a deposit — illustrated with the case of a TZ8J electric mixer heading to a project in Vietnam.

UN R100: The Safety Spine of an Electric Powertrain

UN Regulation No. 100 — the international standard for electric vehicle safety, applied in some form by most markets that regulate vehicle imports beyond basic customs — exists because a 600 kWh battery pack is a serious industrial object whose protection requirements deserve engineering, not improvisation. Its core sections, translated into plain language:

  • Isolation resistance — the high-voltage system must maintain minimum electrical isolation between live parts and the chassis, so a single fault cannot energise the body a driver or firefighter touches.
  • Protection against direct and indirect contact — enclosures, connectors, and marking standards that keep hands, tools, and water away from live conductors, including IP ratings for the pack and its connectors.
  • Isolation monitoring — the vehicle must continuously detect insulation degradation and warn before it becomes danger.
  • Post-crash electrical safety — after a defined collision load, the HV system must not present shock, fire, or electrolyte hazards.
  • Recharge safety — charging connections, interlocks, and protections covering the vehicle side of every charge session.

When a truck carries a valid R100 family certification, it means an accredited technical service has physically tested a production-representative vehicle for each of those properties, and the manufacturer's factory holds a quality arrangement that keeps production conforming. For the buyer, R100 is why you can hand the keys to a technician with confidence — the isolation monitoring, the interlocked service disconnect, the crash protections were verified by someone other than the salesman.

UN R10: Why Your Truck Must Not Interfere (or Be Interfered With)

UN Regulation No. 10 covers electromagnetic compatibility: the truck's inverters, motors, BMS, and DC-DC converters switch hundreds of kilowatts at frequencies that radiate; the same vehicle carries radios, telematics, and safety controllers that must keep working amid that noise — and near radio towers, radar installations, and other vehicles. R10 certification proves two directions: the truck's emissions stay within limits that protect other equipment, and its own systems demonstrate immunity to the electromagnetic environments it will encounter. In practical fleet terms, R10 is the difference between a mixer truck that passes an airport-perimeter radio environment without its BMS throwing phantom faults, and one that does not. Ports, mining sites with heavy radio traffic, and installations near defence or aviation facilities are where non-compliant vehicles become undeployable assets.

The distinction that trips buyers most: the approval certificates cover the type (the design family), while the COC covers your individual vehicle. A seller waving a type approval without issuing a COC per truck is like a title deed without a transfer — technically interesting, commercially useless.

How to Verify Before Paying

Three checks, all cheap, all before the deposit. First, match the approval's type designation to the exact model and battery configuration you are buying — approvals are configuration-specific, and a certificate for the 350 kWh variant does not cover the 423 kWh one. Second, check validity and issuing authority on the approval number with your destination regulator or an inspection company; the certificates follow a standard numbering format any conformity agent can decode in minutes. Third, name the documents in the purchase contract with delivery dates, making final payment contingent on the complete document pack — the single most effective clause in vehicle export contracts, and one that costs nothing to add. In our own export practice, Fenghan supplies the full pack (approvals, COC, datasheets, VIN records, inspection certificates) as a condition of shipment, which is exactly the standard any buyer should hold their supplier to.

Why This Also Protects Resale and Insurance

Approvals matter after the truck arrives, too. Insurers ask for them because an uncertified HV system is an unquantified fire and liability risk, and premiums (or outright refusals) follow. Second-hand buyers ask because a truck that cannot be re-registered in a neighbouring market is worth a fraction of one that can. And financing banks ask because their collateral is the truck's book value, which rests on its ability to stay legal. A few hours of document diligence at purchase protects the entire ownership chain that follows — which is why we publish our platforms' certification status openly and encourage buyers to have it verified by their own conformity agents. The boring documents, it turns out, are the load-bearing ones.

The Contract Clause That Protects Buyers

Everything in this article reduces, commercially, to one paragraph of contract language. The clause that has saved our customers the most money and time is unglamorous: "Final payment is contingent on delivery of the complete documentation pack — type approval certificates matching the contracted configuration, Certificate of Conformity per vehicle, technical datasheets in the destination-market language, and inspection certificates — no later than five days before vessel departure." That single sentence reorders the entire transaction, because it moves document delivery ahead of the supplier's cash, and every conformity problem becomes the exporter's schedule pressure rather than the buyer's demurrage. Sellers with their paperwork in order accept it without discussion; the ones who argue are providing information of a different kind.

The clause works best paired with two practical habits. First, the document review happens when the certificates arrive — weeks before shipping — not at the destination port when every day of delay is charged in storage. A conformity agent in the destination market can verify approval numbers, validity dates, and configuration match within days for a modest fee, and that fee has repaid itself in every transaction where it caught something. Second, keep the approval-to-configuration match discipline alive for repeat orders: manufacturers update battery sizes, motor variants, and software versions continuously, and an approval family that covered last year's 350 kWh variant may not cover this year's 423 kWh configuration. The repeat-order trap is the most common one we see in the market — buyers who verified diligently on unit one and assumed unit twenty was identical.

For fleet buyers without in-house conformity expertise, the working division of labour is simple: the exporter supplies and stands behind the certificates (as we do as a condition of every shipment), the destination agent verifies them independently, and the contract holds the timeline together. Three parties, one document pack, zero surprises at the port — which is, in the end, the entire art of importing electric trucks without drama.
