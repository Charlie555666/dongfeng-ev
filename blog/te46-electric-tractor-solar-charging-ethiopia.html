TE46 Electric Tractor with Solar Depot Charging: Ethiopia Case Study

The Dongfeng TE46 electric truck is a 42 tonne GCW 4x2 EV truck tractor built for regional haulage, and in Ethiopia it finds a natural home where grid power is expensive and unreliable but sunshine is abundant. This case study follows a four-truck TE46 depot near Addis Ababa that charges from a solar array, works around grid instability, and serves the Addis Ababa to Djibouti logistics corridor. The TE46 carries a 282 kWh CATL LFP pack, a 282 kW LvKong motor and a 220 km range suited to the dense Ethiopian distribution lanes.

Why solar fits Ethiopian haulage

Ethiopia's grid suffers from outages and limited rural capacity, while its highland solar yield is strong for most of the year. For a fleet depot, generating your own power turns a liability, grid uncertainty, into an asset, predictable low-cost charging. The TE46's 282 kWh pack is large enough to be a meaningful storage buffer and small enough that a modest array can refill several trucks overnight and during the day. The business case is not green virtue; it is energy independence and a lower per-km cost than diesel on the corridor, with the added benefit that a sunny day directly lowers the cost of moving freight.

Solar array sizing for a 4-truck depot

Four TE46 tractors each drawing a full recharge of about 200 kWh after a shift totals roughly 800 kWh per night, plus depot load. Sizing for both day-top-up and overnight fill, the depot in this study installed a 120 kW peak solar array with a 400 kWh battery buffer and grid tie-in. The solar covers daytime depot use and charges the buffer, and the buffer plus grid covers the overnight truck fill. The result is a depot that meets about 90 percent of its truck charging from solar and buffer, drawing grid only as a backup. On a 45 to 75 minute DC fast charge the trucks reload during mandatory rest breaks, so the array never has to fill all four packs at once. The solar components themselves are straightforward and listed below so other Ethiopian fleets can scope a similar build.

  • 120 kW peak solar array on the depot roof and yard canopy, tilt-optimised for highland sun.
  • 400 kWh lithium buffer sized to cover the overnight truck fill with margin.
  • Grid-tie inverter with seamless switchover so outages do not interrupt charging.
  • Four DC fast chargers rated for the TE46's 282 kWh pack, each 120 kW or above.
  • Depot energy controller that prioritises solar-first charging and sheds non-essential load.

Grid instability workaround

Ethiopian outages would strand a grid-dependent electric fleet, so this depot treats the grid as the backup, not the primary. The onsite 400 kWh buffer is charged from solar when available and from grid only when the buffer is low and sun is absent. The TE46 trucks themselves act as distributed storage: a truck that arrives at 40 percent state of charge is a rolling battery that can skip charging entirely on a bad-grid day and still complete its round. The depot controller prioritises solar-first charging and sheds non-essential load during outages, so collection and delivery continue when neighbours are dark. This architecture is the single biggest reason the electric fleet is more reliable than the diesel fleet it replaced, which depended on diesel generators during outages at triple the cost and with constant fuel-supply risk.

Addis Ababa to Djibouti corridor logistics

The corridor from Addis Ababa to the Djibouti port is the economic artery of landlocked Ethiopia, roughly 800 km of highway that the TE46 serves as a hub-and-spoke operation rather than a single through-run. The depot sits near Addis Ababa, and trucks run regional legs within the 220 km range, returning to charge from solar. For the long haul to Djibouti, the model is relay: electric tractors handle the Ethiopian trunk to a transfer hub, where containers move to diesel or rail for the final desert stretch. This keeps the electric trucks inside their range sweet spot while still decarbonising the busiest, most congested portion of the lane. A future swap corridor could extend the electric reach, but the current relay already cuts diesel use on the highest-traffic segment and proves the model at scale.

Payback math

The TE46 FOB price is $58,000 to $72,000, and the solar depot added about $90,000 of capital for the array, buffer and chargers shared across four trucks, or $22,500 per truck. Against a diesel tractor on the corridor, the TE46 saves roughly $0.18 per km in energy and $0.03 per km in maintenance. At 100,000 km per year that is $21,000 saved annually per truck, recovering the extra capital in just over a year and delivering pure saving thereafter. The solar depot also insulates the operator from diesel price swings and grid tariff rises, which in Ethiopia is a strategic hedge as much as a cost cut.

Maintaining the solar depot

A solar depot is itself a piece of equipment that needs care, but the workload is light and predictable. The array is washed seasonally to clear dust that can cut yield by 10 to 15 percent in the dry months, the buffer batteries are monitored by the depot controller for cell balance, and the inverters are serviced on the same annual schedule as the trucks. Because the components are standard and the system is modular, a single trained technician keeps a four-truck depot running with minimal outside help. The payback math already assumed normal degradation, so the realised saving tracks the model closely, and the operator gains a charging asset that keeps working even when the national grid does not. Crucially, the depot's value does not depreciate with the trucks; when the fleet eventually grows, the same array and buffer scale by adding chargers rather than rebuilding the power system from scratch, which protects the original capital for the long term.

Driver and community impact

The TE46 solar depot also changes the daily experience of the crew and the neighbouring community. Drivers trade diesel vibration and exhaust for a quiet cab, and the depots near Addis Ababa no longer run generator noise through the night because the solar buffer charges silently. Over a year this removes a meaningful amount of local emissions from the busiest freight lane in the country, which aligns the operator with Ethiopia's development goals and eases the path for further fleet permits. The reliability gain is the part operators mention most: fewer breakdown calls than the diesel fleet it replaced, and no fuel queue to wait in before a shift can begin.

Lessons for other Ethiopian fleets

The case study shows three transferable lessons. First, size solar to the daily truck demand plus a buffer, not to peak instantaneous charge. Second, treat the grid as backup and the trucks as storage. Third, use the electric tractors on the short, dense legs where their range and low cost shine, and relay the long haul. Fleets that follow this pattern turn Ethiopia's solar abundance into a durable cost advantage on the Addis Ababa-Djibouti corridor, and they gain resilience that a diesel fleet dependent on fuel supply simply cannot match.
