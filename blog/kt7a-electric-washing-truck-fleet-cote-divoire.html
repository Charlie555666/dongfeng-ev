KT7A Electric Washing Truck Fleet Planning for Cote d'Ivoire Cities

The Dongfeng KT7A electric truck is a 16 tonne GVW EV truck road-washing unit built for clean, quiet urban street care, and Cote d'Ivoire's cities, led by Abidjan, are adopting it to keep pavements clear without the noise and fumes of diesel washers. Fleet planning for a washing truck is a different exercise from freight: it is about water, routes and shift timing as much as vehicles. This guide covers Abidjan street-washing routes, water tank capacity planning, shift scheduling and a municipal budget case for the KT7A.

Why an electric washing truck suits Abidjan

Abidjan's dense neighbourhoods and coastal humidity make street washing a daily public-health task, but a diesel washer at 6 a.m. is a noise and smoke nuisance in residential blocks. The KT7A's near-silent 200 kW LvKong drivetrain and its 200 km range let a crew start earlier and work closer to homes without complaint. The 200 kWh CATL LFP pack recharges overnight at the depot, so the truck is full for the morning round, and the low per-km energy cost suits the stop-start nature of washing work where a diesel would idle and burn fuel for nothing. For a city balancing cleanliness with liveability, the electric washer is a rare win on both fronts, and it also removes exhaust fumes from the very streets being cleaned.

Abidjan street-washing routes

A practical Abidjan deployment divides the city into timed loops rather than one long sweep. The KT7A works best on a 40 to 60 km loop per shift that returns the truck to the depot with charge to spare, leaving the 220 km ceiling as headroom for extended or double rounds. Typical loops cover the Plateau and Treichville commercial corridors in the early morning, the Cocody and Yopougon residential bands mid-morning, and the market zones after trade closes. Planning loops by district rather than by distance keeps the water and the charge both sufficient, and it lets the municipality publish a washing calendar so residents know when to expect the truck. A good route plan also avoids sending the washer into the same congestion it is meant to serve, which protects both range and water efficiency.

Water tank capacity planning

The KT7A is specified with a tank sized to its 16 tonne GVW and its washing duty, and the planning question is how many kilometres one fill covers. A washing truck typically uses 8 to 12 litres per metre of curb washed, so a full tank serves a predictable length of street before the depot refill. Municipalities should site the refill point on the loop rather than at a distant central depot, because hauling an empty tank across the city wastes both water-trip time and battery range. A loop-based refill also keeps the truck working instead of commuting, which raises the effective coverage per vehicle per day.

Shift scheduling

The quiet KT7A lets Cote d'Ivoire cities run washing earlier and later than a diesel would permit. A recommended schedule is a pre-dawn commercial-corridor pass to clear overnight dust before traffic, a late-morning residential pass while streets are active but quiet, and an evening market-zone pass after trade. The daily tasks a supervisor should confirm are listed below so nothing falls between shifts.

  • Pre-dawn pass on Plateau and Treichville before commuter traffic builds.
  • Mid-morning residential pass in Cocody and Yopougon at lower noise tolerance.
  • Evening market-zone pass after trade closes to clear the day's debris.
  • Overnight full charge of the 200 kWh pack so the morning starts at 100 percent.
  • Weekly nozzle and filter check to keep water pressure and coverage consistent.

Because charging is overnight and the 200 kWh pack covers a full day of stop-start washing, the truck needs no mid-shift charge for a standard single shift, and a second crew can take the same truck on a double shift with one fast top-up of 45 to 75 minutes. The 5 to 6 minute battery-swap option is useful where a depot wants zero downtime between shifts, letting one pack charge while the other works.

Planning your KT7A fleet

Start by mapping Abidjan into timed loops, sizing the refill point to the loop, and setting the shift calendar around the quiet electric operation. Buy chargers with the trucks, file the grid connection early, and pilot two units before scaling. The KT7A turns street washing from a noisy diesel chore into a clean, cheap, resident-friendly service.

Water sourcing and treatment

Street washing is only as clean as its water, and Abidjan fleets should plan the refill source as carefully as the routes. Municipal supply is the default, but a settlement tank that lets sediment drop out protects the KT7A's pumps and nozzles from grit that would otherwise shorten their life. Some cities add a mild, environmentally safe surfactant at the depot to improve dust capture without harming stormwater, which is a low-cost upgrade that raises the perceived quality of the wash. The 6,000 L tank means a single source fill serves a full morning loop, so the refill point can be a fixed, monitored connection rather than a roving water truck, which improves both cost and accountability for the municipality running the service.

Seasonal and event considerations

Abidjan's washing plan should flex with the calendar. The long dry season raises dust and increases wash frequency, so the depot should hold charger and water headroom for extra rounds, while the wet season can shift effort to drainage-clearing and market-zone hygiene after storms. Public events, holidays and sporting fixtures create one-off cleaning surges that the quiet KT7A handles overnight without disturbing residents. A municipality that builds this flexibility into the shift calendar gets consistent street quality year-round and avoids the complaint spikes that a rigid diesel schedule produces when noise forces crews into worse time slots that upset the public.
