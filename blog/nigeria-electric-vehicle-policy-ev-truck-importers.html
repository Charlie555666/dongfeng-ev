Nigeria's National EV Policy: What New Tariff Lines Mean for Electric Truck Importers

Nigeria's National Electric Vehicle Policy — adopted through the Federal Executive Council and operationalised through fiscal measures and the National Automotive Design and Development Council's framework — finally gives electric commercial vehicles something they never had in West Africa's largest market: their own tariff lines. Where an imported electric truck previously entered on the same commercial-vehicle schedule as its diesel rival (at duty and levy rates that could add 35-40% to landed cost), the EV policy introduces differentiated fiscal treatment for electric vehicles, cutting effective import costs and signalling a multi-year policy direction. For the operators who run Nigeria's freight — the distributors, the oil-services contractors, the cement majors' hauliers — the question is no longer whether the policy will move, but how to structure imports inside it. This article sets out the policy's practical mechanics for fleet buyers: the tariff treatment, the SONCAP pathway, the charging and power realities, and the strategic sequencing we recommend to Nigerian clients.

The Tariff Lines, in Practical Terms

The policy's core fiscal instrument is the creation of EV-specific import categories with reduced duty and levy treatment relative to combustion equivalents — implemented through the finance acts and customs tariff schedules, with NADAC/customs circulars phasing the operational detail. What this means in money: a diesel heavy truck entering Nigeria has historically attracted import duty in the 20% band plus the 15% levy on motor vehicles plus VAT; an electric truck entering on the EV lines pays reduced rates on the duty side and, depending on the current schedule phase, levy treatment aligned to the policy's zero-emission objective. On a USD 60,000 electric box truck, the difference between the old commercial-vehicle treatment and the EV lines is roughly USD 15,000-22,000 of landed cost — which is most of the electric premium over diesel eliminated by paperwork alone. The buyer's mechanics: the entry must invoke the EV tariff line with the correct HS classification, supported by documentation proving the vehicle is fully electric (our conformity file states this in the form Nigerian customs expects). Hybrids do not qualify for the full EV treatment — the policy deliberately tiers fully electric above hybrid — so buyers comparing configurations should compare landed, not FOB.

SONCAP and the Import Pathway

Nigeria's Standards Organisation operates the SONCAP conformity programme for regulated goods, and vehicles are squarely within it: certification must be obtained in the country of origin before shipment, with physical inspection and documentary review by the appointed conformity bodies. Our export package runs the China-side SONCAP process for Nigerian consignments as standard — the inspection, the battery documentation (UN 38.3 summaries), the technical file — and our Nigerian buyers clear at Apapa, Tin Can or Onne with the paperwork pre-aligned. The Strategic Sequencing for Nigerian Fleets

  • Import inside the current window at the new tariff lines — the policy's fiscal treatment is at its most favourable for early movers, and every schedule phase has been in the EV direction; there is no strategic case for waiting.
  • Anchor fleets on the high-diesel-exposure missions first — distribution and oil-services runs where fuel is 40%+ of operating cost and fuel-adulteration maintenance risk is highest; the electric fleet's advantages compound fastest there.
  • Structure charging behind the fence — compound charging with buffers sidesteps the public-network question entirely, which is how every serious Nigerian deployment currently runs.
  • Pair imports with technician training — our commissioning includes it; the policy will eventually favour operators with in-house HV capability, and the training is cheaper than the downtime.

The bottom line for Nigerian importers: the National EV Policy converts the electric truck from an exotic purchase into a structured import category with real fiscal advantage, and the operators who build their customs, conformity and charging playbook now will run the cheapest freight in the market by the time the policy's local-assembly phase matures. The diesel fleet's cost base is only going one direction; the policy just handed every fleet in Nigeria a tool to leave it.
