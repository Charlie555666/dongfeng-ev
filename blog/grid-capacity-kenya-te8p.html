The Depot's Power Connection Is the Real Infrastructure

Fleet electrification projects succeed or stall at the same unglamorous point: the electrical connection behind the depot fence. Chargers are products, bought and installed in weeks; grid capacity is a process, with utility applications, transformer lead times and civil works measured in months. The fleets that electrify smoothly are the ones that start the power conversation first - before the truck order, before the charger purchase. The arithmetic is encouraging: a working depot for ten to twenty trucks typically needs a connection in the hundreds of kVA to low single-digit MW, well within standard industrial supply in most Kenyan cities. The TE8P electric 6x4 heavy tractor and its fleetmates charge mostly overnight at managed rates, which keeps the required connection far smaller than the nameplate charger sum suggests. This guide walks the process in the order it actually happens.

Sizing the Connection: The Load Calculation Done Right

The load calculation starts from the fleet's energy appetite, not the chargers' nameplates. Work the example: a TE8P with a 528 kWh pack running 330 km of daily duty returns each evening needing 150-250 kWh depending on how much of the pack the day consumed. Ten such trucks need 1.5-2.5 MWh overnight - spread across an eight-hour managed charging window, that is 200-300 kW of sustained draw, not the megawatt a naive sum of ten 120 kW chargers would suggest. Managed charging software is the multiplier: staggering start times and throttling to finish-by-morning targets keeps the site peak low and the transformer modest. The demand figure that goes on the utility application should come from this modeling - fleets that apply for the nameplate sum pay for capacity they never use, in some markets for years.

The Utility Process: Applications, Studies and Lead Times

Utility connection processes in Kenyan markets follow a recognizable sequence, and knowing it sets realistic expectations. Application: load requirement, site details, intended use - the load calculation from proper modeling makes this credible and fast. Technical study: the utility assesses local network capacity; where reinforcement is needed, costs and timelines are quoted - this is the stage where lead times of three to twelve months appear, and why the process starts before the truck order. Connection agreement and construction: civil works, transformer installation, metering. Commissioning: energization and tariff activation. The practical accelerants: engage a local electrical contractor experienced with the utility, present the managed-charging load profile - utilities approve modest, well-managed loads faster - and where Nairobi has EV infrastructure programs, use them; several Kenyan utilities now offer streamlined EV fleet connection tracks.

Inside the Fence: Switchgear, Chargers and Layout

The depot's internal electrical design converts the connection into charging capacity, and layout decisions here are expensive to revisit. The architecture: main switchgear at the connection point, distribution to charging positions, and charger placement where trucks actually park - the overnight line, not a special charging bay that costs shunting time every day. Cable runs favor clustered layouts; canopy structures for Kenyan sun or rain protection combine naturally with the solar option. Sizing inside the fence should exceed day-one needs modestly - conduit and switchgear capacity for the fleet you plan at year three costs little at construction and much at retrofit. The TE8P's charging interface is the DC fast-charging standard in its class; charger procurement then becomes a commercial comparison of reputable suppliers rather than an engineering risk, and our team supplies the vehicle-side charging specifications to your electrical designer.

Resilience: Keeping the Fleet Charged When the Grid Blinks

Grid reliability is a fair question in many Kenyan markets, and the resilient depot design answers it in layers. First layer: the fleet's own buffer - trucks charged overnight sit ready through daytime outages, and a fleet charging to full carries a day or more of operational reserve in its batteries. Second layer: backup generation sized to the fleet's minimum daily energy need - not the full charging peak - keeps operations alive through extended outages at modest capital cost. Third layer: solar with storage, which in the Kenya climate turns the depot roof and canopy into a partial energy supply independent of grid conditions. The planning principle is matching resilience investment to the duty's criticality: a municipal fleet serving Nairobi mornings justifies more backup than a flexible commercial operation. We model outage scenarios with fleets at the design stage - the answer is usually cheaper than the anxiety.

Costing the Connection: What to Budget

Connection costs vary widely with site and utility, but the structure is predictable and the numbers are plannable. Budget lines: utility connection and any network reinforcement contribution - the most variable item, from modest to significant depending on local capacity; transformer and switchgear - standard industrial equipment with standard pricing; internal cabling and civil works - layout-dependent; chargers - the visible item, and usually not the largest; and design and project management. Across Kenyan fleet projects, total depot electrical infrastructure for a ten-to-twenty truck fleet typically runs from the low tens to low hundreds of thousands of dollars - against which the fleet's energy savings of $2,000-$5,000 per truck per month on hard duty provide the payback context. Deployment Timeline from Order to Operation

Realistic scheduling prevents the most common fleet electrification failures. A typical TE8P deployment runs five to seven months end to end: production takes 30-60 days depending on configuration and order size; ocean freight to the Port of Mombasa adds 25-45 days; customs clearance and homologation steps add two to six weeks depending on Kenyan procedures.
