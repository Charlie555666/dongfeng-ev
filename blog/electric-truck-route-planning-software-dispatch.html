Electric Truck Route Planning Software for Fleet Dispatch

An electric truck fleet lives or dies by its software. Unlike a diesel truck that refuels anywhere, an EV truck must be dispatched around charger locations, energy use and payload. Range-aware route planning software turns those constraints into on-time schedules. This guide explains how dispatch tools manage a Dongfeng EV fleet such as the TE8L electric tractor, and why fleets in the Jordan electric truck market adopt them from day one.

Why EV Truck Dispatch Differs from Diesel

A diesel dispatcher thinks about fuel cost and driver hours. An EV dispatcher also thinks about state of charge, charger availability and elevation. A TE8L electric tractor with a 400 kWh pack and 360 kW motor covers about 300 km per charge at 49t GCW, but that range falls with hills, headwinds and a full 31t load. Software that ignores these factors strands trucks; software that models them keeps the fleet moving. The core job of route planning is to never assign a trip the battery cannot finish, and to do that it must know the truck, the load and the road better than a diesel planner ever needed to.

Core Features of Range-Aware Routing

The best tools share a set of capabilities built around the electric truck's limits. The list below covers what a fleet manager should expect from a dispatch platform.

  • Live state of charge pulled from each truck's telematics every 30 seconds.
  • Route energy model using grade, payload, temperature and speed profile.
  • Charger map with real-time plug availability and 350 kW DC sites flagged.
  • Automatic charger booking so two trucks never claim one stall.
  • Driver app showing remaining range, next stop and suggested speed.

These features turn a 400 kWh pack into a predictable asset. The TE8L electric tractor reports cell temperatures so the tool can also warn if the 25 C to 35 C cooling loop is strained on a hot Jordan afternoon. Without live SOC, a dispatcher is guessing; with it, every assignment is backed by the actual energy left in the pack at the depot gate.

Energy Forecasting and Payload Modeling

Good software forecasts kWh per km before the trip, not after. For the TE8L electric tractor the baseline is about 1.3 kWh per km at 49t GCW on flat road, rising to 1.8 to 2.2 kWh per km in hill country. The table shows how payload and terrain move the number.

By feeding the model the actual load weight, the dispatcher sees the true range and can split a long haul into two legs with a 45 to 90 minute charge in the middle. The 8-year or 4,500-cycle CATL LFP pack tolerates this daily cycling without worry. A 300 km assignment on a flat route leaves 20 percent reserve; the same assignment loaded to 49t GCW over hills needs the mid-route charger or it will not make it back.

Charger Booking and Depot Scheduling

In the Jordan electric truck market, where charging sites are still building out, booking matters. The software reserves a stall when it plans the route, so the TE8L electric tractor arrives to a free 350 kW DC plug and charges 20 to 80 percent in 45 to 90 minutes. At the depot, overnight charging is scheduled to finish by the 05:00 dispatch window using off-peak electricity at $0.05 to $0.18 per kWh. The tool also balances the fleet so no single charger is overloaded on the grid connection. A 10-truck depot drawing 350 kW each would need a 3.5 MW supply, so the scheduler staggers starts to flatten the demand peak and avoid a costly grid upgrade.

Driver Adoption and Training

Software fails if drivers ignore it. Train drivers to trust the range readout, follow the suggested speed, and plug in when the tool says, not when the pack hits empty. A TE8L electric tractor driven at 80 km/h instead of 90 km/h gains 8 to 12 percent range, which the dispatcher can bank as reserve. Run a two-week pilot with one truck and one dispatcher before rolling the tool across the fleet, and reward drivers who finish shifts with the highest average state of charge remaining.
