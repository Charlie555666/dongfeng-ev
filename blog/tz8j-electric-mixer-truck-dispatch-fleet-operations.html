Ready-mix is a scheduling business. Concrete has a working life measured in minutes, a pour cannot wait, and a truck that arrives late or with a drum that has stopped turning has destroyed its own cargo. Electrification does not change those constraints — it changes the cost of the waiting, which in ready-mix is substantial. Dispatch Sequencing: Three Rules That Matter More With Electric

Standard ready-mix dispatch logic applies, but electrification adds two constraints and one opportunity:

  1. Sequence by state of charge as well as by time. The dispatch board should show SOC alongside drum status. A truck at 25% should not be sent on the longest run of the day.
  2. Prioritise plant-queue charging. Where chargers are installed at the plant, sequence the fleet so each truck spends at least one loading queue connected. This is the single highest-value operational change available to an electric mixer fleet.
  3. Protect the concrete clock first. Charging must never delay a pour. The rule is: charge when the truck is already waiting, never make the truck wait to charge.

Drum Discipline

Drum management is the operational discipline that separates professional mixer fleets from the rest, and it matters more when the drum runs on the traction pack:

  • Agitating speed, not fast speed, while waiting. Drum rotation at agitating speed draws 4–9 kW; high-speed charging or discharge draws 12–22 kW. Keeping the drum at agitating speed while queuing saves meaningful energy over a shift.
  • Observe the drum-stop policy. Concrete specifications permit a defined period of non-rotation. Use it: a truck waiting 40 minutes does not need the drum turning for all 40.
  • Discharge promptly on arrival. The 90-minute concrete clock is unforgiving, and delays cost the entire load — far more than any energy saving.
  • Track drum hours as a maintenance metric. Drum drive and hydraulic service intervals should be logged by drum-hours, not kilometres.

Washout: Environmental and Operational

Washout is where ready-mix operations most often fall foul of environmental regulation:

  • Never wash out on a public road or into a storm drain. Cement-laden wash water is high-pH and is a standard enforcement target.
  • Use a designated washout area with settlement, and recycle wash water where the plant permits it.
  • Wash out before the concrete sets in the drum — a hardened drum is a major repair, not a cleaning job.
  • Schedule washout before plugging in so the final charge of the day is not interrupted by a washout cycle.

Charging Schedule Integration

Three configurations, in order of preference:

  • Plant charging during queues plus overnight depot charging — the default and the most efficient. Covers high-cycle days.
  • Overnight depot charging only — viable where cycles are fewer and routes short. Confirm against measured shift energy before committing.
  • Battery swap for continuous 24-hour pour campaigns, at 5–6 minutes per exchange. See swap station deployment.

Whichever you choose, energise and load-test the chargers before the trucks arrive — the commissioning rule that prevents most first-month problems.

Common Mistakes in the First 90 Days

  1. Charging only overnight when the shift energy requires daytime top-ups — shows up as trucks unable to finish the day.
  2. Dispatching without SOC visibility — the board must show charge state, not just drum state.
  3. Leaving drums turning during long waits — a policy failure, not an engineering one.
  4. Washing out after plugging in — interrupts the charge and wastes the daily window.
  5. Not instrumenting the pilot — without kWh/m³ data, the second-order decision is guesswork.

Handling Delays and Rejected Loads

Every ready-mix operation eventually faces a load that cannot be placed: the pour stops, the site is not ready, or the concrete exceeds its working life. How the fleet handles that event determines whether it costs a load or a day.

  1. Define the return threshold. Set a clear time-at-site limit after which the truck returns to the plant rather than waiting. A returned load can often be re-tempered or redirected; a load that sets in the drum cannot.
  2. Keep a redirection option open. A load rejected at one site may be usable at another nearby pour within its working life. Dispatch should know what else is running.
  3. Never let the drum stop. The single absolute rule: while concrete is in the drum, the drum turns. Battery state of charge is irrelevant to this decision, which is why the 333 kWh pack and the plant-charging strategy exist.
  4. Log every event. Rejected loads are a dispatch-quality metric. A pattern of rejections at one site is a contract conversation, not a fleet problem.
