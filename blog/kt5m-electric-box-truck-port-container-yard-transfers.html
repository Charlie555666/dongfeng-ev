KT5M Electric Box Truck for Port Container Yard and Bonded Warehouse Transfers

Port logistics conversations usually start with tractors, but a large share of port-area freight movement is done by rigid box trucks: container freight station transfers, bonded warehouse moves, LCL consolidation, empty equipment repositioning and the general shuttle traffic between gate, yard and off-dock facilities. That work is short, repetitive, queue-heavy and emissions-constrained — which makes it one of the cleanest electrification targets in the entire port ecosystem. Daily distance lands at 90–180 km with a very high number of stops, starts and idling periods — the worst possible duty for diesel and close to the best possible duty for an electric drivetrain.

Why Ports Electrify Rigid Transfers Before Long Drayage

Three reasons the rigid transfer fleet usually moves first:

  • The duty is entirely within the port estate or its immediate periphery. Charging is installed once and covers the whole operation. There is no dependence on public charging corridors.
  • Queues are long and unavoidable. Gate processing, yard slotting and customs inspection produce idle time measured in hours per shift. Diesel burns through it; electric does not.
  • Port air quality programmes are binding. Many port authorities now publish emissions inventories and set reduction targets. Yard and transfer fleet emissions are among the most visible and most easily abated.

Energy Budget for Transfer Duty

Port transfer duty has an unusual energy profile: low distance, high stop frequency, substantial auxiliary draw from tail-lift or hydraulic equipment and, in tropical markets, continuous cab air-conditioning while queuing.

  • Traction: 0.75–1.00 kWh/km in dense yard duty with frequent acceleration from rest.
  • Idle and auxiliary: 1.5–4 kW continuous while queuing with air-conditioning running — 8–24 kWh over a shift of queuing.
  • Tail-lift and handling: 1.0–2.0 kWh per lift cycle where fitted.
  • Typical shift total: 110–210 kWh — comfortably inside the 262 kWh pack for single-shift operation, and inside the 310 kWh option where a second partial shift is required.

Gate and Queue Management: The Productivity Lever

Electrifying the fleet without fixing queue discipline delivers only part of the available benefit. The largest productivity gains come from operational changes:

  1. Pre-clearance and appointment systems cut gate dwell, which is the largest single time loss in port transfer work.
  2. Staggered dispatch prevents a fleet arriving at the gate simultaneously.
  3. Yard slotting close to the CFS reduces the longest internal leg.
  4. Telematics on queue time, not just distance — measure dwell separately, because it is the variable you can actually fix.

Charging Inside a Port Estate

Port estates present both an advantage and a constraint:

  • Advantage: substantial electrical infrastructure and in-house engineering staff. Charger installation is an internal capital project, often already planned as part of shore-power or yard electrification programmes.
  • Constraint: available capacity is frequently committed to cranes, reefers and shore power. Survey the substation before sizing chargers — this is the step most often skipped and most often costly.
  • Configuration: 22–44 kW AC per parking bay for overnight depot charging is usually sufficient and cheapest. A shared 120 kW DC unit per 4–6 trucks supports two-shift operations.
  • Environment: salt-laden air is corrosive. Specify marine-grade enclosure protection for chargers and connectors — see corrosion protection for coastal operations.

Specification Checklist for Port Transfer Fleets

  1. Trip distances and counts for each of the four circuits.
  2. Average and peak gate dwell time.
  3. Body requirement: dry box, curtainsider, or tail-lift equipped.
  4. Whether customs sealing or bonded operation imposes body constraints.
  5. Available electrical capacity at the depot and its distance from parking.
  6. Ambient conditions: tropical heat, salt air, monsoon rainfall.

Security and Customs Considerations

Port transfer work sits inside a customs-controlled perimeter, which imposes requirements that do not apply to ordinary distribution.

  • Bonded movements. Where goods move under customs seal between a bonded warehouse and the terminal, the body must support sealing and the sealing method must be documented in the operating procedure.
  • Driver and vehicle accreditation. Port access credentials are issued per driver and per vehicle; adding electric units to an accredited fleet requires the registration data to match, which is why the specification sheet matters.
  • Gate camera and plate recognition. Confirm the electric units are correctly registered in the terminal operating system so that automated gate processing is not delayed by an unrecognised vehicle record.
  • Charger siting inside the secure perimeter. Charging infrastructure located in a customs area may need additional approval; raise it during the port authority engagement rather than after installation.

Where transfer work extends onto public roads — typically the off-dock depot leg — the vehicle also falls under general road vehicle rules, including mass and dimension limits and, in many cities, low-emission zone access. Electrification helps with the latter but not with the former: check the GVW against the road classification before committing to a route.
