Does the Electric Truck Business Case Work at Small Scale?

Yes - with one condition: utilization. The electric truck's economics - 60-75% lower energy cost per kilometer, 40-60% lower maintenance - scale with kilometers driven, not fleet size. A five-truck fleet running 200 km daily per truck captures the same per-kilometer savings as a five-hundred-truck fleet. What changes at small scale is the margin for error: a large fleet absorbs a poorly matched truck; a small fleet cannot. So the small-fleet rule is simple - electrify the highest-mileage, most regular route first, with the truck precisely matched to it. The KT5M electric box truck, with 280 km of certified range from a 140 kWh pack, covers the urban and regional duty cycles where small fleets earn their living.

What Is the Minimum Charging Setup for a Small Fleet?

Smaller than most buyers fear. A single depot charger - 60-120 kW DC - serves a fleet of three to eight trucks comfortably, because small fleets rarely need simultaneous fast charging: trucks charge overnight sequentially and top up during loading windows. The KT5M recovers 20-80% in about an hour on a 120 kW unit, so one charger supports a working day's staggered needs. The electrical service requirement - a three-phase connection of adequate capacity - exists at most commercial premises in Jordan, or can be upgraded for a fraction of the truck investment. What small fleets do not need: public charging dependence, battery-swap subscriptions, or million-dollar depot rebuilds. Charge where the trucks sleep, and the infrastructure question shrinks to one charger and a utility conversation.

What Mistakes Should Small Fleets Avoid?

Five, in order of frequency. Buying for the exception rather than the rule - specify for the route you run daily, not the longest trip of the year; rent for exceptions. Undersizing charging - one reliable charger beats two cheap ones. Skipping driver training - a single day of energy-efficient driving instruction returns 10-15% range improvement, free capacity forever. Ignoring the data - the KT5M's telematics tells you within weeks which routes beat planning figures and which need adjustment; small fleets cannot afford to fly blind. And going unsupported - the Jordan electric truck market page outlines regional support structures for Jordan, and our team works with single-unit buyers as readily as hundred-unit ones. Small scale is a reason to be precise, not a reason to wait.

Deployment Timeline from Order to Operation

Realistic scheduling prevents the most common fleet electrification failures. A typical KT5M deployment runs five to seven months end to end: production takes 30-60 days depending on configuration and order size; ocean freight to the Port of Aqaba adds 25-45 days; customs clearance and homologation steps add two to six weeks depending on Jordanian procedures. Running in parallel, the depot charging installation should complete before the trucks land, and driver and technician training should finish during commissioning week. The sequence that works: confirm specifications and sign the contract, start certification paperwork immediately, order chargers at the same time as the trucks, and book training against the vessel arrival date. Fleets that run these tracks in parallel put trucks into revenue service within days of clearance; fleets that run them sequentially lose months. Driver Acceptance and Daily Operation

Driver acceptance decides whether an electric fleet reaches its modeled savings, and the KT5M earns acceptance quickly. The 160 kW motor delivers instant torque with no gear hunting, the cab is quieter than any diesel equivalent, and regenerative braking reduces fatigue in stop-start duty. Drivers typically adapt within a week, and most prefer the electric truck once they experience a full shift without engine noise and vibration. The habits that matter are small: smooth acceleration, anticipation to maximize regeneration, charging connection on return to depot, and honest reporting of dash warnings. Fleets in Jordan report the fastest adoption when they involve senior drivers in the pilot phase and let peer experience do the convincing, rather than imposing the change top-down. Energy consumption per driver is visible in the telematics data, and a little friendly competition between drivers on efficiency measurably improves fleet-wide consumption in the first quarter.
