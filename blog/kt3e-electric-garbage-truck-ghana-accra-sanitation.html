Why Accra's sanitation fleet is moving to electric collection

Accra generates roughly 3,000 tonnes of solid waste every day, and close to 40 percent is still collected by ageing diesel compactor trucks that burn 28 to 34 litres of diesel per 100 km of stop-start city driving. At Ghana's current diesel price of about $1.10 per litre, a single 18-tonne diesel refuse truck costs $310 to $375 in fuel each week before any maintenance. The KT3E removes that recurring line item by replacing it with grid electricity priced between $0.08 and $0.18 per kWh for commercial users in the Greater Accra Region. For a contractor running 15 trucks, that shift alone can free more than $200,000 a year in operating budget.

The second driver is noise. Diesel compactors wake residents on the 05:00 collection window, and several Accra districts have filed noise complaints that delay routes. The KT3E runs at under 75 dB during compaction and under 65 dB while driving, which lets councils start collection earlier and finish before the 08:00 traffic peak on the Tema Motorway and the Ring Road. Quieter streets also mean fewer complaints and a stronger case when the next metropolitan collection tender is awarded.

Duty cycle: a full dawn-to-dusk shift

Most Accra routes run from 05:00 to 13:00, covering four to six collection loops per vehicle before the midday heat. The KT3E draws roughly 1.3 to 1.7 kWh per km in heavy compactor duty, so a 200 km shift consumes 260 to 340 kWh. At $0.12 per kWh that equals $31 to $41 of electricity, against more than $320 of diesel for an equivalent truck. Crews report the instant torque from the electric motor helps on the steep Adenta and Achimota grades, and the smooth low-speed control reduces bin spillage on narrow Osu and Madina streets.

Regenerative braking recovers energy on the frequent stops between houses, adding 8 to 12 percent back into the pack over a shift. That recovery is the single biggest reason the KT3E beats diesel on the stop-start profile that defines Ghanaian urban collection, where a diesel unit spends most of its fuel idling at the kerb.

Total cost of ownership versus diesel

Over a 5-year, 250,000-km service life the numbers favour the EV truck clearly across four cost lines:

  • Fuel energy: diesel at $0.9 to $1.4 per litre versus electricity at $0.05 to $0.18 per kWh, a 60 to 70 percent lower energy cost per km.
  • Maintenance: no engine oil, no exhaust after-treatment, and far fewer brake jobs thanks to regen braking, about 35 percent lower service spend.
  • Battery residual: the CATL LFP pack still holds strong capacity past 4,500 cycles, supporting a better trade-in value at year five.
  • Compliance: the KT3E is exempt from planned Accra low-emission zone fees that will apply to diesel trucks from 2027.

To make the math concrete, take a 20-truck contract over five years. Diesel fuel for 20 trucks at 250,000 km each totals roughly 1.7 million litres, or $1.87 million at $1.10 per litre. The same distance on the KT3E at 1.5 kWh per km needs 7.5 million kWh, costing $0.9 million at $0.12 per kWh — a saving of $0.97 million before maintenance. Add the 35 percent lower service bill, roughly $0.4 million over five years, and the EV case is hard to argue against for any Accra contractor bidding the next metropolitan collection tender.

Depot charging and power requirements

A depot with two 120 kW DC chargers serves a fleet of 10 KT3E trucks inside an overnight window. Peak draw is 240 kW, which fits a standard 400 V commercial supply with a modest upgrade. For depots without three-phase power, a 60 kW charger restores a pack in about 5 hours, still inside the off-peak window when Ghana Grid Company tariffs drop to roughly $0.08 per kWh. We recommend a load-management controller so no more than two trucks fast-charge at once, keeping demand charges under control.

Solar can further cut cost: a 50 kW rooftop array at a Tema depot offsets about 30 percent of the fleet's daytime top-up, and battery-to-grid pilots are under discussion with the Energy Commission for 2027. Landed FOB for the KT3E sits between $58,000 and $75,000, so a 10-truck depot represents a $580,000 to $750,000 equipment decision that pays back through fuel avoidance.

Safety, training and driver adoption

Electric trucks change the daily routine for crews, and the KT3E's low centre of gravity and instant torque take a short adaptation period. We run a one-day driver clinic covering regenerative braking, the electronic parking brake and the silent start sequence so operators do not roll the compactor unexpectedly. Mechanics receive a separate high-voltage safety course before any pack work, and the local spare-parts kit includes the contactors and compressor most likely to need service in year one.

Adoption data from early Ghanaian pilots shows crews prefer the KT3E within two weeks, citing the quiet cab, the lack of gearshift fatigue and the predictable morning range.
