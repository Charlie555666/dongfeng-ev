Cement Plant Shuttle Math

A typical Baghdad or Basra batching plant runs trucks on a 25 km loop: 12 km loaded to the site, 12 km back empty, plus yard maneuvering. At 1.45 kWh/km for the chassis and roughly 0.4 kWh per cubic metre for drum rotation, a full 8 m3 load costs about 36–40 kWh per round trip. A 350 kWh pack therefore supports roughly eight to nine round trips, or a full shift, before the midday top-up. With a 240 kW charger at the plant, a 45-minute lunch break returns 150–180 kWh, enough for the afternoon. This is the duty cycle where an EV truck beats diesel hardest, because the diesel is idling the drum through every queue.

Drum Power and Mix Quality

The drum is powered from the traction battery through a dedicated inverter, so rotation speed is held constant regardless of chassis speed. That matters for mix quality: a constant 2–3 rpm in transit prevents segregation and keeps slump within spec even on a 40-minute haul in summer heat. Because the drum draws from the same liquid-cooled pack, the thermal loop also protects drum performance, and drivers report steadier slump than on mechanically driven diesel drums that slow with engine rpm. The result is fewer rejected loads and a tighter spec for ready-mix contracts.

Depot Charging for Mixer Fleets

A mixer fleet charges differently from a line-haul fleet because the trucks return to one plant every shift.

  • Install 240–360 kW DC at the batching plant so the lunch top-up is enough.
  • Schedule charging around the two daily peak pour windows, not continuously.
  • Keep one spare charger module on site; a fault should not stop the next shift.
  • Pre-cool the pack in summer so the afternoon charge holds full rate.

Scheduled service is light: reduction-gear oil check at 60,000 km, coolant swap at 200,000 km, and drum-bearing inspection at 30,000 km. Because the drum and chassis share one pack, there is no second fuel system to maintain.

Maintenance and Driver Training

A mixer fleet's biggest hidden cost is the drum driveline, and the TZ8J removes the diesel half of it. With one pack driving both chassis and drum, there is no separate engine to tune, no PTO shaft to align, and no hydraulic pump driven off a struggling motor. The reduction gear and the drum bearing are the only rotating service items, and both are spec'd for the dusty, aggregate-heavy environment of an Iraqi batching plant. Drivers need roughly half a day of training: the drum inverter holds speed automatically, so the only new habit is watching the pack gauge instead of the fuel gauge and plugging in at the plant between pours. Dispatch can keep the same round-trip math it used for diesel because the 8 m3 load and the 25 km loop are unchanged, so the move to electric does not disturb the daily schedule.
