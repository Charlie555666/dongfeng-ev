Electric Power Take-Off (ePTO) on Electric Trucks: How Mixers, Sweepers and Reefers Draw Battery Power

The feature that makes an electric truck more than a battery on wheels is the electric power take-off, or ePTO. Where a diesel truck spins its attachments — mixer drum, garbage compactor, sweeper brooms, sprinkler pump, tipper hoist, reefer unit — from a belt off the engine, an electric truck draws that power straight from the traction battery through a dedicated motor and inverter. This article explains how ePTO works, how to size the battery for it, and why it is more efficient than the hydraulic PTO it replaces.

What ePTO Actually Is

On a diesel truck, a power take-off is a mechanical gearbox tap on the transmission that drives a pump or shaft when the engine runs. The electric truck replaces that mechanical link with an electrical one: the traction battery feeds a separate ePTO inverter that powers the body equipment’s motor directly. The chassis can be stationary, the drum or compactor still runs, and no diesel engine idles to make it happen. Power levels for body equipment run 10–40 kW continuous, well within the headroom of a 180–600 kWh CATL LFP pack. The elegance is in the decoupling: the body works whether or not the truck is moving, at a speed the operator sets, with no mechanical line from a hot engine through a clutch to a pump.

Every one of these bodies on a diesel chassis shares one failing engine and one fuel tank; on the electric chassis they share one pack and one control system, which is simpler to maintain and far cleaner to operate. The reefer case is the clearest: a diesel reefer is effectively a second engine with its own fuel, emissions, and maintenance, while an electric reefer is just another ePTO load on the same pack.

Sizing the Battery for ePTO Duty

The trap in an ePTO spec is forgetting the attachment in the energy budget. A mixer that drives its drum 40% of a shift at 30 kW adds roughly 12 kWh per operating hour of body load on top of the 1.5–2.0 kWh per km of propulsion. Sizing rules we apply:

  • Add the ePTO continuous load × hours of body operation to the propulsion energy for the day.
  • Hold pack state of charge above the warranty floor — CATL LFP is happy at high SOC, which ePTO-heavy duty exploits.
  • Spec the pack 15–25% above the propulsion-only figure so the drum or reefer never forces a charge interruption.
  • Confirm the ePTO inverter rating against the peak start-up current of the heaviest body (compactors and reefers spike on start).

A second sizing rule is thermal: a pack that powers a 40 kW reefer in 45°C heat needs cooling headroom the temperate-market default omits. Fenghan specifies heat-rated cooling on every ePTO truck bound for the Gulf and North Africa so the body load never trips the pack into protection.

ePTO Efficiency vs Hydraulic PTO

The diesel hydraulic PTO chains three losses: engine to transmission, transmission to pump, pump to hydraulic motor — each a mechanical and fluid loss that burns fuel even when the body idles. The electric ePTO chains one: battery to inverter to motor, with regeneration possible on hoist and compactor down-strokes. The result is 15–30% less energy for the same body work, and the elimination of the diesel engine that the hydraulic system depends on. On a sweeper or mixer the operator also gets instant, infinitely variable control — drum speed and brush pressure set from the cab without a clutch. The efficiency gain shows up directly in cost per cubic metre of concrete or cost per kilometre swept, which is what the contract is written on.

Deployment Considerations for Body Builders

Two practical points decide a clean ePTO integration. First, the body manufacturer must speak the truck’s high-voltage protocol so the drum or compactor starts and stops on the cab command rather than a separate switch; Fenghan coordinates this at order time. Second, the ePTO and propulsion inverters should share cooling and diagnostics so the depot sees one thermal picture, not two. Done well, the operator experiences the body as just another function of the truck; done badly, it is a second system to fault-find. The difference is integration discipline, not hardware cost.

Conclusion

Electric power take-off is what turns a battery truck into a working truck. By drawing 10–40 kW straight from the traction battery, ePTO runs mixers, compactors, sweepers, sprinklers, tippers, and reefers more efficiently and more cleanly than the hydraulic PTO it replaces — provided the pack is sized for the body load, not just the road. For any specialised body, ePTO is the reason to go electric.
