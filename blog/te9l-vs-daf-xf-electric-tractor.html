Dongfeng TE9L vs DAF XF Electric: The Long-Haul Electric Tractor EV Truck Comparison

Every serious international fleet buyer now asks the same question in two halves: what does a European electric tractor like the DAF XF Electric deliver for its price, and what does a Chinese flagship like the Dongfeng TE9L deliver for roughly half of it? The XF Electric — DAF's battery-electric variant of Europe's workhorse XF cab-over — is a genuine engineering achievement: 270–525 kWh pack options, PACCAR's dealer network across Europe, and cab comfort refined over decades of continental haulage. The TE9L is our answer from the other direction: 600 kWh of CATL LFP energy, a 360 kW-class LvKong drive, 49 t GCW capability and an FOB around USD 118,000–145,000. This comparison is written for the buyer deciding between them — and for the buyer in Africa, the Middle East, Central Asia or Latin America deciding whether the European badge is worth the premium in their market at all. The price line deserves immediate framing. In Europe, purchase incentives, CO2 credit economics and dealer-financing structures narrow the effective gap — the XF Electric's sticker is not what a European fleet pays net. Outside Europe, none of those supports exist: a Middle Eastern, African or Central Asian buyer pays full price for both trucks, and the effective delivered-price gap runs 2:1 or wider. That is the market reality this comparison is really about.

Where the DAF XF Electric Wins

  • European regulatory integration. EU type approval, tachograph integration, driver-cocooning, and full compliance with GSR2 and the coming safety revisions — the XF Electric is engineered for the densest regulatory environment on Earth. If your fleet runs in the EU, this matters enormously.
  • Dealer network and parts proximity. PACCAR's European service web means a technician and a part within hours almost anywhere in the EU. That support density is unmatched by any Chinese exporter's remote-support model today.
  • Cab and driver environment. DAF's XF cab is the benchmark of continental trucking comfort — a real factor in driver retention for long-haul operators in high-wage markets.
  • Residual value machinery. European dealers can finance the XF Electric against a residual-value assumption the secondary market partially underwrites; Chinese electric tractors are still building their European resale record.

Where the TE9L Wins

  1. Battery capacity per dollar — by a factor of two to three. The TE9L carries up to ~600 kWh of CATL LFP for roughly half the XF Electric's effective ex-EU price. Outside Europe, range-per-dollar is the scarce commodity and the TE9L delivers more of it than any truck we export.
  2. Energy certainty. CATL LFP chemistry with an 8-year / 4,500-cycle warranty frame — the most bankable battery warranty structure in the industry — versus the variant-dependent European pack options.
  3. Heat tolerance. The TE9L's thermal file is engineered for 45–50 °C Gulf and equatorial duty; European electric tractors are optimised for European temperature bands. In Gulf, South Asian or Sahel summer duty, that engineering difference is not cosmetic.
  4. Market-fit flexibility. GCC, Central Asian, African and Latin American markets have no CO2-credit machinery favouring EU brands, no dealer-network advantage for DAF, and often 49 t GCW regimes the 44 t-tuned XF Electric cannot exploit.
  5. Independent charging compatibility. CCS2 at 240 kW class integrates with the OCPP charger ecosystems growing across the Gulf, Central Asia and Latin America without vendor lock-in.

The Economics Outside Europe

Run the same corridor duty — 300 km/day, 45 t GCW, 330 operating days — in a Gulf or Central Asian market with diesel at USD 0.85/L and electricity at USD 0.07/kWh:

  • TE9L: energy cost per year ≈ USD 8,300 (at 1.2 kWh/km); maintenance ≈ USD 4,000; annual operating advantage over diesel ≈ USD 34,000–40,000. Incremental capital over a diesel tractor ≈ USD 60,000–75,000 — payback inside 24 months.
  • XF Electric: identical energy economics, but the incremental capital over diesel is USD 150,000–220,000 in non-EU markets — payback of 5–7 years with no CO2-credit machinery to shorten it.

Same physics, different arithmetic. The XF Electric is a superb truck priced for a European support-and-incentive ecosystem; outside that ecosystem, the buyer pays the badge premium without receiving the offsetting machinery. The TE8L covers the 49 t segment at a lower price point for shorter corridors if the TE9L's range is more than the duty needs.

Honest Boundaries for the TE9L

Three things we tell every TE9L buyer plainly. First, EU-market entry is not its game — homologation and dealer economics make the XF Electric the rational EU choice; the TE9L's markets are the rest of the world. Second, cab comfort, while improving every generation, is not the DAF XF cabin — fleets whose drivers expect European ergonomics should trial before committing. Third, local service depends on our spares-and-remote-diagnostics package rather than a native dealer web — we mitigate with first-year consignments and regional technicians, but a buyer in a very remote market should plan a technician training programme as part of the order.

The Verdict by Market

Buying in Europe: the XF Electric, because its price is net of supports and its dealer web is unmatched. Buying anywhere the sun is hot and the budget is real: the TE9L delivers 600 kWh of CATL range, 49 t capability and a two-year payback that the European badge cannot mathematically justify. The global electric tractor market is splitting exactly along that line — and it is why the TE9L's order book grows fastest in the markets Europe forgot to subsidise.

The Portfolio Answer

Some buyers, reading this comparison, ask which truck a fleet should buy if it operates in both worlds — European routes and everything else. The portfolio answer is increasingly common among the international groups we serve: XF Electric-class units for the EU-regulated business where their supports and dealer web apply, TE9L-class units for the Gulf, African, Central Asian and Latin American corridors where their price and thermal engineering win, and a single charging-standards discipline (CCS2) across both so that the depot architecture never forks. The badge on the grille matters less than the honest match of machine to market — and the honest match, outside Europe, has been decided by arithmetic. DAF builds a superb truck for the ecosystem it was engineered for; we build one for the other eighty percent of the planet's freight corridors.

Both companies will sell every truck they can build this decade. The buyer's job is only to make sure each one is working on the right continent.
