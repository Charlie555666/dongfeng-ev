How Do I Choose Between Battery Swap and Fast Charging for Electric Trucks?

Quick answer: Choose fast charging if your trucks work a single shift and return to a depot with hours to spare, because it is far cheaper to build. Choose battery swap if your trucks run two or more shifts and a 75-minute charging stop would idle revenue-earning vehicles, because swap restores a full pack in 5 to 6 minutes.

What Is the Real Difference Between the Two

Fast charging pushes energy into the truck's fixed battery at 120 to 240 kW, restoring 20 to 80 percent in 75 to 90 minutes, and the charger itself costs a few tens of thousands of dollars. Battery swap physically exchanges the entire pack at a station costing $350,000 to $600,000 plus a shared battery pool, but the exchange takes 5 to 6 minutes, about the time a diesel truck spends at the pump. The TZ5E electric dump truck is available in both specifications precisely because fleets split on this choice.

The deeper difference is asset structure. With charging, you own the battery and its degradation. With swap, the station operator owns and manages the packs, and you pay an energy-plus-service fee per exchange, which moves battery risk off your balance sheet.

How Do I Know Which One My Fleet Needs

The deciding variable is schedule pressure. Map your trucks' daily calendars. If every vehicle parks at the depot for six or more hours overnight, charging wins on cost with no operational compromise. If vehicles run back-to-back shifts and the only gaps are 30 to 60 minutes, a 75-minute charge costs you payload hours every single day, and swap pays for itself in recovered utilization. A useful threshold: if charging downtime costs more per day than the swap service fee, swap is the right answer.

  • Single-shift depot fleets: choose 120 to 240 kW depot charging.
  • Two-shift fleets with 60-minute gaps: swap usually wins.
  • Continuous 24-hour operations: swap is almost always correct.
  • Remote single-truck sites: charging, because swap needs density.
  • Mixed fleets: depot charging plus one shared regional swap hub.

How Do I Make the Final Decision

Run the numbers on your own calendar: calculate the revenue a truck earns per productive hour, multiply by the charging downtime per day, and compare the annual figure against the swap station's service fees and capital recovery. If downtime cost exceeds swap cost, swap wins; otherwise charge. Frequently Asked Questions

How do I choose between battery swap and fast charging?

Choose fast charging if trucks park at a depot six or more hours daily, since a 240 kW charger costs far less to build. Choose battery swap if trucks run multi-shift schedules where a 75-minute charge would idle revenue, because swap restores a full 350 to 450 kWh pack in 5 to 6 minutes.

How much cheaper is depot charging than a swap station?

A depot with 240 kW chargers costs $80,000 to $160,000 for a 20-truck fleet, versus $350,000 to $600,000 plus a battery pool for a swap station. Charging energy runs $0.08 to $0.15 per kWh off-peak, while swap adds a $0.20 to $0.30 per kWh service fee on top of energy.

Does battery swap damage the battery faster than charging?

No, swap often extends pack life because removed packs charge slowly and cool at the station rather than fast-charging hot in the truck. CATL LFP packs carry an 8-year or 4,500-cycle warranty either way, and in the swap model the station operator manages that warranty for you.

Can one electric truck use both swap and charging?

Yes. Swap-specification models such as the TZ5E electric dump truck retain a 240 kW DC charging port, restoring 20 to 80 percent in about 75 minutes at the depot while also accepting 5 to 6 minute pack exchanges at any compatible station, so fleets can run a hybrid model.

Which fleets gain the most from battery swapping?

Multi-shift fleets with tight schedules gain the most: urban delivery on 18 to 24 hour cycles, port drayage tractors, and mining dump trucks running 40 to 50 cycles per day.
