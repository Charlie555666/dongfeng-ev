Electric Truck Plug Standards: CCS2 vs GB/T Export Guide

Electric truck fleets live or die by their connector standard, because the plug you choose at import time locks in every charger, adapter and maintenance contract for the next decade. A Dongfeng EV truck built with a CCS2 inlet will never clip into a GB/T station without an external adapter, and a 600 kWh pack sized for MCS megawatt charging needs a different cable architecture entirely. Buyers who ignore this on the first purchase order usually pay twice: once for the trucks, then again for redundant depot hardware. This guide compares CCS2, GB/T and MCS so overseas fleet operators and government procurement teams can specify the right inlet before the vessel leaves the Chinese port.

The global heavy EV truck market has not settled on a single plug, and that fragmentation is the single biggest hidden cost in any cross-border rollout. Europe, the Gulf Cooperation Council and most of Latin America have standardized on CCS2. China, the world's largest electric truck producer, still fits the GB/T connector as its domestic default, and many export units ship with that inlet unless the buyer requests otherwise. A third standard, MCS (Megawatt Charging System), is now arriving for the largest 500-600 kWh packs. The KTH3 electric cargo truck is a good example of a mid-size 350-424 kWh unit that can be ordered with either CCS2 or GB/T to match the destination market.

CCS2: the export default for Europe, GCC and Latin America

CCS2 (Combined Charging System 2) uses a seven-pin DC connector rated up to 500 A and 1,000 V, which supports 350-360 kW DC fast charging on most truck-grade units. For a 350 kWh pack, a 360 kW CCS2 charger moves the battery from 10 percent to 80 percent in roughly 60-75 minutes. European fleets favor CCS2 because the public charging network, the zero-emission zone rules and the local service ecosystem are all built around it. The Gulf and Latin American importers increasingly demand CCS2 for the same reason: spare parts and trained technicians are easier to find.

For most electric truck operators running daily urban or regional routes of 200-350 km, CCS2 at 120-360 kW is more than enough. A 282 kW LvKong motor paired with a 262-350 kWh CATL LFP pack recharges overnight on a 150 kW unit or during a driver break on a 300 kW unit. The practical ceiling is the truck's own onboard acceptance, not the connector. Procurement teams should also note that CCS2 cables for trucks are heavier than car cables, rated for 5-7 meters and often liquid-cooled above 200 kW. Budget $3,000-8,000 per cooled cable and plan storage reels so the cable does not sit on the ground and degrade. A depot with ten 360 kW stalls therefore carries $30,000-80,000 of cable inventory alone, which is why right-sizing the connector at import is cheaper than re-equipping later.

GB/T: the China standard still on most Chinese export trucks

GB/T 20234 is the Chinese national standard, available in GB/T DC (nine pins) and GB/T AC variants. It is electrically similar to CCS2 in voltage and current ceilings but uses a different physical keying and communication protocol (GB/T 27930 versus ISO 15118). Many Dongfeng export trucks ship with GB/T inlets because the production line defaults to the domestic connector. That is fine for fleets operating in China or in markets where Chinese-built charging infrastructure dominates, but it creates friction elsewhere.

The good news is that GB/T-to-CCS2 adapters are mature and widely available at 60-250 kW ratings, typically costing $1,200-4,500 per unit depending on current rating. Adapters above 250 kW get expensive and bulky, so operators who need 300+ kW charging should specify CCS2 at the factory rather than adapt later. Battery swap models, which complete a pack exchange in 5-6 minutes, sidestep the connector question entirely because the swap cabinet handles the power interface. Some importers ask whether they can simply convert a GB/T truck to CCS2 in the field. The answer is no for the high-voltage DC side without replacing the inlet, the wiring harness and the charging control unit, a job that costs $4,000-9,000 per truck and voids parts of the warranty. Factory specification is always cheaper and safer, which is why the purchase order language matters more than the truck's sticker price.

MCS: megawatt charging for 500-600 kWh packs

MCS (Megawatt Charging System) is the coming standard for the largest electric trucks. It is specified up to 1,250 A and 1,000 V, delivering up to 1 MW of power. That matters for 500-600 kWh packs such as the one in the TZ3V dump truck (600 kWh) or the TE8P heavy tractor (600 kWh / 510 kW). At 1 MW, a 600 kWh pack can be topped from 10 percent to 80 percent in about 35-45 minutes, a figure that starts to rival diesel refuel times for high-utilization operations.

MCS hardware is still rolling out in 2026, with pilot depots in mining and port logistics leading adoption. Fleets buying 600 kWh trucks today should ask for a CCS2 depot charger now and reserve MCS bay space, because retrofitting cable trays and cooling later costs far more than planning for it during the initial grid connection. Plan a 1.5-2.5 MVA grid feed for every 20-truck depot so the megawatt bay can be energized when the cabinet arrives. As a rule of thumb, treat the connector-related spend as a real line item of $2,000-5,000 per truck across inlets, adapters, cables and spares.

Adapters and dual-port trucks: closing the gap

Where standards collide, three practical options exist:

  • Specify CCS2 at the factory for Europe, GCC and Latin America-bound units; add $0-1,500 to the build for the re-terminated inlet.
  • Order GB/T units for China-aligned markets and buy 60-250 kW adapters at $1,200-4,500 each for occasional cross-standard charging.
  • Choose battery-swap models for operations where 5-6 minute pack exchange removes the connector dependency.
  • Reserve one MCS-capable bay per 20-truck depot if any 500-600 kWh units are in the fleet plan.

Dual-port trucks that carry both CCS2 and GB/T inlets are available on some chassis and cost about $2,000-3,500 extra, but they simplify mixed-standard depots and reduce adapter inventory. For government tenders that must serve multiple regions, the dual-port option is often the lowest-risk specification and avoids a stranded-asset problem if the local standard shifts.

How to specify the connector in your import order

Write the connector requirement into the technical schedule of the purchase order, not the cover letter. State the inlet type, the maximum DC current the truck will accept, and the communication protocol (ISO 15118 for CCS2, GB/T 27930 for GB/T). Confirm that the onboard charger acceptance matches the depot charger output: there is no value in a 360 kW charger if the truck only accepts 150 kW. The Dominican Republic electric truck market shows a typical mixed-standard situation where CCS2 public chargers are being built while many imported Chinese units still arrive with GB/T, making adapter planning essential.

Request a factory charging compatibility sheet listing every connector, cable and protocol the vehicle supports, and have your local electrician verify the depot side before the trucks clear customs. A mismatch discovered after arrival means weeks of downtime and air-freighted adapters at premium prices. Keep the signed compatibility sheet with the vehicle registration file so future mechanics do not improvise a connection.

Cost and lead-time reality check

Budget the charging interface as a line item, not an afterthought. CCS2 inlet re-termination adds $0-1,500 per truck. GB/T adapters run $1,200-4,500 each. A 360 kW DC charger for a depot costs roughly $35,000-70,000 installed, while a 1 MW MCS cabinet can reach $150,000-300,000 once cooling and switchgear are included. Grid connection for a 20-truck depot with 360 kW chargers needs about 1.5-2.5 MVA, and utility upgrades take 6-18 months in most markets, so the connector decision should be made at least a year before first delivery.

Get the inlet right on the purchase order and the rest of the depot plan falls into place. Specify CCS2 for Western and GCC fleets, keep GB/T adapters for China-aligned operations, and reserve MCS capacity for any 500-600 kWh units. Finally, document the standard in the operator manual and the spare-parts list so that drivers and mechanics never improvise a connection. A wrong adapter at 1,000 V can destroy a $40,000 battery pack in seconds. The connector you choose is not a detail; it is the foundation of the entire electric truck investment.
