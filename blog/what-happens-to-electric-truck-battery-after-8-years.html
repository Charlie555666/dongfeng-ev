What Happens to an Electric Truck Battery After 8 Years? Warranty Guide

Quick answer: After 8 years an electric truck battery typically retains 70 to 80 percent of its original capacity, well inside the 8-year or 4,500-cycle warranty that ships with Dongfeng LFP packs. The pack is not waste: it still holds value for second-life stationary storage at about $20 to $40 per kWh, or it can be recycled. A replacement pack today costs roughly $90 to $130 per kWh at the pack level, so planning for end of warranty is a budget item, not a crisis for any EV truck operator.

What Does an Electric Truck Battery Look Like After 8 Years?

An LFP pack from a TZ3V electric dump truck that has run eight years of shifts shows gradual, predictable wear rather than sudden failure. Cells lose capacity and internal resistance rises, but the pack stays safe and usable because LFP is chemically stable. A fleet health report at year eight typically shows the pack still delivering 70 to 80 percent of nameplate energy, enough for reduced but real duty. The battery management system logs every cycle, so you can prove condition to a buyer or insurer with data rather than guesswork.

How Much Capacity Is Left at 4,500 Cycles?

The warranty is written to 8 years or 4,500 cycles, and field data shows packs crossing that line at 70 to 80 percent state of health. A cycle here means a full equivalent charge and discharge, so a truck that partly charges daily can hit 4,500 cycles before eight years, while a lightly used unit may reach eight years with fewer cycles. Either way the chemistry's flat degradation curve means the last percent of warranty life is as usable as the first, with no cliff before the 70 percent floor that triggers a claim.

Does the Warranty Cover a Battery Below 70 Percent?

Yes, the 8-year or 4,500-cycle warranty covers the pack and replaces it free if capacity falls below 70 percent before the limit is reached. If the pack is still above 70 percent at the end of warranty, it is performing as designed and stays in service. Buyers should read the exact threshold and the claim process, because some warranties prorate or require authorized servicing. Keeping maintenance records and using approved chargers protects your claim if a premature drop occurs well inside the coverage window.

Which Second-Life Uses Make Sense for Old Packs?

A pack at 70 to 80 percent capacity is ideal for stationary storage where weight and volume do not matter: solar buffers, depot peak shaving, microgrids and backup power. These second-life units sell for about $20 to $40 per kWh, turning a retired truck pack into revenue instead of scrap. A 424 kWh pack could fetch $8,000 to $17,000 in the second-life market. Only when a pack is unfit for storage does it move to recycling, where metals are recovered for new cells and the loop closes responsibly.

How Much Does an Electric Truck Battery Replacement Cost?

A replacement LFP pack costs roughly $90 to $130 per kWh at the pack level in 2026. For a 424 kWh pack that is about $38,000 to $55,000, and for a 600 kWh mining pack about $54,000 to $78,000. Spreading that over the years after warranty, the per-km replacement cost is modest compared with cumulative diesel fuel saved. Many fleets pre-fund a battery reserve from the savings earned while the original pack was under warranty, so the swap is planned rather than painful when the time arrives.

Are Fast Chargers Shortening Battery Life?

Fast charging at 360 kW does not materially shorten life if the pack stays liquid-cooled, with degradation still tracking the 4,500-cycle warranty. Occasional fast charges are safe; sustained high-temperature abuse is what actually accelerates capacity loss. The battery management system limits charge rate when cells are too cold or too hot, so following the recommended charging profile keeps the pack on its designed curve. For most fleets, convenience charging is a non-issue for longevity.
