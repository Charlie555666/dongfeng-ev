Saudi SABER and SASO Certification Step by Step for EV Truck Importers

Importers bringing an EV truck into Saudi Arabia must clear the SABER platform and SASO technical regulations before a unit is released at Dammam or Jeddah. SABER is the Saudi Product Safety Programme that issues the Certificate of Conformity (PCoC) and the Shipment Conformity Certificate (SCoC) that customs demands at the border. For an electric truck the high-voltage battery, the charging system, and the thermal safety file are the items SASO scrutinises most. This step-by-step guide explains the 2026 path so importers can plan lead time and avoid the costly mistake of shipping before the certificates are live. What SABER and SASO Actually Require

SASO (Saudi Standards, Metrology and Quality Organization) sets the technical rules; SABER is the online system that issues the certificates against those rules. For road vehicles the relevant family is the Saudi GSO vehicle regulation plus the SASO energy and electrical-safety requirements. An electric truck is assessed on three pillars: the vehicle type approval, the high-voltage electrical safety of the battery and harness, and the charging inlet compatibility with regional standards. The importer opens a SABER account, registers the product with the manufacturer data, selects a SASO-accredited conformity body, and uploads the test evidence.

The single most common failure point is submitting a battery test report that does not map to the exact pack model and chemistry on the unit. Dongfeng EV trucks use CATL LFP packs; the report must name that pack, the cell format, and the thermal-runaway test result. A mismatch between paperwork and physical pack is an automatic SCoC rejection.

Step 1: Product Certificate of Conformity (PCoC)

The PCoC is the long-lived certificate for the product type, valid for one year. The importer or manufacturer registers the TE8L or other model on SABER with the HS code, the technical dossier, and the SASO-aligned test reports. For the TE8L electric tractor the dossier covers the 350 kWh CATL LFP pack, the LvKong 282 to 350 kW motor, the insulation monitoring, and the charge inlet. SASO review typically takes 5 to 15 working days once a complete file is in, and a satisfactory factory audit or existing IECEE recognition can shorten it. Budget two to four weeks of calendar time before you book ocean freight.

Step 2: Shipment Conformity Certificate (SCoC)

The SCoC is issued per shipment and is the document customs scans at the port. It references the active PCoC and confirms the specific batch matches the certified type. Apply for the SCoC after the bill of lading is cut but before the vessel berths; SASO issues it in 1 to 3 working days for a clean file. If the PCoC has lapsed or the HS code differs from the registered product, the SCoC is blocked and the container sits. The clean rule is: renew the PCoC before it expires, and never change the declared specification mid-shipment.

Prepare the Arabic-language safety label and operator manual early. SASO requires the high-voltage warning markings and the emergency response card to be in Arabic on the vehicle, and a separate Arabic manual in the document set. This is a frequent and easily avoided delay.

Step 4: Customs Clearance and Depot Readiness

With the SCoC live, Dammam or Jeddah customs clears the unit against the SABER record. Plan for 3 to 7 working days of clearance plus transport to the Riyadh or Eastern Province depot. The depot must have a commissioned DC charger before the truck earns its first revenue kilometre; a 120 to 350 kW post with climate-controlled cooling is the right spec for a 350 kWh tractor in Gulf summer heat, where pack temperature management is the difference between 280 km and 230 km of real range.

  • Open SABER account and link the importer CR and SASO accreditation.
  • Register the model with exact pack, motor, and HS code.
  • Submit the dossier via an accredited conformity body.
  • Secure the PCoC and diary its one-year expiry.
  • Cut the SCoC per shipment before berthing.
  • Stage the charger so the truck works on arrival.

Planning depot charging for this EV truck

Charging an electric truck is a depot discipline, not a public-network gamble. A 350 kWh pack working a 260 km day typically returns with 25 to 35 percent in reserve, so overnight demand is about 210 kWh per truck. On a 120 kW DC charger the pack climbs from 20 to 80 percent in roughly 105 minutes; on 240 kW that falls to about 52 minutes, which lets two trucks share one charger on staggered shifts.

Size the site transformer for your busiest shift plus 30 percent headroom, keep cable runs under 30 metres to limit voltage drop, and meter each charger separately so energy cost per truck stays visible. In Saudi Arabia, grid connection lead times of three to nine months are common, so apply for the connection the day you sign the truck order, not the day the ship arrives.
