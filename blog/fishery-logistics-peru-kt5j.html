Fishery Logistics: A Duty Cycle Built for Electric

Fishery supply chains run on short, fixed, time-critical circuits: landing site to ice plant, ice plant to harbor, harbor to processing factory, factory to cold store or port. Distances are typically 20-120 km, routes repeat daily, and vehicles return to a fixed base every night - the exact profile where electric trucks outperform diesel. The loads are time-sensitive but rarely weight-limited, so battery mass costs nothing in payload. And the cargo is unforgiving: fish quality falls measurably for every hour without ice or refrigeration, which makes reliability the purchasing criterion above all others. An electric delivery truck like the KT5J electric delivery truck, with 220 km of certified range and an electric drivetrain with a fraction of diesel's failure points, fits this duty cycle as if designed for it.

Cold Chain Handling: Ice, Insulation and Electric Power

Seafood moves either iced in insulated boxes or mechanically refrigerated - and electric trucks serve both well. Insulated-body work needs no engine power at all, so the electric conversion is seamless; the KT5J's 100 kWh pack powers the drivetrain while ice does the cooling, exactly as diesel practice but at 60-75% lower energy cost. Mechanically refrigerated bodies can run electric transport refrigeration units powered from the vehicle or from standalone battery packs, eliminating the diesel-driven compressors that burn fuel at the dock and in traffic. Either way, the cold chain's critical point - the dockside wait - improves: an electric truck queues silently with zero exhaust around food-grade cargo, and zero fuel burn holding temperature.

Charging at Harbors, Plants and Landing Sites

Fishery charging infrastructure follows the fish. The anchor point is the processing plant or cold store, where three-phase power already runs refrigeration compressors - adding a 60-120 kW DC charger is an extension, not a project. Harbor authorities across Peru are electrifying quays for shore power, and truck charging rides the same investment. Landing sites are the weak link: small sites may lack grid capacity, but fishery logistics rarely needs charging there - trucks charge where they sleep, at the plant or harbor depot. For early-morning landing collection runs, the truck leaves full, collects on schedule, and returns with capacity to spare. In Lima, utility coordination for harbor charging should start early, but the electrical load of a fishery fleet is modest next to the refrigeration it already powers.

Charging Planning for KT5J Fleets

Charging infrastructure decisions made at order time shape fleet economics for a decade. The KT5J supports DC fast charging through a CCS2 inlet, recovering 20-80% of its 100 kWh pack in roughly an hour on a 120 kW unit, while overnight AC or lower-power DC charging covers base load at the cheapest tariff. A sound depot plan matches charger count to shift patterns rather than truck count - fleets running single shifts rarely need more than one fast charger per four to six trucks plus overnight points per bay. Grid connection capacity should be confirmed with the utility before truck delivery, because transformer upgrades carry the longest lead times in the whole project. Fleets in Peru should also model tariff structures: time-of-use pricing rewards overnight charging discipline, and the savings fund the infrastructure faster than most buyers expect.
