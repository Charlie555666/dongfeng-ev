Lima's Clean Air Programme: KT7A Electric Washing Truck for Peru's Municipal Street Fleet

Lima has one of Latin America's most persistent urban air problems — a desert-coast megacity of nearly 10 million where fine dust, traffic emissions and the winter garúa haze combine into a public-health priority that appears in every municipal programme and most donor-financed urban projects. Street washing and flushing is part of the city's formal response: high-pressure washing of avenues and central reservations, dust suppression on the peri-urban access roads, and pre-dawn cleaning of the historic centre and Miraflores-San Isidro tourist corridors. We have covered Peru's mining corridors and fleet electrification; this piece is the municipal electric truck street-cleaning strategy — the KT7A electric washing truck on the heavy washing missions, supported by the KT3F electric sprinkler truck on dust-suppression routes. Country import context is in the Peru market guide.

Two Vehicles, Two Cleaning Missions

Street cleaning splits into two distinct engineering problems, and the fleet should split with them:

MissionVehicleConfigurationTypical Lima route

Dust suppression wateringKT3FCATL 166/200 kWh, 18 t GVW, sprinkler systemPeri-urban access roads and construction corridors

The KT7A is the heavy platform: 6x4 with a 25 t GVW carrying larger water capacity and the washing-system hydraulics for high-pressure jetting, which in a diesel truck means an engine running at governed speed for hours. Electrically driven, the washing pump draws exactly the power the job needs at zero engine idle — the same advantage we documented for Gulf street-washing fleets, now applied to a city that measures its air quality the way others measure traffic.

Economics at Peruvian Tariffs

Peru's industrial electricity is competitively priced at roughly USD 0.10–0.14/kWh — substantially below the Latin American average — while diesel retails at market prices near USD 4.5–5 per gallon equivalent. For a washing truck on Lima duty:

  • Diesel washing truck: fuel-plus-idle costs near USD 0.38/km plus washing-pump fuel share — total energy cost around USD 55–70 per 10-hour shift.
  • KT7A electric: around USD 10–14 per shift at Peruvian industrial tariffs, with the pump duty included.
  • Annual saving at 280 shifts/year: roughly USD 13,000–15,000 per truck, before the maintenance gap (no engine, no clutch, sealed drivetrain on dusty duty) adds another USD 3,000–4,000.

The Air Quality Case

Lima's street-cleaning procurement increasingly lives inside air-quality and climate programmes — municipal decarbonisation commitments, the national framework's clean-air initiatives, and donor-financed urban projects where emissions criteria score explicitly. The electric washing fleet answers on three lines:

  1. Tailpipe zero: the vehicle cleaning the air cannot be adding to the problem — the line item that municipal communications teams love and tender evaluators increasingly score.
  2. Quiet pre-dawn operation: Lima's washing runs 3–7 a.m. in residential central districts; the KT7A's near-silent operation extends the operable window and removes the noise-complaint ceiling on route density.
  3. PM reduction narrative: street washing is itself a PM10 mitigation measure; electrifying the fleet doubles the story — suppression and non-emission in the same programme, reportable in the same monitoring data.

Charging Design for a Municipal Fleet

A district-scale fleet — say eight KT7A and six KT3F — needs a modest depot: three 120 kW DC chargers on rotation for the fleet's daytime recharge (washing fleets charge between shifts and overnight, not during them), one 240 kW fast point for schedule flexibility, and rooftop solar of 60–100 kWp on the municipal depot — Lima's coastal desert location delivers one of the best solar yields of any capital city we serve, and a solar-fed cleaning fleet is a public programme with a visible centrepiece. Total infrastructure budget near USD 200,000–250,000, with the fuel savings of the fourteen-truck fleet repaying it in under two years.

The Tender Structure

Peruvian municipal procurement scores cost per kilometre cleaned and increasingly environmental criteria; the electric bid converts its operating advantage into the price line and its emissions into the score line. Two structural recommendations from our tender practice: bid the charging infrastructure and its solar centrepiece into the proposal as programme infrastructure (evaluators fund what they can see), and structure the fleet delivery in phases matched to the contract's route-expansion schedule — our staged delivery practice — so the programme's cash flow matches the municipality's. Districts in the Lima metro can start with a three-truck KT7A pilot on the tourist and historic corridors, where the visibility of the programme is highest and the route lengths are shortest.
