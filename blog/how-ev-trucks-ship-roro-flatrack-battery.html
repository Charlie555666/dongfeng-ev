How Electric Trucks Ship: RoRo, Flat-Rack and Container Transport for EV Truck Exports

Shipping an electric truck is not like shipping a diesel one, and the differences are all in the battery. A 400–600 kWh lithium pack is a Class 9 dangerous good on the ocean — one that requires the right documents, the right state of charge, and a carrier that will accept it. None of this is difficult when it is prepared correctly, and all of it becomes expensive when it is improvised. This article compares the three shipping methods we use for electric truck exports — using the TZ3Z electric dump truck as the size reference — and explains the battery-specific rules every fleet buyer should understand before signing a supply contract. For most of our orders the decision is geometric, not financial: an 8x4 dump truck with its body raised for shipping may exceed RoRo deck heights and move to flat-rack; a fleet of twenty units to one destination port often sails break-bulk on a geared vessel at a per-unit rate that beats both alternatives. The Battery Rules That Actually Matter

Three requirements govern lithium-battery vehicle shipments by sea:

  • State of charge window: packs ship at a partial charge — the range carriers and insurers accept is roughly 20–40% SoC, low enough to minimise stored energy in a thermal event, high enough to avoid a deep-discharge arrival. We document the shipped SoC in the export file, and the truck's BMS maintains it through transit via its low self-discharge.
  • UN 38.3 test summary: the pack's certification against the UN Manual of Tests and Criteria — vibration, thermal cycling, short-circuit, impact, overcharge and forced-discharge testing. This is a document the carrier's dangerous-goods desk will not waive, and our packs ship with the test summary and the manufacturer's dangerous-goods declaration prepared in advance.
  • Classification and declaration: vehicles powered by lithium batteries ship under UN 3171 (battery-powered vehicle), Class 9, with the declaration made by the shipper — us — and the carrier's acceptance recorded. Fleets importing their own trucks through unfamiliar forwarders hit this as a surprise; through our process it is routine paperwork generated at the factory with the rest of the file.

Transit Protection: What We Specify

Beyond compliance, the physical preparation decides whether the truck arrives as new or arrives as a claim:

  1. HV isolation verified at port gate-out — the service disconnect positioned and checked, with the record in the export file
  2. Charge port sealed and covered — salt air is the enemy of every connector; marine-grade covers go on at the terminal
  3. Coolant loop verified — thermal management fluid level and freeze point documented, so nothing about the first power-up after discharge is a guess
  4. Cab and body sealing — door and body seals checked, desiccant placed in cabs for long transits, battery enclosure breathers confirmed closed
  5. Lashing and chocking per vessel plan — the battery mass sits low, which is favourable for stability, but lashings must be to the vessel's plan with the driver's signature obtained at load for RoRo units

Insurance and the Pre-Delivery Inspection

Marine cargo insurance for battery vehicles rates on the value and the documentation: a clean file with UN 38.3, SoC records and carrier acceptance gets standard rates; an improvised file gets loaded premiums or refusal. On arrival, our discharge protocol applies before the truck ever leaves the port: visual inspection against the shipping photographs, SoC readout from the BMS, charge-port condition, and a function check of the HV interlocks. For clients with their own engineers, the checklist is in the handover documentation; for others, our commissioning team performs it. The pre-delivery inspection is also the point at which the warranty clock is formally set running — a clean record at discharge protects the client's position for the full term.

Cost Planning for Buyers

Order-of-magnitude ocean freight for a single heavy electric truck from a Chinese port: USD 4,500–8,000 RoRo to major destinations depending on route and season; flat-rack rates above that by 15–30%; large consolidated fleets materially cheaper per unit. Add the fixed compliance costs (dangerous-goods handling, documentation, pre-shipment inspection) that barely vary with shipment size — which is why we encourage clients ordering multiple units to consolidate into single shipments wherever their delivery schedule allows. The saving is real, and the paperwork is identical either way: it costs the same to do it right for one truck or for twenty.

Arrival Protocol: From Discharge to First Revenue Kilometre

The shipping article ends at the destination quay, and the fleet's experience begins there — so the arrival protocol deserves its own treatment. Our discharge sequence for electric truck consignments: visual inspection against the pre-shipment photographs while the unit is still in the port area (the lashing points, the body edges, the charge port cover — the places transit writes its story), the BMS state-of-charge readout as the first electrical act (confirming the shipped SoC held through the voyage), the charge-port condition check before the first plug-in, and the HV interlock function check before the first drive. The records from this inspection set the warranty clock formally running and protect both sides' position for the full term — which is why it is performed to a checklist in the handover documentation, and why we encourage the client's own engineer to attend where possible.

The first days after arrival carry a few practical disciplines that cost nothing and return reliability. A full charge on the first night at the depot — not because the battery needs it, but because the charge session's telemetry confirms the thermal and charging systems' post-voyage health in the most honest test there is. A suspension and steering check after the first loaded day, catching any transit-settled fastener before it becomes a wear pattern. And the driver familiarisation session before revenue service — the half-day that covers the regenerative braking feel and the charging routine, and that quietly does more for the fleet's first-month experience than any hardware specification could.

Fleets that follow the protocol start their electric operation with a documented, healthy, familiar fleet; fleets that skip it start with questions. After a six-figure asset has crossed an ocean safely, the discipline of its first week is the cheapest insurance in the entire procurement.
