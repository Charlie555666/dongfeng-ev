Keeping the Chargers Alive: Operations and Maintenance for EV Truck Charging Stations

Fleets obsess over truck uptime and treat chargers as furniture. The arithmetic says otherwise: a truck that cannot charge is a truck that cannot roll, and on a depot with four chargers, each dispenser carries a quarter of the fleet's daily energy. A charger down for two weeks — a common real-world failure — does not merely delay trucks; it forces peak-hour charging, demand-charge spikes and schedule cuts that quietly destroy the TCO model. This piece is the O&M discipline for charging infrastructure: the preventive schedule, the KPIs, the spares strategy and the monitoring that keeps the energy flowing. Our worked example is a port-adjacent depot serving a fleet of TE46 electric tractors — the profile we design for in Saudi and Gulf port operations, where heat and 24/7 duty make charger reliability a first-order design problem.

What Actually Fails on a Charging Station

Charging stations are industrial power electronics in the weather. The failure distribution we see across our clients' depots:

ComponentFailure modeShare of downtimePrimary defence
Connectors and cablesContact wear, pin damage, cable strain~35%Inspection at every session; cycle-count replacement
Cooling systemsFilter clogging, fan failure, coolant leaks~25%Monthly filter regime; heat-driven schedules in hot markets
Power modulesDerating, module failure~20%Thermal monitoring; module-level spare on site
Comms and backendsOCPP session failures, firmware faults~15%Managed updates with rollback; offline charge mode
Civil and electricalPlinth damage, breaker trips, ingress~5%Site discipline; drainage design

The top two rows — connectors and cooling — are nearly two-thirds of all downtime, and both are almost entirely preventable by routine. That is the good news: charging O&M is mostly a checklist, not a mystery.

The Preventive Schedule

Our standard schedule for a fleet DC depot, tuned for hot-climate operation:

  • Every session (driver duty): visual connector check — pins, latch, cable lay; report-and-replace discipline for any damaged connector.
  • Weekly (depot technician): connector contact resistance sampling, cable strain-relief inspection, cabinet filter check, site housekeeping around dispensers.
  • Monthly: filter replacement in hot or dusty markets (quarterly in temperate ones), cooling-system function test, torque check on power connections, thermal-camera scan of cabinets and joints.
  • Quarterly: insulation-resistance testing, protective-relay verification, emergency-stop tests, firmware currency review under the managed-update protocol.
  • Annually: full power-module performance verification against nameplate, coolant replacement per OEM schedule, civil inspection, and a full depot failover drill — grid-loss and charger-loss scenarios executed live.

Executed on schedule, this regime holds fleet charging availability at 98%+ — the level at which charging never appears on the operations report. Executed ad hoc, availability drifts to 92–94%, which is enough to start cutting routes.

The KPIs That Run the Depot

Four numbers, reviewed weekly, tell you whether the charging system is healthy: dispenser availability (target 98%+ monthly, measured in 15-minute intervals, derated output counted as unavailable); session success rate (completed sessions ÷ initiated, target 99%); energy delivered versus plan (the nightly schedule's kWh delivered, which catches soft failures that availability metrics miss — a "working" charger delivering 60% of rated power); and mean time to repair for critical faults (target under 24 hours on-site). Every charging backend we deploy reports all four automatically; the discipline is looking at them weekly and acting on the trend, not the average.

Spares: What Sits on the Shelf

The spares strategy follows the failure table: connectors and cable sets for every dispenser type (cycle-counted consumables — budget them like brake pads), a full filter set for a year of the monthly regime, one power module per four dispensers held on site, cooling fans and pumps for the common cabinet types, and the comm-module spare that turns a dead OCPP session into a 30-minute swap. The on-site spares kit for a four-dispenser depot costs roughly USD 15,000–25,000 — less than the cost of a single two-week dispenser outage on a twenty-truck fleet, which at port duty can run to USD 40,000–60,000 in disrupted operations.

Heat, Dust and the Gulf Case

Hot-climate depots deserve a special note because they concentrate every failure mode: 50 °C ambient halves cooling-filter life, accelerates connector wear through thermal cycling, and pushes power modules toward derating exactly when the fleet needs them most. Our Gulf-specified depots run: monthly (not quarterly) filter regimes, shaded dispenser canopies with positive-pressure cabinet ventilation, connector inspection at every session without exception, and derate-aware scheduling that shifts heavy charging to night hours — which the off-peak tariff structure rewards anyway. Port operations running 24/7 TE46 duty under these rules hold the same 98% availability as temperate depots; the difference is that in the Gulf the discipline is mandatory rather than advisory.

The O&M Operating Model

Who does the work? Three workable models: in-house (a trained depot technician with the schedule above — our technician training programme certifies exactly this), a charger-Vendor service contract (appropriate where the vendor has regional coverage — always with the response-time SLAs from our contract guide), or a hybrid, with routine in-house and module-level repair contracted. What does not work is no model: chargers maintained on complaint, which is how depots drift into the 92% availability zone and the fleet starts designing its schedule around broken infrastructure.

The charger is the fleet's fuel pump, refinery and filling station in one box. Maintain it like the production asset it is, and the trucks never know it exists; neglect it, and every element of the business case pays the price.
