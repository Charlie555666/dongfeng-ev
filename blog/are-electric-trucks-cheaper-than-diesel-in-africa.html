Are Electric Trucks Cheaper Than Diesel in Africa?

Quick answer: Yes, electric trucks are cheaper than diesel in Africa on total cost of ownership for high-mileage duty cycles, typically beating diesel inside 24 to 44 months once you count energy, maintenance, and duty relief, though the higher upfront price means low-mileage users should wait. The break-even depends on kilometres driven, local diesel price, and the available green-import incentives, but for fleets running above 150 km per day the EV truck wins on five-year cost in almost every African market we model.

How Do You Actually Compare the Cost?

The honest comparison is total cost of ownership, not sticker price. A diesel truck looks cheaper on day one because its upfront is 20% to 60% below an equivalent electric truck, but that gap ignores three streams of saving. First, energy: an electric truck consumes roughly 1.0 to 1.4 kWh per km, and at African commercial tariffs of US$0.08 to US$0.20 per kWh that is US$0.08 to US$0.28 per km, against US$0.20 to US$0.35 per km for diesel at 18 to 30 litres per 100 km. Second, maintenance: the LvKong motor has a fraction of the service points of a diesel, cutting scheduled maintenance by 40% to 60%. Third, duty: many African states grant reduced import levies on certified zero-emission vehicles, shaving the landed cost.

Build the model on five-year cost per km, not purchase price, and the electric truck usually wins for any fleet above 150 km per day. Below that, the lower utilisation does not earn back the premium fast enough, and a diesel or a smaller EV class is the rational choice.

What Does the Energy Saving Look Like in Practice?

Take the TZ3V electric dump truck, a 600 kWh mining rigid, and a comparable diesel rigid at a Nigerian or Ghanaian site. The diesel burns 80 to 120 litres per hour under load; the electric version draws from a depot charger at a per-kWh rate that is a fraction of diesel. On a 200 km per day duty the electric truck saves roughly US$10,000 to US$18,000 per year in energy and maintenance alone, against an upfront premium of US$40,000 to US$70,000, recovering inside 30 to 48 months. For lighter duty, the KT5J at 1.1 kWh per km saves US$7,000 to US$11,000 per year and pays back even faster, often inside 24 to 36 months.

The saving scales with kilometres. A fleet running 300 km per day nearly doubles the annual benefit versus a 150 km per day fleet, which is why the highest-mileage African operators, port, mining, and dense-city logistics, adopt first and the occasional user waits.

Which Markets in Africa Favour the EV Truck?

The friendliest markets combine high diesel prices, decent grid at depots, and green-import incentives. South Africa, Nigeria, Kenya, and Morocco lead because they pair established logistics corridors with emerging duty relief on electric vehicles. The Nigeria electric truck market guide shows Apapa and Lagos depot charging, SONCAP clearance, and the duty line that lowers the landed cost of a certified EV truck. Kenya's grid at Nairobi and Mombasa supports depot charging for city fleets, while Morocco's industrial tariff makes the per-km energy cost among the lowest on the continent.

Are Electric Trucks Reliable Enough for African Conditions?

Reliability is the fair sceptic's question, and the answer is improving fast. The CATL LFP pack used across the Dongfeng EV truck range is tolerant of heat and cycle stress, carries an 8-year or 4,500-cycle warranty to 70% state of health, and the LvKong motor has far fewer failure points than a diesel powertrain. Dust and heat are managed with sealed harnesses and pack cooling; the realistic limitation is charger availability on long rural legs, not the truck itself. For return-to-base duty, which is most African commercial freight, the electric truck is as available as diesel and cheaper to keep running.

What to confirm before you order

  • Duty cycle: longest daily route, loaded and empty legs, gradient profile, and ambient temperature range.

  • Payload: body weight versus rated GVW, so the 260 km real range holds with your actual load.
  • Support: local partner for HV work, spare-parts stock level, and response time commitments in writing.
  • Finance: FOB or CFR terms, letter-of-credit conditions, and currency cover for the balance on arrival.
