Gradeability and Regen on Mountain Loops

The TZ3Z climbs a 32 percent grade fully loaded, covering the steepest ramps on Colombian bench works, and its 420 kW motor delivers full torque from a standstill at any altitude, where a turbo-diesel loses power in the thin mountain air. On the loaded descent, regenerative braking recovers 12 to 18 percent of the climb energy, the highest recovery rate in the range because the loops are so steep.

That recovery changes the energy math fundamentally. A 20 kilometer mountain loop with 600 meters of elevation change can net out at the consumption of a much flatter route, and brake wear falls by more than half because the motor does most of the retarding.

Charging at the Mountain Base

The TZ3Z returns to the quarry or plant base each shift, where a 240 kW DC charger restores 20 to 80 percent in about 75 minutes during breaks and overnight. Contractors running double shifts add a second charger and stagger start times. Colombia's hydro-heavy grid keeps electricity cheap and low-carbon, which strengthens both the cost and the sustainability case.

  • Site a 240 kW charger at the quarry or batching plant base.
  • Stagger double-shift starts so one charger serves three trucks.
  • Train drivers to ride regen, not friction brakes, on descents.
  • Track energy per ton and per meter of climb to benchmark loops.
  • Pre-position brake spares; regen extends but does not eliminate wear.

Cost per Ton Versus Diesel

A diesel tipper on mountain duty burns 30 to 35 liters per hour and eats brake linings on the descents, while the TZ3Z's electricity cost per hour is roughly a fifth of the diesel fuel bill at Colombian tariffs, and regen halves brake wear. Across a 2,500-hour mountain season the combined saving is the difference between a marginal and a profitable haulage contract.
