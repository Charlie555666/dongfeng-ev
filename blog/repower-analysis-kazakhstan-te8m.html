What Diesel-to-Electric Conversion Actually Involves

A diesel-to-electric repower strips the engine, gearbox, fuel system and exhaust from an existing truck and installs an electric motor, battery packs, power electronics and a new cooling system. Done well, it preserves the chassis, cab and axles a fleet already owns. Done badly, it produces a truck with no factory warranty, questionable weight distribution and range that disappoints. Conversion shops quote $40,000-$90,000 per truck depending on battery size, plus four to twelve weeks of downtime per unit. The critical comparison is not conversion cost alone but what you get for the money: a converted truck carries the age and wear of its original chassis, while a new electric 6x4 tractor like the TE8M electric 6x4 tractor arrives with a factory-integrated drivetrain, a 400 kWh CATL LFP pack and an 8-year battery warranty.

The True Cost of a Repower Project

Advertised conversion prices rarely survive contact with reality. Beyond the kit and labor, budgets absorb engineering certification, brake and suspension upgrades to handle battery weight, dashboard and control integration, and the inevitable surprises inside a ten-year-old chassis. Fleets in Kazakhstan that have costed both paths typically find a quality conversion landing at 60-80% of the price of a new electric truck - before counting the conversion's weaker resale value and shorter remaining chassis life. Battery Warranty: The Gap That Decides Most Deals

This is where most conversion projects fail the business case. A converted truck's battery typically carries one to three years of limited coverage from the conversion shop, not the cell manufacturer. A new TE8M carries the CATL LFP factory warranty of 8 years or 4,500 full cycles with a guaranteed capacity floor, transferable to second owners. For a fleet in Kazakhstan, that difference is bankable: lenders finance against warranted assets, and residual values follow warranty depth. A converted truck with 24 months of battery cover is nearly unsellable at year five; a factory electric truck with warranty still running retains documented value. When conversion quotes look attractive, ask one question first - who warranties the battery, for how long, and with what capacity guarantee.

Downtime and Fleet Continuity During Conversion

A repower program takes trucks off the road one by one, four to twelve weeks each, which forces fleets to hire replacement capacity or run short. Staggering conversions across a twenty-truck fleet means a year or more of operational disruption. New electric truck delivery follows the opposite rhythm: production and shipping to the Khorgos dry port corridor run five to seven months, but your diesel fleet keeps working until the day the new trucks arrive, and changeover happens in a single commissioning week with driver training included. For revenue-critical fleets in Kazakhstan, continuity often outweighs the theoretical capital saving of conversion. The fleets that convert successfully are those with genuine spare capacity or seasonal windows - everyone else should model the downtime cost honestly before signing.

When Conversion Does Make Sense

Repower has legitimate niches. Specialist bodywork worth more than the truck - custom tippers, expensive crane installations, municipal bodies with years of life - can justify converting the chassis underneath. Very young donor vehicles repossessed or written down can make the arithmetic work. Operators with in-house engineering teams and fabrication capacity cut conversion costs sharply. And in markets where new electric truck imports face long homologation queues, conversion of an already-registered vehicle can shortcut certification. These cases are real but narrow. For standard 6x4 tractor duty on Kazakh roads, the factory route wins on warranty, integration quality, support and resale - which is why most fleets that study both paths end up ordering new.
