KTH3 vs KT5M: Which Dongfeng Electric Cargo Truck Fits Your Fleet?

Choosing between the KTH3 and the KT5M, two Dongfeng electric cargo truck models, comes down to one question every fleet manager asks when electrifying: which electric truck fits the route, how much payload, and on what roads? Both are battery-electric trucks built for urban and regional distribution, but they sit in different weight classes with different battery envelopes. This KTH3 electric cargo truck and KT5M electric cargo truck comparison lays out the numbers so Vietnamese fleet operators can match the right electric truck to the right route rather than guessing on price alone.

Vietnam's logistics landscape mixes dense city cores with long inter-provincial corridors, so a single model rarely fits every lane. The KTH3 leans toward agility and lower capital cost; the KT5M leans toward capacity and range. Understanding where each shines prevents the classic mistake of over-buying a heavy truck for light city work, or under-buying a light truck for regional hauls that strand it on the charger. The sections below break the decision down by the factors that actually move the total cost of ownership.

Payload and Wheelbase

The KTH3 is the lighter, more agile unit aimed at dense city delivery where maneuverability beats brute capacity. The KT5M steps up to a longer wheelbase and heavier GVW, suiting regional box-truck and palletized distribution work. Both ride on LvKong drive motors, but the KT5M draws more from its larger pack to hold speed on open roads and to carry the extra mass without sagging range.

For Hanoi or Ho Chi Minh City last-mile loops with frequent stops, the KTH3's tighter turning circle reduces cycle times and eases curb-side delivery. For Haiphong-to-inland distribution with heavier loads, the KT5M carries the extra tonnage without range penalty and keeps drivers comfortable on the longer seated stretches between drops.

Battery, Range and Charging

Both trucks use CATL LFP chemistry for safety and cycle life, but the KT5M supports a larger pack to cover longer regional legs. Energy consumption sits in the 0.9 to 1.3 kWh per kilometer band for cargo bodies, depending on load, terrain and how aggressively the cab is cooled in Vietnam's humid heat.

Both qualify for overnight depot charging, and either can use a 240 kW DC charger during midday breaks. The larger KT5M pack simply means more delivered range before the break, which is why it suits operators who cannot afford a long midday charge window on a tight delivery schedule.

Body Options and Upfitting

Cargo trucks live or die by their body. Dongfeng supports several factory and approved-upfitter bodies for both platforms, and the right body is often the real productivity lever. A mismatched body wastes the electric truck's efficiency advantage.

  • Box van / dry freight body for e-commerce and retail replenishment.
  • Flatbed and stake body for construction materials and market deliveries.
  • Refrigerated body for pharma and seafood cold chain across Vietnam's coastal and delta logistics network.
  • Curtain-side body for fast palletized loading at distribution centers.
  • Tipper-light variant for light bulk loads on the KTH3 chassis.
  • Dropside body for furniture and appliance distribution with rear-lift tailgates.

Best-Fit Use Cases

Match the truck to the mission rather than buying on price alone. The KTH3 wins where stop-and-go density dominates: parcel delivery, supermarket backhaul, and inner-city freight under low-emission-zone rules. The KT5M wins on heavier, longer runs: inter-provincial distribution, beverage plant delivery, and refrigerated regional corridors where payload pays the bills and range must absorb unpredictable traffic.

Total Cost and Resale View

Beyond specs, operators should weigh the capital gap against utilization. The KTH3's lower FOB in the cargo band (USD 45k to 85k) makes it easy to justify for a single city lane, while the KT5M's higher capacity justifies its price only when the extra payload is actually sold every day. Both carry the same 8-year / 4500-cycle battery cover, which protects resale far better than a worn diesel driveline would.

Charging Depot Design for Mixed Fleets

Operators who run both the KTH3 and the KT5M should design one depot that serves both rather than building two separate yards. The shared CATL LFP chemistry and the same 1.5 to 2 hour fast-charge window mean a single 240 kW DC charger bank can top up either model between runs, and overnight depot charging at 60 to 120 kW per truck covers the typical Vietnamese duty cycle without stress. Consolidation also lets the operator negotiate a better power connection and a single maintenance contract instead of fragmenting spend across vendors and sites, which is a meaningful saving that never appears on a truck spec sheet.

The depot, not the truck, is where most Vietnamese operators quietly win or lose on electric truck total cost. A shared yard reduces idle charger capacity, simplifies the warranty claim path for the common 8-year / 4500-cycle pack, and lets a single energy-management system optimize every vehicle on site. Buyers who treat charging as an afterthought usually discover the mistake in their second year of operating costs, while those who plan the depot up front capture savings from the very first charge cycle.

For mixed fleets the commonality dividend compounds. The same lift gear fits both chassis for battery service, the same coolant and compressor parts cover thermal maintenance, and the same driver training deck applies to both cabs. That is why a KTH3 plus KT5M combination is often cheaper to run than two unrelated brands at similar headline price, and why the right question is never which truck is best in isolation but which pair of trucks best covers the whole network.

Verdict

Neither truck is universally better. The KTH3 is the cost-efficient urban workhorse; the KT5M is the regional heavy hauler. Operators running both profiles should mix the two to right-size each route and maximize the return on every electric truck in the fleet, rather than forcing one model to do a job it was not designed for.
