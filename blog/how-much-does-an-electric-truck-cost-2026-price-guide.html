How Much Does an Electric Truck Cost? 2026 Price Guide by Class

Quick answer: In 2026 an electric truck costs US$45,000 to US$180,000 FOB China depending on class — light delivery US$45k–62k, medium box US$55k–75k, heavy cargo US$70k–98k, dump US$75k–165k, tractor US$68k–145k, heavy-haul US$130k–180k, and mixer US$105k–150k. The sections below break the price down by class with real specs and the landed-cost stack.

Why Does Electric Truck Price Vary So Much by Class?

The dominant cost driver is the battery. A battery-electric truck is, at its core, a pack sized to the duty plus a motor and chassis, and the pack scales almost linearly with payload and range. A 7.5–9 t delivery truck needs roughly 106–130 kWh; a 49 t heavy cargo unit needs 262–350 kWh; a long-haul tractor needs 424–500 kWh. Because CATL LFP cells are the largest single line item, the price ladder tracks battery size first and vehicle mass second. The KT5J electric cargo truck sits at the cheap end; the TE8L electric tractor sits higher because of its 350–424 kWh pack.

Secondary cost drivers are the motor power, the axle configuration (4x2 vs 6x4 vs 8x4), and duty-specific bodies such as mixers and dumps. None moves the price like the pack, but together they explain why two trucks of similar GVW can differ by tens of thousands of dollars.

How Much Does a Light Delivery Electric Truck Cost?

The light delivery class is the entry point and the best value. The KT5J electric cargo truck is a 7.5–9 t platform with a 106–130 kWh CATL LFP pack, a LvKong permanent-magnet motor of 120–180 kW, 180–220 km real-world range, and a 35–60 minute 20–80% DC charge. At US$45,000–62,000 FOB it undercuts every other class and returns its premium fastest on dense city duty because energy and maintenance savings are largest relative to a small sticker.

  • Pack. 106–130 kWh CATL LFP, 8 yr / 4,500-cycle warranty to 70% SOH.
  • Motor. LvKong permanent-magnet, 120–180 kW.

How Much Does a Heavy Electric Tractor Cost?

The heavy tractor class is where long-haul economics live. The TE8L electric tractor is a 6x4 prime mover with a 350–424 kWh CATL LFP pack, a 282–350 kW LvKong motor, 230–280 km loaded range, and a 50–60 minute 20–80% DC charge. At US$95,000–145,000 FOB it is the workhorse for corridor and port haul, and it pairs with the cheaper TE46 terminal tractor (US$68k–95k) for yard duty. The price step from light delivery to heavy tractor is almost entirely the extra ~300 kWh of pack.

What Sits on Top of the FOB Price?

FOB is only the start of the landed cost. A buyer must add ocean freight and insurance (roughly US$3,000–6,000 per unit depending on kit vs finished), import duty and VAT (market-specific, often 0–25% combined on a green line), and a depot charger (US$15,000–60,000 for a 60–350 kW post plus installation). A TE8L landed typically reaches US$110,000–160,000 before the charger line, while a KT5J lands near US$55,000–75,000. The charger is a one-time fleet asset, not a per-truck cost, so it dilutes across the units it serves.

How Does the Price Compare to Diesel Upfront?

An electric truck typically costs 20–60% more upfront than a comparable diesel. The offset is operating cost: a KT5J at ~1.1 kWh/km versus a 9 t diesel at 22 l/100 km saves roughly US$7,000–9,000 per year in energy and maintenance, recovering the premium in 26–42 months at typical duty. A TE8L saves ~US$13,000–18,000 per year, recovering inside 30–44 months. The higher the annual kilometres and diesel price, the faster the price gap closes — which is why the highest-mileage fleets adopt first.

How to Read the Price Without Getting Burned

The FOB number is the easiest figure to compare and the least complete. Buyers who shop on FOB alone miss the duty differential, the freight mode (finished vs CKD kit changes both cost and lead time), and the charger they must buy anyway. The disciplined approach is to request a landed-cost model that includes the green-duty line, the freight, and a shared charger amortised per truck, then rank options on five-year total cost rather than sticker. On that basis the light delivery class wins for city fleets, the heavy tractor wins for corridor haul, and the heavy-haul class wins only where range and tonnage genuinely demand the 500 kWh pack. Paying for pack you do not use is the most common way to over-spend on an electric truck, so spec the battery to the lane and let the price follow.
