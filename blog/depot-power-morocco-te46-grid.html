The Depot's Power Connection Is the Real Infrastructure

Fleet electrification projects succeed or stall at the same unglamorous point: the electrical connection behind the depot fence. Chargers are products, bought and installed in weeks; grid capacity is a process, with utility applications, transformer lead times and civil works measured in months. The fleets that electrify smoothly are the ones that start the power conversation first - before the truck order, before the charger purchase. The arithmetic is encouraging: a working depot for ten to twenty trucks typically needs a connection in the hundreds of kVA to low single-digit MW, well within standard industrial supply in most Moroccan cities. The TE46 electric terminal tractor and its fleetmates charge mostly overnight at managed rates, which keeps the required connection far smaller than the nameplate charger sum suggests. This guide walks the process in the order it actually happens.

Sizing the Connection: The Load Calculation Done Right

The load calculation starts from the fleet's energy appetite, not the chargers' nameplates. Work the example: a TE46 with a 262 kWh pack running 200 km of daily duty returns each evening needing 150-250 kWh depending on how much of the pack the day consumed. Ten such trucks need 1.5-2.5 MWh overnight - spread across an eight-hour managed charging window, that is 200-300 kW of sustained draw, not the megawatt a naive sum of ten 120 kW chargers would suggest. Managed charging software is the multiplier: staggering start times and throttling to finish-by-morning targets keeps the site peak low and the transformer modest. The demand figure that goes on the utility application should come from this modeling - fleets that apply for the nameplate sum pay for capacity they never use, in some markets for years.

The Utility Process: Applications, Studies and Lead Times

Utility connection processes in Moroccan markets follow a recognizable sequence, and knowing it sets realistic expectations. Application: load requirement, site details, intended use - the load calculation from proper modeling makes this credible and fast. Technical study: the utility assesses local network capacity; where reinforcement is needed, costs and timelines are quoted - this is the stage where lead times of three to twelve months appear, and why the process starts before the truck order. Connection agreement and construction: civil works, transformer installation, metering. Commissioning: energization and tariff activation. The practical accelerants: engage a local electrical contractor experienced with the utility, present the managed-charging load profile - utilities approve modest, well-managed loads faster - and where Casablanca has EV infrastructure programs, use them; several Moroccan utilities now offer streamlined EV fleet connection tracks.

Inside the Fence: Switchgear, Chargers and Layout

The depot's internal electrical design converts the connection into charging capacity, and layout decisions here are expensive to revisit. The architecture: main switchgear at the connection point, distribution to charging positions, and charger placement where trucks actually park - the overnight line, not a special charging bay that costs shunting time every day. Cable runs favor clustered layouts; canopy structures for Moroccan sun or rain protection combine naturally with the solar option. Sizing inside the fence should exceed day-one needs modestly - conduit and switchgear capacity for the fleet you plan at year three costs little at construction and much at retrofit. The TE46's charging interface is the DC fast-charging standard in its class; charger procurement then becomes a commercial comparison of reputable suppliers rather than an engineering risk, and our team supplies the vehicle-side charging specifications to your electrical designer.

Resilience: Keeping the Fleet Charged When the Grid Blinks

Grid reliability is a fair question in many Moroccan markets, and the resilient depot design answers it in layers. First layer: the fleet's own buffer - trucks charged overnight sit ready through daytime outages, and a fleet charging to full carries a day or more of operational reserve in its batteries. Second layer: backup generation sized to the fleet's minimum daily energy need - not the full charging peak - keeps operations alive through extended outages at modest capital cost. Third layer: solar with storage, which in the Morocco climate turns the depot roof and canopy into a partial energy supply independent of grid conditions. The planning principle is matching resilience investment to the duty's criticality: a municipal fleet serving Casablanca mornings justifies more backup than a flexible commercial operation. We model outage scenarios with fleets at the design stage - the answer is usually cheaper than the anxiety.

Costing the Connection: What to Budget

Connection costs vary widely with site and utility, but the structure is predictable and the numbers are plannable. Budget lines: utility connection and any network reinforcement contribution - the most variable item, from modest to significant depending on local capacity; transformer and switchgear - standard industrial equipment with standard pricing; internal cabling and civil works - layout-dependent; chargers - the visible item, and usually not the largest; and design and project management. Across Moroccan fleet projects, total depot electrical infrastructure for a ten-to-twenty truck fleet typically runs from the low tens to low hundreds of thousands of dollars - against which the fleet's energy savings of $2,000-$5,000 per truck per month on hard duty provide the payback context. At an FOB range of $95,000-$120,000, the TE46 fleet's infrastructure is a rounding error in the five-year TCO - but only if it is started early. Deployment Timeline from Order to Operation

Realistic scheduling prevents the most common fleet electrification failures. A typical TE46 deployment runs five to seven months end to end: production takes 30-60 days depending on configuration and order size; ocean freight to Tanger Med Port adds 25-45 days; customs clearance and homologation steps add two to six weeks depending on Moroccan procedures. Running in parallel, the depot charging installation should complete before the trucks land, and driver and technician training should finish during commissioning week. The sequence that works: confirm specifications and sign the contract, start certification paperwork immediately, order chargers at the same time as the trucks, and book training against the vessel arrival date. Fleets that run these tracks in parallel put trucks into revenue service within days of clearance; fleets that run them sequentially lose months. Driver Acceptance and Daily Operation

Driver acceptance decides whether an electric fleet reaches its modeled savings, and the TE46 earns acceptance quickly. The 282 kW motor delivers instant torque with no gear hunting, the cab is quieter than any diesel equivalent, and regenerative braking reduces fatigue in stop-start duty. Drivers typically adapt within a week, and most prefer the electric truck once they experience a full shift without engine noise and vibration. The habits that matter are small: smooth acceleration, anticipation to maximize regeneration, charging connection on return to depot, and honest reporting of dash warnings. Fleets in Morocco report the fastest adoption when they involve senior drivers in the pilot phase and let peer experience do the convincing, rather than imposing the change top-down. Energy consumption per driver is visible in the telematics data, and a little friendly competition between drivers on efficiency measurably improves fleet-wide consumption in the first quarter.

Charging Planning for TE46 Fleets

Charging infrastructure decisions made at order time shape fleet economics for a decade. The TE46 supports DC fast charging through a CCS2 inlet, recovering 20-80% of its 262 kWh pack in roughly an hour on a 120 kW unit, while overnight AC or lower-power DC charging covers base load at the cheapest tariff. A sound depot plan matches charger count to shift patterns rather than truck count - fleets running single shifts rarely need more than one fast charger per four to six trucks plus overnight points per bay. Grid connection capacity should be confirmed with the utility before truck delivery, because transformer upgrades carry the longest lead times in the whole project. Fleets in Morocco should also model tariff structures: time-of-use pricing rewards overnight charging discipline, and the savings fund the infrastructure faster than most buyers expect.
