Why used electric trucks are gaining value in 2026

Two years ago the used market for electric trucks barely existed. In 2026 that has changed: growing fleets, better battery diagnostics and rising diesel costs mean a three- to five-year-old TZ5E now sells for a meaningful share of its original price in Tanzania. The reason is simple — the expensive part, the 350 kWh battery, is covered by an 8-year warranty and its health can be proven with a data log rather than guessed. That transparency is exactly what used-vehicle buyers were missing, and it is why resale values are firming up.

How battery health data sets the resale price

On an electric truck the resale price is essentially a function of three things: age, mileage, and battery state of health. A TZ5E with 3 years of service, moderate mileage and 90% SOH is worth far more than an identical truck with an unknown pack. For Tanzanian sellers in Dar es Salaam, Dodoma and Arusha, the practical advice is to keep a continuous BMS log and to commission an independent SOH test before listing. A clean data sheet can add several percentage points to the sale price and shortens the time to sell dramatically.

Which models hold value best

Demand is strongest for trucks that do a clear, repeatable job. In Tanzania, TZ5E electric dump trucks used on fixed routes with predictable daily distances resell fastest, because the buyer can immediately size the truck to their own duty cycle. Specialised or over-specified electric trucks are harder to move. The lesson for buyers is to choose the configuration that matches the widest possible set of future tasks rather than a single niche, which protects resale value from the start.

  • Zero tailpipe emissions: compliant with low-emission zones in Dar es Salaam, Dodoma and Arusha.
  • Lower energy cost: electricity in Tanzania is far cheaper per km than diesel.
  • Less maintenance: no engine oil, filters or aftertreatment service.

Preparing an electric truck for resale

A truck that is being sold should be presented the way it will be bought: with evidence. That means a full service history, a recent BMS health report, a tyre and brake condition report, and a clean fault-code history. Cosmetic preparation matters less than documentation here. For Tanzania fleets replacing part of a TZ5E fleet, we help arrange the transfer of the remaining warranty entitlement, which is a genuine value line in the listing.

The secondary market for batteries and components

Even at end of vehicle life, the 350 kWh pack has value. A pack that has fallen below 70% SOH for traction duty still holds 60-80% of its original capacity and is attractive for stationary storage, solar buffering or forklift duty. In Tanzania, where depot solar is expanding, there is a real market for second-life packs. That residual value underwrites the whole resale picture and is a reason electric trucks depreciate more gracefully than many buyers expect.

Buying used: a due-diligence checklist

For fleets in Dar es Salaam, Dodoma and Arusha considering a used TZ5E, the checklist is short but non-negotiable: verify the VIN against the original export documents, obtain the BMS log, run an independent SOH test, inspect the HV connectors and coolant loop, and confirm the remaining warranty. A truck that passes all five is a low-risk purchase; one that fails any of them should be priced accordingly. Done properly, used electric trucks offer some of the best value in the heavy-vehicle market today.

Why Tanzania is ready for electric trucks now

The conditions that make an electric truck viable are all present in Tanzania. heavy diesel dependence on the Central Corridor. Freight demand concentrates in and around Dar es Salaam, Dodoma and Arusha, where routes are short and predictable and the depot is never far away. Fuel logistics add cost and delay that a fleet charging its own vehicles simply avoids. For a TZ5E electric dump truck on a corridor freight to Zambia and the DRC duty cycle, this is not an experimental technology but a practical replacement for diesel, and the operators moving first are the ones who lock in the lowest cost per kilometre before their competitors do.

A step-by-step implementation plan

Electrifying a fleet in Tanzania is a project, and running it in phases is what keeps it manageable. Phase one is a site and route audit: list every duty cycle, measure real daily distance and load, and map the depot power supply. Phase two is a pilot of two to five TZ5E units on the most predictable routes, with chargers installed and drivers trained. Phase three is measurement — energy cost per kilometre, uptime, maintenance hours. Phase four is scaling what the data supports. Each phase de-risks the next and keeps the capital commitment matched to proven performance rather than optimism.

Choosing the right configuration

Within the TZ5E range there are choices that matter more than cosmetics. The 6x4 layout suits the axle load and traction pattern of your duty cycle. Battery capacity of 350 kWh should be sized to the longest realistic daily route plus a safety margin, not to the biggest number on the brochure. Body and equipment specification — tipper, box, reefer, tank or compactor — should be matched precisely to the job in Tanzania. Getting these four decisions right at order stage is far cheaper than modifying a truck after it lands.
