Electric Truck Fleet Demand Charges: Peak Shaving Guide

For any fleet running an electric truck depot, the electricity bill is not just energy times tariff; it is energy plus demand charges, and demand charges are where unprepared operators get surprised. A KT5M electric cargo truck charging at the wrong moment can spike a depot's peak draw and inflate the bill for the whole month. This guide explains how peak shaving, staggered charging, and battery-buffered depots cut EV truck electricity costs by 20 to 40 percent, written for buyers in the Thailand electric truck market where commercial tariffs penalize peaks sharply.

What Demand Charges Actually Are

Many commercial electricity tariffs bill not only for the kilowatt-hours you consume but for the highest 15-minute average power you draw in the billing period. That peak sets a demand charge that you pay whether you hold it for minutes or the whole month. A fleet that plugs in every truck at the end of a shift creates a sharp, brief spike that defines the month's bill. The energy may be cheap, but the demand penalty is not, and it is the part most first-time EV fleets miss.

Staggered Charging

The simplest fix is staggering. Instead of charging all trucks the instant they return, you sequence them across the night so only a few draw power at once. A depot with ten KT5M trucks on 336 kWh packs does not need ten simultaneous fast chargers; it needs a managed queue that keeps peak draw flat. Staggered charging alone typically cuts demand charges by 15 to 25 percent with no extra hardware beyond a controller.

Peak Shaving with Managed Charging

Peak shaving means actively limiting depot draw to a ceiling you choose, slowing or pausing charging when other site loads push toward the limit. A smart controller watches the whole site, not just the trucks, and throttles chargers to keep the monthly peak low. For the Thailand electric truck market, where air-conditioning and process loads already create daytime peaks, folding truck charging into a site-wide shaving plan is the efficient move.

Battery-Buffered Depots

A battery-buffered depot adds a stationary storage pack that charges when grid draw is cheap and discharges to feed the trucks during peaks. The trucks never see the grid spike because the buffer absorbs it. This pushes savings toward 40 percent and also provides backup during outages, a real benefit in regions with unreliable supply. The buffer can be retired EV packs, giving a second life to warranty-expired modules and lowering the cost further.

  • Audit your tariff: find the demand charge clause before you size chargers.
  • Sequence charging with a controller; never let all trucks start at once.
  • Set a site power ceiling and let software throttle around it.
  • Consider a buffer pack if demand charges exceed roughly 30 percent of the bill.

Why the KT5M Fits This Model

The KT5M electric cargo truck with its 336 kWh pack is an overnight-charging vehicle by nature, which is the friendliest profile for demand management. A depot can fill the KT5M slowly across off-peak hours, avoiding fast-charge spikes entirely. Fleets that pair the KT5M with a shaving plan often find their per-truck energy cost lands well below the diesel equivalent even before counting maintenance savings.

Solar and the Thailand Context

In the Thailand electric truck market, high solar irradiation makes a solar-plus-buffer depot compelling. Charge the buffer from the sun by day, discharge to trucks by night, and the demand charge all but disappears. The 20 to 40 percent saving range is conservative for sites that combine staggered charging with on-site generation, and it is the difference between an EV business case that works and one that limps.

Reading Your Electricity Contract

Before buying a single charger, obtain the tariff schedule and find the demand clause. Some contracts bill demand as the single highest interval; others use a rolling twelve-month peak that is hard to reset. The structure decides your strategy. Where the peak is a single month, you can occasionally spike for an emergency; where it is rolling, you must never spike at all. This distinction alone changes whether battery buffering pays for itself.

A Worked Depot Example

Consider a depot with eight KT5M trucks returning at 18:00. If all plug in at once on a 120 kW feed, peak draw hits nearly 1 MW for an hour, setting a brutal demand charge. Sequence the same eight across four hours and peak draw falls to about 240 kW, an eighth of the spike, with no truck short of charge by morning. The controller costs almost nothing; the saving on the demand line pays for the solar or buffer add-on within a year or two.

Negotiating With the Utility

Many utilities offer EV or fleet tariffs with lower demand rates in exchange for managed charging. Fleet buyers should ask specifically for these before signing, because the default small-business tariff is rarely the best fit for a charging depot. A half-day of negotiation with the local provider can cut the effective demand charge by a third, which flows straight to the bottom line of every KT5M electric cargo truck in the fleet.

Buffer Sizing Math

A buffer pack should cover the gap between your site's natural load and your chosen ceiling for the few peak hours. For a depot drawing 200 kW of base load and a 400 kW ceiling, a 200 kW, two-hour buffer covers most events. Retired EV modules make this cheap, and the buffer also rides through short outages that would otherwise strand trucks. Size it to the worst credible hour, not the average, and it pays back through both demand savings and uptime.

Monitoring and Continuous Improvement

Install sub-metering on the chargers so the demand picture is visible, not assumed. Fleets that watch the curve find stray loads, mis-set timers, and opportunities to shave further. The Thailand electric truck market rewards exactly this discipline, where commercial tariffs make every avoided kilowatt of peak worth real money across a large fleet.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

The first mistake is sizing chargers to simultaneous peak instead of sequenced duty, which wastes capital and spikes demand. The second is ignoring air-conditioning and process loads when setting the ceiling, so the truck charge quietly pushes the site over. The third is treating the buffer as optional when demand charges already exceed 30 percent of the bill. Avoid these three and most depots land in the 30 to 40 percent saving band without heroics.

Phasing the Investment

You do not need the full system on day one. Start with staggered charging, which is software only and recovers 15 to 25 percent. Add managed peak shaving next, then a buffer if the bill still stings. Each step is independently justified, so the depot improves cash flow from the first week rather than waiting for a large upfront build. This phased path suits fleets that want proof before they spend.

The operational rule is simple: treat the depot's peak draw as a cost you control, not a fact you accept. Stagger first, shave second, buffer third, and only then size chargers. Done right, the electricity line on a KT5M fleet drops by a fifth to two-fifths, and the KT5M electric cargo truck becomes not just cleaner but clearly cheaper to run than the diesel it replaces.
