Vehicle-to-Grid for Fleets: Turning Your EV Truck Into a Revenue Asset

A diesel truck earns money exactly one way: by hauling freight. An electric truck can earn two ways — hauling freight and selling energy back to the grid. This is vehicle-to-grid, V2G: bidirectional charging that lets a parked EV truck with a 262–600 kWh CATL pack act as a grid-connected battery, absorbing cheap power overnight and discharging it during expensive peak hours, or standing ready as reserve capacity the grid pays to have available. A single TE8M with a 600 kWh pack holds more energy than a dozen household batteries; a depot of fifteen of them is a 9 MWh power station that happens to haul containers by day. This article explains the technology, the revenue streams, the battery-wear question every fleet manager asks first, and — honestly — which duty cycles and markets make V2G real money and which make it a slide in a conference deck.

How V2G Works: The Hardware and Protocol Stack

  • Bidirectional chargers. Standard DC fast chargers are one-way. V2G requires bidirectional units — power electronics rated to export at meaningful power (typically 20–100 kW per truck in fleet applications; the full pack is not delivered all at once, nor does it need to be).
  • ISO 15118. The international standard that lets the vehicle and the charging infrastructure negotiate — including the "Plug and Charge" authentication and the bidirectional energy exchange messaging. It is the protocol layer that turns a truck into a grid participant rather than an appliance.
  • The aggregator. Grid markets buy flexibility in megawatt blocks, not per-vehicle. An aggregator contracts with the fleet, stacks its depot's discharge capability into a marketable bloc, and splits the revenue. The fleet's dispatch software simply promises availability windows; the aggregator handles the market interface.
  • The fleet's own dispatch brain. The truck telemetry already knows every pack's state of charge and tomorrow's route plan. V2G scheduling is an overlay: promise to the grid only the energy the morning's duty can spare.

Peak shaving deserves emphasis because it is the entry point almost every fleet should take first: in markets where commercial bills carry demand charges (billing by peak kilowatt draw), discharging trucks during the depot's own peak windows flattens the site's measured demand — savings that require no aggregator, no market enrolment, and no contract lawyer, just bidirectional chargers and scheduling software.

The Math: What a Depot Fleet Can Earn

Modelling a 15-truck depot (mixed TE tractors and TZ tippers, average 400 kWh per pack) in a market with USD 0.10 off-peak / USD 0.28 peak spread, participating in arbitrage on weekdays at 50% depth of the available energy:

  • Energy traded per truck-day: ~200 kWh (round-trip efficiency ~85% nets ~170 kWh delivered)
  • Gross arbitrage per truck-day: ~USD 13–20; annualised at 250 days: USD 3,200–5,000 per truck
  • Depot potential (15 trucks): USD 48,000–75,000 per year
  • Plus availability payments where capacity markets exist: often USD 1,000–3,000 per truck-year for enrolling capacity that is rarely actually dispatched
  • Plus demand-charge savings from self-peak-shaving: highly site-specific, frequently USD 10,000–40,000 per year for a depot with a spiky load profile

Set against the bidirectional charger premium (roughly 30–60% over one-way DC units of the same class), the business case closes inside the pack's warranty window in the right market — and never closes in a flat-tariff one. Which is the entire strategic point: V2G is a market-structure play, not a universal feature.

The Battery-Wear Question, Answered Straight

Every fleet manager asks the same first question: doesn't cycling the pack for the grid eat the warranty I'm counting on? The honest engineering answer:

  1. Cycle depth is what ages packs, not cycle count alone. Shallow cycles in the middle of the state-of-charge window (roughly 30–70%) age LFP chemistry far more gently than the deep cycles of driving duty. A V2G program that holds trucks between those bands adds wear at a small fraction of the equivalent full cycles the label suggests.
  2. Count the arithmetic both ways. The Dongfeng range's 8-year / 4,500-cycle warranty covers driving duty with enormous headroom for most fleets — a city truck using one cycle per working day consumes barely a third of it. V2G consumes the slack deliberately: revenue from cycles the fleet wasn't going to use anyway.
  3. Thermal behaviour matters more than cycling. Grid discharge at moderate power warms the pack less than highway hauling. The CATL pack's liquid cooling barely notices a V2G event.
  4. Contract the wear question explicitly. Where V2G revenue is material, warranty terms should be negotiated to state how grid-discharge cycles are counted. Get it in writing; it is the difference between an asset and a dispute.

Which Duty Cycles Make V2G Real

  • Strong: night-parked day-freight fleets. City delivery and distribution trucks (KT5M/KT5J-class) park from ~20:00 to ~05:00 — the entire high-value evening peak and overnight arbitrage window — with packs half-full and morning routes that can spare 30–40% of the energy. This is the natural V2G fleet.
  • Strong: depot-parked terminal and drayage fleets. Port shuttle tractors (TE46-class) run structured shifts and park at a depot the fleet owns — one site, one connection, one negotiation with the aggregator.
  • Moderate: construction tippers and mixers. Seasonal and weather-gapped duty leaves packs parked at high SoC for long stretches — good for capacity-market availability payments, harder for daily arbitrage.
  • Weak: continuous-operation mining and line-haul fleets. Trucks that run two shifts or charge opportunistically around the clock have no reliable parked-energy window to sell. These fleets should skip V2G and spend the money on swap or buffer storage instead.

Where the Markets Are

Geography decides the business case more than hardware does. Europe leads: capacity markets, aggressive evening peak pricing driven by solar-duck curves, and regulatory frameworks that increasingly treat depot fleets as grid resources. Australia and parts of North America follow with high tariff spreads. Emerging markets are more interesting than they look: unreliable grids invert the value proposition — a truck fleet that can power its own depot through outages (or sell backup power to an adjacent factory) captures value that no tariff structure measures but every operator understands. In those markets the "revenue" line is actually avoided downtime, which needs no aggregator to bank.

Buyer's Checklist

  1. Audit your tariff structure first: no spread, no business — check peak/off-peak spreads and demand-charge rules before buying hardware.
  2. Verify bidirectional capability in the charging specification at procurement; retrofitting later is the expensive path.
  3. Insist on ISO 15118 in the charger and vehicle communication spec — it is the interop layer that keeps you aggregator-independent.
  4. Negotiate the warranty's grid-cycle accounting before enrolling, not after the first claim.
  5. Start with behind-the-meter peak shaving — the no-contract entry point — and graduate to market enrolment once the depot's energy behaviour is proven.

The Contract Checklist Before You Enrol

Before any aggregator signature, put five items in writing on both sides: the availability windows you are promising (and the penalty structure when a truck's route overruns — the classic failure mode is a grid commitment colliding with a freight surge); the depth-of-discharge floor protecting your morning dispatch energy; how grid-discharge cycles are counted against the battery warranty; the revenue split and settlement frequency; and the exit terms, because fleet operations change and a V2G contract that cannot flex with them is a liability. Fleets that negotiate these five points up front treat V2G as what it should be — a low-effort second revenue stream on assets that already earn. Fleets that skip the negotiation discover what an availability penalty feels like the week their biggest customer doubles its order.
