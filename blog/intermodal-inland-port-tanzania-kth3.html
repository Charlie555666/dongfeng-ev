Why Intermodal Shuttles Are the First Duty to Electrify

Intermodal work - shuttling containers and swap bodies between port, rail terminal and nearby distribution centers - is the single best first application for electric trucks, and experienced fleet buyers know it. The duty cycle is everything electrification wants: short legs of 10-60 km, predictable routes, return-to-base patterns, and hours of queuing where a diesel burns fuel at idle while an electric truck simply waits. The KTH3 electric cargo truck turns those queuing hours into zero-energy hours, and its 300 km of certified range covers a full shift of terminal shuttles with margin. Operators at major gateways report energy cost per container move falling 60-70% against diesel yard tractors, with the bonus that drivers finish shifts without a day of diesel idle noise and vibration.

Mapping the Duty Cycle: Moves, Not Miles

Intermodal productivity is measured in moves per shift, not kilometers, and that reframing matters when specifying an electric truck. A terminal tractor or shuttle cargo truck in Dar es Salaam typically completes 20-40 container moves per shift, accumulating only 80-150 km but working nine to ten hours. The energy math follows: at 1.2-1.8 kWh per kilometer in stop-start yard duty with frequent lifting of fifth-wheel loads, a full shift consumes 120-200 kWh - comfortably inside the KTH3's 262 kWh pack with reserve for peak season surges. The planning discipline is to meter actual moves with telematics for the first month, confirm the energy-per-move figure for your terminal layout, and only then finalize fleet size. Guesswork over-specifies the fleet; data right-sizes it.

Charging Strategy for Terminal Operations

Terminal charging differs from depot trucking in one respect: opportunity windows are built into the work. Shift changes, meal breaks and vessel gaps give 30-45 minute windows where a DC fast charger returns meaningful range - a 120 kW charger adds roughly 120 km to the KTH3 in a 40-minute break, which covers an entire additional shift block. Most intermodal operators settle on a hybrid: overnight slow charging to full, plus one or two opportunity sessions timed to natural work pauses. Infrastructure sits inside the terminal fence, which sidesteps public charging queues entirely and lets the operator negotiate industrial tariffs directly with the utility. In Tanzania, we help fleets model charger count against move volumes - the ratio that matters is chargers per truck at peak, not chargers per truck on paper.

The Economics: Cost per Container Move

The intermodal business case runs on cost per move, and here the electric advantage is structural. A diesel yard tractor or shuttle truck burns 4-8 liters per hour in typical terminal duty - much of it at idle or low-speed creep - costing $6-$12 per operating hour in fuel alone. The KTH3 performs the same hour of work on 8-14 kWh, which at industrial electricity tariffs runs $1-$2.50. Across a two-shift operation moving 30 containers per shift, the difference compounds to five figures per truck per year, before maintenance savings - no engine idling means no idle-hours maintenance schedule, and regenerative braking means brake wear measured in years. At an FOB range of $75,000-$95,000, payback against a diesel equivalent typically lands inside three years on two-shift duty, faster where diesel is taxed or subsidized electricity is available.

Specifying an Intermodal Truck: What Matters Most

Specification for intermodal duty prioritizes differently than highway work. Fifth-wheel height and oscillation must match terminal trailer fleets; turning circle matters more than top speed; cab access - steps, handles, door width - decides driver fatigue across 60-plus daily entries and exits; and visibility around the fifth wheel is a safety specification, not a comfort one. Battery sizing follows the shift pattern: the KTH3's 262 kWh pack covers double-shift duty with opportunity charging, which is usually the right answer versus oversizing the pack for a theoretical worst day. Confirm TBS PVoC certification paperwork early, because terminal equipment sometimes falls under different rules than road vehicles in Tanzanian jurisdictions. Deployment Timeline from Order to Operation

Realistic scheduling prevents the most common fleet electrification failures. A typical KTH3 deployment runs five to seven months end to end: production takes 30-60 days depending on configuration and order size; ocean freight to the Port of Dar es Salaam adds 25-45 days; customs clearance and homologation steps add two to six weeks depending on Tanzanian procedures. Running in parallel, the depot charging installation should complete before the trucks land, and driver and technician training should finish during commissioning week. The sequence that works: confirm specifications and sign the contract, start certification paperwork immediately, order chargers at the same time as the trucks, and book training against the vessel arrival date. Fleets that run these tracks in parallel put trucks into revenue service within days of clearance; fleets that run them sequentially lose months.
