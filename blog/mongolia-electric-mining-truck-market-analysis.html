Mongolia Electric Mining Truck Market Analysis: Coal, Cold and the Case for EVs

Mongolia's mining economy, built on the Oyu Tolgoi copper-gold complex and the Tavan Tolgoi coal basin, is one of the most demanding environments on earth for a truck, and that is exactly why the electric mining truck is gaining ground. Long, repetitive pit and haul cycles, altitude, and winters that drop below minus 40 degrees Celsius make the EV dump truck a strong total-cost play despite the cold. This market analysis covers the fleet drivers, the cold-weather reality, and why the TZ3V electric dump truck and its heavy siblings fit. The Fleet Drivers: Oyu Tolgoi and Tavan Tolgoi

Oyu Tolgoi, operated near the Gobi, runs underground and open-pit haul with a steady appetite for mid-size rigid trucks on fixed loops. Tavan Tolgoi and the wider South Gobi coal fields move enormous tonnage over short, repeatable distances that are ideal for battery-electric haul because the route is predictable and the depot charger sits at the pit rim. In both cases the duty cycle is the friend of the EV truck: high daily kilometres on a known loop, return-to-base charging, and no need for long-haul range. A 600 kWh rigid on a 5 to 8 km haul cycle can run a full shift on one or two charges and never leaves the mine.

The economic pull is diesel displacement. A diesel rigid in this class burns 80 to 120 litres per hour under load; at Mongolian and imported fuel prices that is a heavy operating line that an electric truck removes almost entirely, replacing it with a per-kWh charge cost that is a fraction of the diesel spend.

Cold Weather: The Real Constraint

Minus 40 degrees is the headline risk for any battery. LFP chemistry, used across the Dongfeng EV dump range, tolerates cold better than many alternatives, but range and charge speed still fall. The mitigation is thermal management: pre-condition the pack while grid-connected, park in heated or insulated shelters where possible, and size the pack with winter margin. Charging and Power at the Mine

Mine sites already carry heavy power for processing, so adding a 350 to 1,000 kW depot charger is usually a feeder upgrade rather than a new connection. Battery-swap, at 5 to 6 minutes for the pack, is an option where a shift cannot afford charge downtime, letting one spare pack serve several trucks. The decisive factor is the loop length: under 10 km, charge or swap at the rim; over 20 km, the economics tilt toward larger packs or a mixed fleet.

  • Size the pack to the loop plus winter margin.
  • Pre-condition while grid-connected each shift.
  • Shelter packs in heated bays below minus 20C.
  • Stage rim charging at 350 kW plus for fast turns.
  • Model diesel displacement not sticker price.

Planning depot charging for this EV truck

Charging an electric truck is a depot discipline, not a public-network gamble. A 350 kWh pack working a 260 km day typically returns with 25 to 35 percent in reserve, so overnight demand is about 210 kWh per truck. On a 120 kW DC charger the pack climbs from 20 to 80 percent in roughly 105 minutes; on 240 kW that falls to about 52 minutes, which lets two trucks share one charger on staggered shifts.

Size the site transformer for your busiest shift plus 30 percent headroom, keep cable runs under 30 metres to limit voltage drop, and meter each charger separately so energy cost per truck stays visible. In Kazakhstan, grid connection lead times of three to nine months are common, so apply for the connection the day you sign the truck order, not the day the ship arrives. Energy cost per kilometre: EV truck versus diesel

A comparable diesel heavy electric truck burns about 0.38 litres per kilometre in mixed duty; at $0.95 per litre that is roughly $0.36 per km. The electric equivalent consumes about 1.15 kWh per km fully loaded; at an industrial tariff of $0.10 per kWh the cost is near $0.12 per km, and overnight or solar-coupled charging can push it below $0.08. Over 70,000 km a year the saving is about $17,000 per truck, before any carbon or low-emission-zone benefit.

The gap widens in stop-start duty, where regenerative braking returns 15 to 25 percent of traction energy instead of burning it as brake dust. In Kazakhstan, where diesel quality and price both fluctuate, locking in an electricity tariff also removes a major budgeting variable from the fleet P&L.
