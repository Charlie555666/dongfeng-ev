Solar Depot Charging for Electric Truck Fleets in Jordan

Why a Solar Depot Makes the EV Truck Cheaper in Jordan

A solar-powered EV truck depot turns Jordan's 300 sunny days into the cheapest fuel in freight. The KT5J electric cargo truck is a 4x2 hauler with a 350 kWh CATL LFP pack and a LvKong motor up to 360 kW, suited to the distribution and municipal duty that Jordan fleets run. When the depot roof carries photovoltaics and a storage buffer, the per-kilometer energy cost drops below the grid rate and the fleet gains immunity from any tariff rise.

Jordan imports most of its energy, so every solar kilowatt hour charged into a truck is a kilowatt hour not bought abroad. For a fleet of twenty KT5J units, a well-sized solar depot can cover 40 to 60 percent of annual charging from the roof alone, and close to 100 percent in the long summer days.

Sizing the Photovoltaic Array

The depot must size PV to the fleet's daily draw, not the peak. A KT5J on distribution duty uses about 180 kWh per day; twenty trucks use 3,600 kWh. In Jordan a watt of PV yields roughly 5 kWh per year, so covering half the load needs about 360 kW of array. The table below shows the sizing ladder for common fleet sizes.

Storage Buffer and Charge Control

Solar peaks at midday, but trucks charge at night, so a stationary buffer bridges the gap. A 500 kWh to 1 MWh lithium buffer charged by PV stores the midday surplus and releases it through the 240 kW DC chargers after dark. That design keeps the fleet off the peak grid and lifts self-consumption above 85 percent.

  • Size the buffer to one night of fleet draw for full island mode.
  • Charge trucks on a staggered timer so the buffer never hits empty.
  • Use the 240 kW DC unit per 10 trucks to keep charge time under 2 hours.
  • Export surplus to the grid only after the buffer and fleet are full.
  • Monitor per-truck state of charge to balance the load.

The buffer also guards against grid events, which matter in a country that values energy security. A depot that can charge its fleet for a day on stored sun is a depot that never stops for a power cut.

Energy Math for the KT5J

The KT5J draws 180 kWh for a typical 150 km distribution day. At the Jordan night grid rate near 0.06 USD per kWh that is 10.80 USD; on solar it is near zero after the array is paid off. Against a diesel box at 18 liters per 100 km and 0.90 USD fuel, the diesel costs 24.30 USD for the same day, so the electric truck saves 13.50 USD daily, or about 4,000 USD per truck per year before maintenance.

Add the maintenance saving of 2,500 USD per year and the KT5J returns roughly 6,500 USD annually per unit. Over the 20-truck fleet that is 130,000 USD per year, which pays the solar depot within a few years and then runs as pure margin for the rest of the asset life.

Operation, Maintenance and Cleaning the Array

A solar depot is low-maintenance but not no-maintenance. Jordan dust and pollen cut panel yield by 8 to 15 percent in a dry month, so a monthly wash with the depot's own water restores output. The inverter and buffer need a yearly inspection, and the 240 kW chargers want a firmware check each quarter. None of this needs a specialist on site; the fleet's own electrician handles it with the supplier's remote support.

  • Wash panels monthly in dry season, quarterly in winter.
  • Log daily yield so a falling trend flags a fault fast.
  • Keep the buffer state between 20 and 90 percent for long life.
  • Trim shade trees and rebuilds that grow into the array.
  • Test the grid export limiter after any utility change.

The payback math depends on that yield staying high, so the wash is not cosmetic, it is revenue. Fleets that skip it quietly lose the solar saving and drift back to grid power, which is why the monthly card is part of the depot manager's KPI.

Grid Independence and Resilience

The buffer turns the depot into a small power island. On a grid event the fleet still charges from stored sun, so a cut that stops a diesel depot at the pump does not stop an electric one. For Jordan, where energy security is a stated goal, that resilience is a policy plus as much as a cost win. Operators report that the buffer also smooths the morning peak when every truck plugs in at once, because the chargers draw from storage first and the grid only tops the buffer later in the day.

Over a year the island mode is rare, but when it is needed it keeps the contract running, and that reliability is part of the solar depot's value that the pure fuel math misses. For a fleet on a fixed delivery promise, the ability to charge through a cut is worth more than the kilowatt hours it saves, and it is why operators spec the buffer generously rather than to the bare minimum.

For Jordan operators, the formula is simple: put panels on the roof, buffer the sun, and charge the EV truck overnight for almost nothing. The KT5J is the cargo hauler built to make that math pay, depot by depot, across the kingdom.
