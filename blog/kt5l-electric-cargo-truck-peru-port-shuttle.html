KT5L Electric Cargo Truck for Peru Port Shuttle Operations

Callao Drayage Is the Perfect Electric Truck Loop

Port drayage is the textbook use case for an electric truck: fixed short routes, heavy loads, frequent stops, and tightening low-emission rules around terminal gates. The KT5L electric cargo truck is built for Peru's Callao-to-Lima corridor, moving containers and break-bulk between the port, inland depots, and last-mile distribution centers without the fuel and maintenance overhead of a diesel shuttle fleet.

Because the KT5L runs the same loop dozens of times per week, every kilometre of diesel avoided is predictable and bankable. Regenerative braking on the loaded return from Lima back to Callao recovers energy that a diesel truck simply wastes as heat in its brakes. The result is a duty cycle where the electric truck's strengths, instant torque, smooth stops, and recovery of descent energy, align perfectly with the work.

Port operators also face a rising administrative burden as terminals introduce appointment systems, emissions gates, and clean-truck programs. An electric drayage fleet is positioned to keep working as those rules tighten, whereas an aging diesel fleet risks being excluded from the most valuable gate slots. Electrifying drayage is therefore as much a continuity strategy as a cost strategy.

Designing the Callao-Lima Shuttle

A productive drayage fleet depends on charger placement and shift design more than on the truck itself. We recommend a depot charger close to the terminal gate and a second at the inland cross-dock.

  • Size battery to cover 4-6 round trips before a midday top-up.
  • Place DC chargers at both the port and the Lima distribution hub.
  • Use telematics to stagger gate appointments and avoid queue idling.
  • Train drivers on regenerative driving to extend effective range.
  • Keep one float truck per shift to absorb breakdowns without diesel backup.

Operators who electrify drayage often find the quietest win is terminal access: cleaner trucks face fewer restrictions as ports tighten emissions at the gate. A second, less obvious win is driver retention. The KT5L's smooth, quiet cab reduces fatigue on the relentless start-stop of drayage, and operators who drive both report a clear preference for the electric shift.

A practical charging layout puts a 240-360 kW DC unit at the port yard so trucks top up during the mandatory waiting time that already exists in any terminal queue. By turning dead time into charging time, the operator avoids building a large battery merely to survive idle periods, which lowers both capital cost and payload penalty.

Mountain Grades and Coastal Heat

The Lima-Callao corridor is flat, but inland distribution toward the Andes introduces grades where the KT5L's full-torque-from-zero motor preserves cycle times. The LFP battery tolerates Peru's coastal heat and high UV, and the sealed powertrain avoids the corrosion that salt air inflicts on diesel shuttles. For fleets running beyond Lima into the foothills, we recommend the larger battery option and a conservative state-of-charge floor to protect both range and pack life.

Coastal Peru also brings significant fog and high salinity, a combination that rusts electrical connections on diesel trucks within a few seasons. The KT5L's sealed connectors and elevated pack enclosure are specified for exactly this, which is why early adopters near Callao report lower unscheduled electrical downtime than their diesel equivalents.

Total Cost of Drayage Ownership

On a 30 km round trip with a loaded container one way, the KT5L typically draws 30-50 kWh, against 14-20 litres of diesel for a comparable shuttle. At Peruvian industrial power and diesel prices, the energy gap is wide, and it widens further once maintenance is included. The electric truck has no diesel engine to overhaul, no exhaust after-treatment to clog, and far less brake wear thanks to regeneration, all of which flatten the five-year cost curve.

Driver Training and Gate Efficiency

The human side of drayage electrification is often underestimated. KT5L drivers moving from diesel need a short familiarization on regen braking and on reading state-of-charge, but most adapt within a single shift because the controls are simpler and the cab is quieter. The bigger operational gain comes from gate efficiency: when a terminal introduces a clean-truck appointment system, electric vehicles are scheduled into the fastest lanes, and the time saved compounds across dozens of daily trips.

We recommend fleets instrument the KT5L with telematics that log gate-in to gate-out time, idle minutes, and energy per trip. That dataset lets the operator prove to the terminal that the electric trucks reduce yard congestion and emissions, which strengthens the case for preferential slots. Over a quarter, the combination of faster gates and lower energy cost typically outweighs the financing on the vehicle, especially for high-loop-count carriers that run the same corridor all day.

For carriers operating multiple corridors, the KT5L's common LvKong drivetrain and CATL LFP pack mean one spare-parts kit and one training program cover the whole fleet, simplifying maintenance across depots in Callao, Lima, and beyond.

Scaling Beyond the Pilot

Once a carrier proves the KT5L on the Callao-Lima drayage loop, the natural next step is to extend the model to adjacent corridors: the feeder runs from Lima to the inland distribution parks, and the shorter port shuttles at nearby terminals. Because the vehicle, charger, and telematics stack are identical, each new corridor adds little operational complexity, and the energy-cost advantage compounds across the whole fleet.

The key to scaling is disciplined charger siting. Rather than building a large charger farm at one depot, we recommend distributing smaller DC units at each location the trucks already visit, port, cross-dock, and distribution center, so charging happens during time the truck would otherwise spend waiting. This distributed model keeps capital low and resilience high: if one charger fails, the fleet simply uses the next node rather than grinding to a halt.

Carriers that reach ten or more KT5L units typically negotiate a managed charging tariff with their utility, smoothing the load and unlocking a lower rate that further widens the gap with diesel. At that scale, the electric drayage fleet is no longer a pilot but the default way the carrier moves freight, and new diesel purchases become the exception rather than the rule.
