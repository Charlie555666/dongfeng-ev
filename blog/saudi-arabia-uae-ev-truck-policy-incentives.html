Saudi Arabia and UAE EV Truck Policy: Incentives, Targets and What They Mean for Fleet Buyers

Policy in the Gulf does not drift — it is decreed, funded and measured. That is what makes the current EV policy wave in Saudi Arabia and the UAE commercially serious rather than rhetorical: both governments have attached national programmes, money and procurement muscle to vehicle electrification, and both are extending it from passenger cars into the commercial and logistics fleets where the region's fuel actually burns. For fleet buyers, the question this article answers is precise: what do Saudi and Emirati policies actually mandate or reward for electric trucks, how do the certification systems work for imported EV trucks, and — most importantly — what should a fleet operator in Riyadh, Jeddah, Dubai or Sharjah do in the next 24 months to be positioned on the right side of the policy curve rather than chasing it?

Saudi Arabia: Vision 2030 and the Saudi Green Initiative

The Saudi framework stacks three layers. At the top, Vision 2030 names sustainable transport and industrial localisation as national priorities. Beneath it, the Saudi Green Initiative (SGI) carries quantified commitments — most famously the target that a substantial share of Riyadh's vehicles be electric by 2030, alongside national emission-reduction goals that make fleet electrification the most visible lever a state-linked company can pull. And at the delivery layer, PIF-funded vehicles and logistics entities (the EV company ecosystem, the national logistics programme with its aim to place Saudi Arabia in the world's top logistics performers) translate targets into procurement: when a PIF-linked distributor or port operator writes a tender, fleet emissions now score.

For heavy fleets specifically, the practical policy instruments to watch: green procurement scoring in government-adjacent tenders (already visible in municipal and port contracts); industrial-city charging build-out in the logistics zones around Riyadh and Jeddah, where developer-side incentives for electric fleets are emerging; and fuel-price normalisation — the kingdom's managed domestic fuel pricing has trended upward over the years, progressively widening the electricity-diesel spread that drives EV truck economics even at subsidised power tariffs. None of this requires a diesel ban to bite; the procurement scoring and the fuel trajectory do the work.

UAE: The Region's Densest EV Framework

The Emirates run the Gulf's most developed EV apparatus. The National Electric Vehicles Policy targets a major share of vehicles on the road being electric by mid-century with intermediate milestones; each Emirate adds its own instruments — Dubai's Green Mobility strategy with government-fleet electrification quotas, Abu Dhabi's charging-network expansion through its utility ecosystem, Sharjah's sustainability-zone initiatives. For trucks specifically, the operative framework items are: DEWA's charging network (the region's densest public infrastructure, increasingly including DC capacity relevant to commercial vehicles), free-zone ESG regimes (Dubai's logistics free zones score tenants on sustainability — and fleet emissions are the biggest line a logistics tenant controls), and ESMA/standard conformity for imported EVs, a system that already handles Chinese electric commercials as a routine category. The UAE's compact city geography — dense delivery radii, strong grid, high stop counts — means policy is reinforcing an operating case that already works.

Why Heavy Fleets Are the Policy's Logical Next Chapter

Gulf EV policy began with passenger cars because that is where global supply chains pointed. But the arithmetic of the region's transport emissions lives in trucks: a single diesel tractor or tipper burns as much fuel as dozens of cars, runs far more hours per day, and concentrates in exactly the zones (ports, industrial cities, logistics hubs) where governments measure air quality and corporate tenants report ESG. A few thousand electrified delivery trucks and a few hundred electrified port tractors move a national fuel-consumption needle that a hundred thousand imported passenger EVs touch only indirectly. Policy makers in both countries understand this, which is why the commercial-fleet chapter — green-tender scoring, charging in logistics zones, state-linked logistics operators leading by example — is now the active front.

  • Ports and free zones first: gated duty cycles, single-owner infrastructure, international customers demanding Scope 3 data — the natural early deployments (we detail the TE46 port-tractor case in a separate article).
  • City logistics second: dense delivery duty where the KT5M-class electric box truck's economics already clear diesel (Gulf-specific numbers in our GCC city-logistics analysis).
  • Construction and mining next: the kingdom's giga-projects and mining expansion under Vision 2030 minerals strategy — the same pit-to-plant logic driving electric mining trucks worldwide.

The Certification Route for Imported EV Trucks

Import compliance is where policy meets paperwork, and both markets have workable paths. In Saudi Arabia, the SABER platform under SASO administers product and shipment conformity certificates; the importer (or their consultant) files the technical dossier — specifications, test reports, battery documentation — and obtains the Shipment Certificate of Conformity before customs clearance. EV trucks from China are an established category in this system. In the UAE, each Emirate's registration authority applies the federal conformity framework; Chinese electric commercials clear it routinely, with left-hand-drive configuration and Arabic/English documentation the practical requirements. In both markets, the battery paperwork chain — UN 38.3 test summaries, MSDS, IMDG declarations for sea transit — travels with the vehicle, and heat-climate specification (50 °C-rated cooling, LFP chemistry) is both a certification talking point and an operational necessity. What the Heat Means for Specification (Policy Meets Physics)

Gulf policy enthusiasm does not change the climate, and any fleet electrification in the region must be engineered for 45–50 °C sustained ambient. That is why the CATL LFP chemistry across the Dongfeng range matters more in the Gulf than anywhere else: wider thermal-stability margins, no runaway-propagation behaviour under abuse testing, and active liquid cooling that holds pack temperature in the safe window through Saudi August. It is also why specification discipline — the 310 kWh option over the 262 kWh where routes plus air-conditioning demand headroom, white/reflective bodies, shaded or overnight-charging choreography — is part of policy compliance in practice: a heat-derated electric truck misses both its economic and its ESG reporting numbers. The 24-Month Playbook for Gulf Fleet Buyers

Reading the policy curve, our advice to fleet operators in both markets is a three-move sequence. Move 1 (now): pilot 3–10 electric units on the highest-density duty — city delivery or port/yard shuttle — with depot charging on existing commercial power; the operating data (kWh/km, cost per delivery, SoH trend) becomes both your scale-up business case and your tender-scoring evidence. Move 2 (12 months): use the pilot data to lock multi-year electric fleet pricing and charging infrastructure plans before policy-driven demand compresses supply and installation queues; align with your free-zone or industrial-city developer on charger siting. Move 3 (24 months): carry verified fleet-emissions data into every government-adjacent and international-customer tender you bid — by then, expect the scoring to have hardened from "bonus points" toward "baseline requirement", and be the bidder for whom it is paperwork rather than panic.
