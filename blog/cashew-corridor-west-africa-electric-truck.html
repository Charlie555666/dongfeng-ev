The Cashew Corridor Goes Electric: KT5M and TE46 EV Truck Strategy for West Africa's Sahel Trade Lane

West Africa grows close to half the world's raw cashew nuts, and the crop moves along one of the region's most distinctive trade lanes: a February-to-July river of jute-bagged nuts flowing from buying stations across Côte d'Ivoire's north, northern Ghana, Burkina Faso and Mali down to the processing plants and port terminals of San Pedro, Abidjan, Tema and Lomé. It is a seasonal, high-volume, corridor-shaped logistics problem — and corridor-shaped logistics is what electric trucks do best. This piece maps an electric cashew fleet around the KT5M electric box truck for the collection and processing legs and the TE46 electric tractor for the trunk hauls to port. Country context is in our Côte d'Ivoire market guide.

Anatomy of the Cashew Season

The corridor has three tiers, each with different duty cycles:

  • Collection tier: bikes, pickups and light trucks aggregating sacks from farm-gate buying points to village and sous-préfecture stores — short, dispersed runs of 10–50 km.
  • Regional consolidation: 10–18 t trucks moving bagged nuts from the stores to regional warehouses in Korhogo, Ferkessédougou, Bondoukou, Bole and Bobo-Dioulasso — legs of 60–200 km on mixed roads.
  • Trunk evacuation: articulated trucks hauling 25–30 t loads from regional warehouses to processing plants and port terminals — 300–600 km legs on the paved corridors south.

The second and third tiers are where the electric opportunity lives. Regional consolidation runs fixed routes between fixed points with overnight returns to a warehouse that can host a charger; trunk evacuation runs scheduled, high-utilisation loads that justify fast-charging infrastructure on the corridor.

Seasonality and Fleet Utilisation

Cashew is seasonal — but the corridor is not idle off-season. The same warehouses and trucks move cotton northbound after ginning, maize and millet at harvest, shea nuts in the Burkinabé and northern Ghanaian belt, and general cargo year-round. A fleet specified for cashew duty is a fleet for the whole Sahelian commodity calendar. The electric drivetrain's low off-season holding cost (no engine corrosion from months of idling, no fuel system degeneration, battery self-discharge measured in single-digit percentages per month) makes seasonal utilisation patterns less punishing than for diesel.

Economics on the Korhogo–San Pedro Lane

Modelling a TE46 doing trunk evacuation through the season — 300 round trips of 550 km, 165,000 km/year on the heaviest corridors:

  • Diesel tractor at West African pump prices: roughly USD 0.42/km fuel — USD 69,000 per year.
  • TE46 at blended Ivorian grid tariffs (~USD 0.12/kWh, 1.5 kWh/km average with loads): about USD 0.18/km — USD 29,700 per year.
  • Annual energy saving: near USD 39,000 per tractor — the CAPEX premium repays inside a single 18-month window.
  • Maintenance on 165,000 km of Sahel corridor: electric saves another USD 8,000–12,000 per year against diesel tractor maintenance at the same duty.

Even at half that annual distance — a more typical mixed utilisation — payback stays inside 30 months. The corridor's economics are strong enough that a cooperative or processor group can underwrite the fleet against the season's trading margin rather than seeking external finance.

Charging Architecture Along the Lane

The corridor's fixed geography means a handful of charging points electrify hundreds of kilometres:

  1. Regional warehouse points: 120 kW DC chargers at Korhogo, Ferkessédougou and Bondoukou warehouses — the nuts are weighed, graded and re-bagged for an hour or two anyway; the trucks charge through every stop.
  2. Mid-corridor fast point: one 240 kW DC charger at the Daloa junction where north–south traffic naturally pauses — shared by multiple operators as a paid charging service, which is itself an emerging business on the corridor.
  3. Port-plant chargers: processing plants at San Pedro and Abidjan host chargers for the inbound fleet; plants are industrial power customers with robust supply.
  4. Solar augmentation: northern Côte d'Ivoire's dry-season irradiance is excellent — a 60–100 kWp array at a regional warehouse with battery buffering keeps collection running through the region's grid dips.

Why Processors Will Push This First

The cashew trade's structure favours early electrification more than most commodities: a handful of large processors and exporters control the volumes, they operate or contract fixed fleets on fixed lanes, and their buyers — Vietnamese and Indian kernel processors, then European and American snack and ingredient brands — face tightening supply-chain emissions expectations. A processor that can document that its raw-nut evacuation ran on electricity from Sahel solar holds a marketing asset that no diesel competitor can copy quickly.
