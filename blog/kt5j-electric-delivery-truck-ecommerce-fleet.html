The Delivery Duty Cycle, Quantified

Before specifications, the shape of the work. An e-commerce distribution vehicle in a dense metro market runs a day that looks like: depart the fulfilment centre or dark store at 07:00 with a full load; execute 60–120 stops across 120–200 km with 8–15 km between cluster stops; return to base by 19:00–21:00; sit overnight. Consumption in this duty — which is stop-start heaven for regenerative braking — runs 0.85–1.15 kWh/km including auxiliaries. The KT5J's battery covers this with 15–40% daily margin in the standard configuration, which is exactly the buffer fleet managers want: a margin that absorbs the abnormal day (road closures, festival volume, re-attempts) without route redesign.

Dark Stores Change the Route Architecture — in Electrification's Favour

The dark-store model (micro-fulfilment nodes serving 1–3 km delivery radiuses) compresses the delivery day even further: 30–80 km of ultra-dense multi-drop cycling, with the truck returning to the same node repeatedly. At these distances the KT5J runs two shifts on one charge, and the charging problem shrinks to a handful of 60–120 kW positions at the nodes — frequently the same power upgrade the dark store's refrigeration and automation needed anyway. Quick-commerce operators also exploit the electric drivetrain's residential-friendly noise signature: pre-dawn shelf resupply runs that local noise rules would block with diesel vans run quietly and legally, moving the restock window earlier and smoothing daytime courier capacity.

Why Route Density Is the Electric Multiplier

The economics of any delivery vehicle scale with stops per kilometre; the electric advantage scales right alongside it. The reason: at each stop, a diesel engine idles while the driver handles the drop — multi-stop urban diesel trucks idle 30–45% of engine-on time — while the KT5J consumes effectively nothing stationary. On a 100-stop day, that idle gap alone is worth 8–15 L of diesel (USD 9–20 at typical urban pump prices), pure waste the electric truck eliminates. Add the per-km energy gap (electric delivery duty at USD 0.10–0.16/km-equivalent vs diesel at USD 0.30–0.48/km-equivalent in most of our markets) and brake-wear savings from regeneration, and the daily operating delta lands at USD 18–35 per truck per day. Across a 60-truck fleet and 320 operating days, that is USD 350,000–670,000 per year — the number that turns the CFO conversation from "sustainability initiative" into "line-item".

Fleet Scaling: The Phased Pattern That Works

Every successful e-commerce electrification we have supported follows a similar three-phase pattern, and skipping phases is where operators stumble:

  1. Pilot (10–15% of routes, 3–6 months). The KT5J units take the densest, most predictable routes; the depot gets its first charge posts; dispatchers learn the range-planning rhythm. Success metric: cost per drop matching or beating diesel from month two.
  2. Core conversion (50–70%). Charging infrastructure scales with the fleet (one dual-gun 120 kW position per 2–3 trucks overnight works for single-shift; shift-double operations want 240 kW positions); maintenance crews take the high-voltage safety training; spares holding is established.
  3. Full network. Diesel residual units rotate to overflow duty or exit; the fleet's energy contract is renegotiated around a known, flat overnight load profile that utilities price favourably.

Charging Choreography for Sort-Based Operations

  • Overnight at the hub is the backbone — trucks return with 15–40% remaining and finish charging by 03:00 at 120 kW, off any peak-tariff window.
  • Midday top-ups are free range — the vehicle-return window during the afternoon sort (when drivers hand back and reload) offers 45–90 minutes of plug time; a 120 kW top-up adds 60–100 km of margin for the evening wave.
  • Peak-day protocol: on festival volumes, the 40% daily buffer plus midday top-up covers double-digit-percentage volume surges without renting diesel capacity.
  • Depot layout: drive-through charging lanes beat nose-in for high-churn fleets — a detail that saves 15–20 minutes per truck per day in aggregate.

The Driver Equation

Fleet managers consistently underweight driver experience, and consistently regret it. In mixed diesel/electric fleets, drivers migrate to the electric units by preference, and the reasons are operational: no clutch in 100-stop duty (a genuinely physical difference over a shift), quieter cab, instant torque in traffic, and no fuel-station detours. Retention data from our clients' fleets shows electric-route drivers logging lower absenteeism and higher stop-completion rates. In logistics operations where driver turnover runs 25–40% annually, an equipment choice that improves retention carries a value most TCO spreadsheets never model.
