Should I Buy or Lease an Electric Truck Fleet?

Quick answer: buy if you run high annual mileage on stable routes and can fund the capital - ownership wins 5-year TCO by 10-25%; lease if you are deploying electric trucks for the first time, preserving capital, or want residual risk transferred. What Does Buying an Electric Truck Actually Cost Over Five Years?

The honest five-year math for ownership, using the TE8L electric 6x4 tractor as the reference. Capital: an FOB range of $120,000-$150,000 plus shipping, duties and registration - call the landed figure the sticker, because everything else discounts from it. Energy: 350 kWh pack, 1.2-1.6 kWh per kilometer on regional 6x4 tractor duty, at industrial overnight tariffs - typically $0.08-$0.15 per kilometer, against $0.50-$0.90 in diesel for the same work. Maintenance: 40-60% below diesel, with no engine, gearbox or aftertreatment service. Residual: the electric residual question is settling as battery-health certification becomes standard practice - a documented pack with an 8-year warranty transfers value credibly. Summed across five years at 60,000-80,000 km annually, ownership routinely lands 20-35% below diesel TCO - the case that makes the lease-versus-buy question worth asking at all.

What Does Leasing Offer That Buying Does Not?

Leasing sells three things, and buyers should price each separately. Capital preservation: the fleet's cash stays in the business - no six-figure outlay per truck, which for a growing Kenyan operator means trucks funded from operating cash flow rather than credit lines. Obsolescence cover: battery and drivetrain technology is advancing, and a closed-end lease hands the residual risk to the lessor - if five-year-old electric trucks prove harder to sell than expected, that is the lessor's problem. And simplicity: full-service leases bundle maintenance, sometimes insurance and telematics, into one monthly figure the CFO can budget exactly. The costs of those benefits: total payments over the term typically exceed purchase-plus-operation by 10-25%, mileage caps and return conditions constrain flexibility, and the fleet builds no asset. Whether the trade is worth it depends on the buyer's capital cost and risk appetite - not on the technology.

How Do the Real Numbers Compare Side by Side?

Worked example on a TE8L over five years at 70,000 km annually, in round figures a fleet can adjust. Buy: landed cost, plus energy at $0.10-$0.14 per km, plus maintenance, insurance and tires, minus residual at year five - total cost of ownership per kilometer typically $0.55-$0.75 depending on market inputs. Lease: monthly payment covering truck and full service, plus energy and insurance, with no residual recovery - total per kilometer typically $0.65-$0.90, the spread being the lessor's margin and risk premium. Diesel benchmark on the same duty: $0.80-$1.10. Both electric structures beat diesel decisively; between them, buying wins on money and leasing wins on cash position and risk transfer. The sensitivity that matters most is utilization - above roughly 50,000 km per year, ownership's advantage widens; below it, leasing's flexibility earns its premium.

When Does Leasing Make More Sense Than Buying?

Five situations favor leasing clearly. First deployments: a fleet's first two or three electric trucks, where operational learning matters more than asset optimization - lease, learn, then buy with knowledge. Capital-constrained growth: operators whose freight contracts justify trucks the credit line will not fund. Rapidly scaling networks: delivery and retail fleets adding routes faster than procurement can cycle purchases. Obsolescence-sensitive buyers: operators who want today's truck for four years and the next generation after, cleanly. And public or corporate fleets with operating-budget structures that simply cannot book capital purchases. In Kenya, we see the pattern most often in first deployments - and the fleets usually convert to purchase at renewal, once their own duty data has removed the uncertainty they paid the lessor to carry.

When Should You Buy Outright?

Ownership wins where utilization is high, duty is stable and capital is available at reasonable cost. High annual mileage concentrates the electric truck's operating advantage into the shortest payback - a 6x4 tractor running 70,000-100,000 km per year recovers the electric premium fastest and banks the savings longest. Stable duty - the same routes, the same depot, year on year - removes the flexibility argument that justifies leasing's premium. And long holding intentions complete the case: the TE8L's CATL LFP pack carries an 8-year, 4,500-cycle warranty, and the drivetrain's service life runs well past it, so a fleet holding trucks seven to ten years harvests the full economic life the diesel equivalent never quite delivers. Deployment Timeline from Order to Operation

Realistic scheduling prevents the most common fleet electrification failures. A typical TE8L deployment runs five to seven months end to end: production takes 30-60 days depending on configuration and order size; ocean freight to the Port of Mombasa adds 25-45 days; customs clearance and homologation steps add two to six weeks depending on Kenyan procedures. Running in parallel, the depot charging installation should complete before the trucks land, and driver and technician training should finish during commissioning week. The sequence that works: confirm specifications and sign the contract, start certification paperwork immediately, order chargers at the same time as the trucks, and book training against the vessel arrival date. Fleets that run these tracks in parallel put trucks into revenue service within days of clearance; fleets that run them sequentially lose months. Frequently Asked Questions

Should I buy or lease an electric truck fleet?

Buy if you run over 50,000 km per truck per year on stable routes and have capital access - 5-year TCO runs 10-25% below leasing. Lease for first deployments, capital-constrained growth, or when you want residual-value risk off your books.

How much does an electric truck cost per month on a lease?

Full-service leases on heavy electric trucks typically run the equivalent of the financed amount plus a 10-25% total premium over purchase - against diesel, both structures still win, since electric energy at $0.08-$0.15 per km undercuts diesel's $0.50-$0.90 decisively.

What is the payback period when buying an electric truck?

Typically 3-4 years on high-utilization duty: a 400-600 kWh electric truck at $120,000-$180,000 FOB saves $2,000-$5,000 per month in energy and maintenance versus diesel at 60,000-80,000 km annual mileage.

Do leased electric trucks include battery warranty?

Yes - the factory battery warranty runs with the truck regardless of ownership structure: 8 years or 4,500 cycles on the CATL LFP pack, transferable and documented, which is also what supports residual values at lease end.

Can I buy an electric truck first and lease later?

The common pattern is the reverse: fleets lease 2-3 trucks for the first deployment year to build operating data, then purchase at renewal once their own route and cost numbers remove the uncertainty - owned fleets at high mileage hold the strongest economics.
