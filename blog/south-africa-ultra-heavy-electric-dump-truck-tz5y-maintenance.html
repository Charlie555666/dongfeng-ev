Why an electric truck needs a different maintenance playbook

A diesel TZ5Y and its electric equivalent share tyres, brakes, axles and cabs, but almost nothing else under the skin. The diesel you replace in South Africa burns fuel, needs oil and filter changes every 10,000-15,000 km, and runs a high-maintenance aftertreatment system. The TZ5Y electric dump truck replaces all of that with a sealed motor, a liquid-cooled CATL LFP pack of 466-600 kWh and power electronics. The result is that a well-run electric fleet spends most of its maintenance budget on consumables and inspections rather than on engine and exhaust repair. Operators in Johannesburg, Rustenburg and Durban who plan for this shift from day one cut workshop hours sharply in the first year.

The preventive maintenance schedule that actually matters

For the TZ5Y we recommend a 90-day / 10,000 km inspection cycle built around six checks: brake pad and disc wear, tyre pressure and tread, coolant level and conductivity on the battery loop, high-voltage connector torque, gearbox and axle oil condition, and a full battery-management-system health log download. None of these is exotic, but the battery-loop coolant and the HV connector checks are the two that diesel-trained mechanics most often skip. A simple checklist app on the fleet tablet keeps them honest. Because the drivetrain has 510 kW (3,400 Nm) of torque available from zero rpm, expect rear tyres to wear faster than on diesel if drivers are not trained to feather the pedal.

Battery and thermal-system care in a hot climate

Heat is the real enemy of pack life, and South Africa does not lack for it. The TZ5Y uses liquid cooling to hold cell temperature in its comfort band, but the cooling loop itself needs attention: coolant condition, radiator fins clear of dust, and pump operation. In dusty mining or construction duty in Johannesburg, Rustenburg and Durban, blow out the pack radiator every service and check for stone damage. Keeping depth of discharge between 20% and 80% for daily duty, and parking under shade or in a ventilated shed rather than in full sun, will do more for the 8-year / 4,500-cycle warranty than any single repair.

Brakes, tyres and suspension on a heavy EV

Regenerative braking means the service brakes on a TZ5Y work far less than on a diesel, so pads last longer — but they can also seize or glaze from under-use, so the annual strip, clean and lubricate of calipers is not optional. Tyres carry the same loads and, because the pack sits low, cornering forces are lower; still, payload 40-45 t duty means premium tyres and disciplined rotation. Suspension and steering components see heavier front-axle loads on some EV layouts, so inspect kingpins, bushes and shock absorbers at every service.

Building the workshop and the team

An electric truck does not need a diesel engine bay, but it does need a dedicated high-voltage bay: insulated tools, a lockout/tagout procedure, a 1,000 V-rated multimeter and at least two HV-certified technicians. Retraining two existing mechanics is usually faster and cheaper than hiring, and the skills transfer quickly because the only genuinely new discipline is the high-voltage safety protocol. For fleets in Johannesburg, Rustenburg and Durban, we run on-site commissioning and a two-day HV safety course as part of delivery.

Why South Africa is ready for electric trucks now

The conditions that make an electric truck viable are all present in South Africa. high diesel prices and grid constraints that favour depot solar charging. Freight demand concentrates in and around Johannesburg, Rustenburg and Durban, where routes are short and predictable and the depot is never far away. Fuel logistics add cost and delay that a fleet charging its own vehicles simply avoids. For a TZ5Y electric dump truck on a platinum and manganese mining haulage duty cycle, this is not an experimental technology but a practical replacement for diesel, and the operators moving first are the ones who lock in the lowest cost per kilometre before their competitors do.

A step-by-step implementation plan

Electrifying a fleet in South Africa is a project, and running it in phases is what keeps it manageable. Phase one is a site and route audit: list every duty cycle, measure real daily distance and load, and map the depot power supply. Phase two is a pilot of two to five TZ5Y units on the most predictable routes, with chargers installed and drivers trained. Phase three is measurement — energy cost per kilometre, uptime, maintenance hours. Phase four is scaling what the data supports. Each phase de-risks the next and keeps the capital commitment matched to proven performance rather than optimism.
