Lagos Goes Electric: EV Truck Strategy for Nigeria's Biggest Freight Market

Lagos is the hardest freight market in West Africa — and, for exactly the same reasons, one of the most promising EV truck markets on the continent. Twenty million people, a port complex handling the majority of Nigeria's container trade, average traffic speeds that sometimes fall below 20 km/h, and a diesel price that has been liberalised to full import parity. When a truck spends most of its day stationary or crawling, a diesel engine burns fuel to accomplish nothing; an electric drivetrain costs almost nothing to run in the same conditions. This article sets out how a Nigerian distribution or FMCG fleet should approach its first EV truck deployment in Lagos with the Dongfeng KT5M electric box truck and KT5J electric delivery truck — the machines, the money, the grid reality, and a phased rollout plan that survives contact with Apapa traffic.

Why Lagos Duty Cycles Favour Electric

Three structural features of Lagos logistics make the fuel-to-power arithmetic unusually decisive:

  • Extreme stop-start density. Distribution runs from warehouses in Ikeja, Agbara and Ikorodu to retail clusters on the island and the mainland involve 60–120 stops per shift. A diesel truck in this duty burns 25–32 L/100 km, a large share of it while idling at gates, markets and police checkpoints. The KT5J consumes 55–70 kWh per 100 km in the same cycle, most of it actually moving.
  • Full import-parity diesel. Since subsidy removal, Nigerian diesel trades near international levels — historically equivalent to USD 0.85–1.10 per litre in naira terms, and vulnerable to FX movement. Electricity from a captive solar-plus-storage depot, by contrast, is priced in a currency the fleet operator partially controls.
  • Predictable, bounded routes. Lagos fleets run fixed radii: depot to market cluster and back, 80–200 km per day. Bounded routes mean the range question — the usual objection to electric trucks — answers itself: a KT5M with a CATL 262–310 kWh pack covering 200+ km of real urban range handles the day with margin to spare.

The Power Question, Answered Honestly

No EV truck article about Nigeria can avoid the grid. Lagos industrial supply has improved with band-based tariffs, but outages remain a planning assumption. The correct architecture is therefore not "charge from the grid" but "charge from a hybrid depot":

  1. Size a solar carport for daytime opportunity charging. A 300–500 kWp array over the depot apron, generating 450–750 MWh a year in Lagos irradiance, covers a meaningful share of daytime top-ups for a 20–30 truck fleet.
  2. Buffer with stationary storage. A 500 kWh–1 MWh buffer bank smooths grid outages and lets the fleet buy grid power only in cheap night bands. Second-life LFP containers are the cost-effective choice and pair naturally with the truck packs' chemistry.
  3. Charge overnight, top up midday. Night charges from the grid (band C off-peak or captive generation) handle the bulk; solar handles the midday splash-and-dash that keeps KT5J units cycling through the delivery day.

This architecture adds USD 250,000–450,000 of energy CAPEX to a fleet project — real money, but it converts an unreliable input into a controllable one, and it is the difference between an EV truck programme that runs and one that stalls.

Import, Duties and Delivery

Nigeria imposes import duty on completely knocked-down and fully built commercial vehicles; EV trucks are assessed under the same HS chapter as their diesel counterparts, so there is no EV penalty — but also, at present, no federal EV incentive to bank on. The practical import path for a Lagos fleet: RORO or container shipment to Apapa or Tin Can Island, pre-shipment inspection under the Nigerian scheme, SONCAP conformity documentation, and careful battery paperwork — UN 38.3 test summaries, MSDS and IMDG-compliant packing declarations, which we as the exporter prepare as standard. Landed cost typically runs 25–40% above FOB once duty, levies, clearing and inland haulage are counted; fleets should budget on that basis, not on the FOB line alone.

One Lagos-specific tip: clear the trucks through a licensed agent you have used before, and have the exporter's invoice, packing list and battery certificates apostilled and consistent to the last digit. Discrepancies in battery documentation are the single most common cause of clearing delays for EV trucks at Nigerian ports.

A Phased Rollout That Survives Contact With Reality

  • Phase 1 (months 1–3): Import 3–5 KT5J units on the highest-density fixed routes; install 2 dual-gun 120 kW chargers and a modest solar carport; train drivers on one-pedal regen discipline — regenerative braking is worth 15–25% of consumed energy in stop-start Lagos duty, and driver technique is the largest single variable in that range.
  • Phase 2 (months 4–9): Expand to 15–25 units including KT5M trunk trucks; commission the buffer storage; shift the charging window to off-peak bands; begin SoH tracking and driver league tables from the fleet telemetry.
  • Phase 3 (months 10–18): Scale to 50+ units across multiple depots; evaluate battery swap for the KT5M trunk layer if utilisation demands 20-hour duty; use the accumulated telemetry and fuel-saving data to secure bank financing for the next tranche.

The fleets that fail at electrification in Africa are almost always the ones that skipped Phase 1 discipline — buying thirty trucks before understanding charging windows, driver behaviour or their own duty-cycle data. The ones that succeed treat the pilot as an instrument, not a gesture.

Case Profile: An Apapa-Area FMCG Distributor

To make the numbers concrete, consider a composite drawn from real deployments we support in comparable West African markets. A Lagos FMCG distributor runs 38 diesel trucks out of an Ikeja warehouse: 30 light delivery units on shop-supply loops averaging 140 km a day, and 8 box trucks on warehouse-to-market trunk runs averaging 180 km. Fuel is its largest operating cost after wages; driver churn and breakdown downtime erode service levels; and two of its principal customers — international consumer-goods companies — have begun asking for emissions data per delivery lane.

The electrified version of this fleet phases in over eighteen months. The first tranche is six KT5J units on the three densest shop-supply loops, supported by two dual-gun 120 kW chargers installed under a new 300 kWp solar carport on the warehouse roof apron. Drivers are trained on one-pedal regen and charge-window discipline; dispatchers re-sequence routes so the highest-density work runs in the morning wave, when the trucks leave at 100% SoC. From month six, the fleet adds KT5M box trucks for the trunk runs and expands the delivery layer to twenty units, with a 500 kWh second-life buffer bank smoothing the site's grid draw into the cheap night band.

By the end of year two the operator holds a verified dataset: kWh/km by route and driver, SoH curves for every pack, fuel-equivalent savings of roughly USD 7,500–9,500 per truck per year, and a CO₂-per-delivery figure its principals accept for their own reporting. That dataset — not the trucks — is what finances the second depot. Nigerian banks are conservative lenders on new technology, but they lend readily against eighteen months of verified operating data and a fuel-bill line item that visibly collapsed. This is the pattern we recommend every Lagos operator follow: pilot small, measure obsessively, scale on evidence.
