Noise: The Condition That Actually Decides

Emissions limits matter, but noise is what determines the working window, and the working window determines the programme. The mechanism is straightforward:

  • Diesel tipper at full load on a haul road: 85–92 dB(A) measured at source, with the dominant contributions from the engine, the exhaust and the cooling fan.
  • TZ5E under the same load: 63–70 dB(A), with the dominant contributions from tyres, hydraulics and the body — items no powertrain choice eliminates.
  • Attenuation over distance means roughly 15–25 dB(A) reduction at 30–50 m from the haul road, putting an electric tipper inside typical residential night limits where a diesel unit is not.

The practical consequence is that contractors bidding night or early-morning earthmoving shifts can win approvals with an electric fleet that would be refused with diesel. Given that night earthmoving is also faster — no traffic, no congestion on the haul route — compliance converts directly into programme time.

Applying for a Night Working Permit With an Electric Fleet

Where a noise-based permit is required, the application is stronger with measured data rather than assertions. Prepare:

  1. Manufacturer noise type-approval data for the specific vehicle, in dB(A), measured under the relevant standard.
  2. A site noise model — haul road location, distance to the nearest sensitive receptor, expected number of movements per hour. Acoustic consultants produce these routinely and they are usually a condition of approval anyway.
  3. A monitoring commitment — boundary monitoring during the first weeks, with results reported to the authority. This is often what converts a hesitant officer into an approval.
  4. A complaints procedure with a named contact. Authorities approve more readily when there is an accountable escalation path.

Air Quality and Non-Road Machinery Rules

Many jurisdictions now regulate construction equipment emissions separately from road vehicle emissions, typically by engine stage or by a site-based emissions inventory. Electric equipment sits outside those frameworks entirely, which produces two benefits:

  • Simplified compliance. No engine stage to evidence, no aftertreatment to maintain, no idling restrictions to police.
  • Enclosed and below-ground work. Basement excavation, tunnels and cut-and-cover work require ventilation sized to diesel particulate and NOx. Electric plant reduces ventilation demand substantially — a genuine cost saving on major projects, and covered in more detail in our tunnel ventilation analysis.

Dust and Water: The Condition That Applies Regardless

Electrification does not exempt a site from dust control, and inspectors will not accept it as a substitute. Pair the TZ5E fleet with electric suppression equipment:

  • KT3F electric sprinkler truck for haul road watering on a schedule.
  • KT7A electric washing truck for hard-surface cleaning and wheel-wash maintenance.
  • Automatic sheeting on the tipper body for any public-road section.

Running suppression electrically also removes the irony of a diesel water truck emitting particulates while suppressing dust.

Charging on a Constrained Urban Site

Urban sites have the smallest electrical envelopes of any application, which makes planning essential:

  • Temporary supply is often 100–250 kVA, shared with site offices, lighting and tools. Smart load management lets a tipper fleet charge inside a modest ceiling by staggering charge windows — see smart charging load management.
  • Night working means daytime charging, which in hot climates adds thermal load. Covered bays or night-charging windows mitigate it.
  • Space is tight. Chargers should be sited so that parking and charging happen in one movement; a fleet that must be reshuffled to charge will not be charged reliably.
