Barranquilla's Caribbean Boom: TZ5E and KT5M Electric Truck Strategy for Colombia's Port City

Barranquilla is having a decade. Colombia's Caribbean capital of industry sits at the mouth of the Magdalena River, hosts the country's principal multipurpose port complex across Puerto Colombia and the expanded terminal zone, and is ringed by construction: the river dredging programme, the Ruta del Maíz highway works, industrial-park expansion at Mamonal, and a housing pipeline that has made the Atlántico department one of Colombia's fastest-growing construction markets. Behind the port, the city runs dense FMCG and retail distribution serving the entire Caribbean coast from Cartagena to Santa Marta.

We have covered Bogotá and the Andean corridor for Colombia before. This piece is the Caribbean strategy: a two-vehicle fleet built around the TZ5E 6x4 electric dump truck (CATL 400 kWh LFP, 25 t GVW) for the construction and aggregates boom, and the KT5M electric box truck (CATL 262/310 kWh, 18 t GVW) for port-city distribution.

Why the Caribbean Coast Is Different from the Andes

Colombia's EV truck conversation usually starts with Bogotá's altitude and steep grades. The Caribbean coast is the opposite case — and in many ways the better one:

  • Flat terrain. Barranquilla sits at 15 m elevation. Consumption on loaded duty stays near 1.1–1.3 kWh/km for a loaded TZ5E versus 1.6+ in the Andes — more kilometres per kilowatt-hour than any Colombian highland market.
  • Heat, not cold. Steady 30 °C-plus temperatures suit LFP chemistry, which prefers warmth to cold; the thermal management system works in its efficient band year-round.
  • Dense, short loops. Construction runs from quarries at Juan de Acosta, Tubará and Galapa into the city are 25–60 km round trips; distribution loops are 60–120 km. Everything returns to a yard each night.
  • Coastal wind and solar. La Guajira's wind build-out is tilting the regional grid mix; Atlántico's own solar resource is strong for depot augmentation.

The Distribution Fleet: KT5M on Port-City Retail

Barranquilla's retail distribution is dominated by FMCG groups and pharmacy and grocery chains running from Mamonal and the Vía 40 logistics corridor into the city and up and down the coast. The KT5M with a 45 m³ box or curtain body on an 18 t GVW chassis is the exact vehicle class this work uses. With a rated 300 km range, a single overnight charge covers a full coast run — Barranquilla to Cartagena is about 130 km — or a dense double-shift city day with a midday 120 kW splash.

For fleets with mixed duty, we specify a split: 310 kWh packs for the coast runs, 262 kWh packs for pure city work. The lighter pack costs less up front and carries less daily energy than the route needs — why pay for electrons you will never use?

Charging Design for a Coastal Operator

A combined fleet of eight TZ5E and eight KT5M needs a modest depot:

  1. Three 120 kW DC chargers at the main yard on the Vía 40 or Galapa corridor — overnight charging in two shifts of vehicles.
  2. One 240 kW DC fast charger for midday top-ups and quarry-leg flexibility.
  3. 60–100 kWp rooftop solar on the depot — Atlántico's irradiance makes this a 4-year payback that simultaneously shaves demand charges and bridges outages.
  4. Smart charging backend staggering sessions to keep site demand under roughly 400 kW, deferring any transformer upgrade.

Budget USD 190,000–250,000 all-in. The sixteen-truck fleet's fuel savings alone repay that inside 14–18 months at Colombian diesel prices.

Import Mechanics: Cartagena or Barranquilla Direct

Colombian EV imports enter through Cartagena's container and RoRo terminals or increasingly Barranquilla's own port. Key items in our standard Colombia package: DIAN customs entry with the reduced EV tariff treatment, RUNT registration support, homologation documentation through the Ministry of Transport process, LHD configuration (the national standard), and Spanish-language manuals, driver training and safety signage. Transit from China runs 32–38 days. We also pre-position a spares kit — HV connectors, coolant, brake components, suspension parts — so the first year of operation never waits on a container.

Colombia's EV Policy Tailwind

Colombia has spent a decade building electric vehicle incentives: reduced import duties and VAT treatment for EVs, departmental and municipal exemptions from pico y placa restrictions in several cities, and a national electromobility strategy that publicly rewards corporate fleet conversions. For Caribbean coast operators, the practical benefits stack up as: lower landed cost on the EV versus equivalent diesel import duty, exemption from circulation restrictions in Barranquilla and Cartagena zones where they apply, and access to green-fleet tender preferences now appearing in port-adjacent logistics contracts.

A Coast-First Strategy

Colombian fleets tend to assume electrification starts in Bogotá. The physics say otherwise — flat, hot, short-loop coastal duty is the easiest electric truck business case in the country. Barranquilla combines that duty with a construction boom, an expanding port and a distribution market serving the whole coast. Build the depot on the Vía 40, start with the quarry ring, and let the coast prove what the Andes will take five more years to prove.
