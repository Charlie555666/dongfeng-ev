KT1D Electric Sweeper Charging and Energy Planning for Municipal Depots

Municipal depots are, in principle, the easiest charging environments in road transport: vehicles return to the same place every night, routes are known, and duty is predictable. In practice, sweeper fleets break that pattern in one important way — they work at night, which means they charge during the day, when tariffs are higher and ambient temperature is at its peak. The Dongfeng KT1D electric sweeper truck — CATL 232/266 kWh LFP, LvKong 128 kW rated / 260 kW peak direct drive with EBS — is designed around that inverted schedule, and this article sets out how to plan depot energy for it properly.

The key insight: because the window is long, charger power per vehicle can be low. A sweeper returning at 25% SOC on a 232 kWh pack needs roughly 175 kWh replaced. Over a 10-hour window that is an average of 17.5 kW — well inside a standard 22 kW AC connection.

Energy Budget: Brush and Suction Dominate

Sweeper energy is not a distance problem:

  • Traction: 0.65–0.90 kWh/km in slow sweeping duty, so 60–120 km uses 40–105 kWh.
  • Brush motors: typically 6–14 kW combined while sweeping, running continuously — 45–115 kWh over an eight-hour shift.
  • Suction fan: the largest single auxiliary load, typically 10–22 kW — 80–170 kWh over the shift if run continuously.
  • Cab HVAC and lighting: 2–5 kW — 15–40 kWh.
  • Typical shift total: 180–330 kWh depending on pack option and duty intensity.

Read against the pack options: 232 kWh covers standard single-shift municipal routes; 266 kWh covers intensive routes, continuous suction duty, or operations in hot climates where cab cooling is a significant load.

Depot Power Sizing

Work the calculation in four steps:

  1. Per-vehicle daily energy from measured or modelled duty — say 240 kWh.
  2. Fleet total: 10 sweepers × 240 kWh = 2,400 kWh per day.
  3. Window: 13:00–03:00 = 14 hours, minus a safety margin → 12 usable hours. Required average site load = 200 kW.
  4. Configuration: ten 22 kW AC sockets (220 kW) with load management, or two 120 kW DC units with staggered scheduling plus redundancy.

AC sockets at every parking bay are usually the better answer for sweeper fleets: cheaper, no scheduling discipline required, and better for pack life because charge rates are low. DC is justified only where a second shift or an unexpected mid-day redeployment is likely.

Daytime Charging in Hot Climates

This is the specific risk of the sweeper pattern. Charging between 13:00 and 18:00 in a hot climate puts the pack at its thermal worst while it is also accepting charge:

  • Provide shade or covered bays. A simple canopy reduces pack cooling load measurably and costs very little.
  • Where the tariff permits, shift charging to the cooler overnight hours — the window allows it, and the pack prefers it.
  • Specify the high-ambient thermal package on every order for markets above 35 °C.
  • Consider solar canopies over the parking area. The duty pattern is close to ideal: vehicles are parked during peak solar production hours. See solar-hybrid charging and solar charging for municipal water trucks.

Redundancy and Uptime

Sweeping is a public-facing service with visible consequences when it fails. Two rules:

  • Specify one spare charge point per bank of 6–10 so a single failure does not strand vehicles.
  • Make plug-in at handover a written procedure. The most common cause of a sweeper not being ready is not a charger fault — it is a driver who parked without connecting. Telematics that report connection status make this enforceable.

Tariff and Demand Charges

Municipal depots are frequently billed on tariffs with demand charges, and a fleet of sweepers charging simultaneously after the morning shift can create a sharp peak:

  • Stagger start times by 15–30 minutes per vehicle, or install a load-management controller that does it automatically.
  • Check for time-of-use windows — a depot that can shift most charging to off-peak hours will materially reduce cost per shift.
  • Measure and review monthly. Demand charges are often the largest avoidable line in a depot electricity bill.
