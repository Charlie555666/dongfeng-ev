Unmanaged Charging Is the Silent Budget Killer

A fleet can buy the right trucks, install the right chargers, and still overspend by 30-40% on electricity - simply by letting every truck charge whenever it plugs in. Unmanaged charging creates two problems: every truck draws full power at once when shifts end, spiking the site's peak demand into the most expensive tariff band; and charging happens in expensive daytime windows while cheap overnight capacity sits idle. The TZ5Y electric 80t mining truck fleet with managed charging tells a different story: software sequences every session, throttles to finish-by-departure targets, and holds the site's peak draw flat. Fleets in Indonesia that add charge management to existing hardware report immediate savings with zero new infrastructure - the single highest-return software purchase in fleet electrification. This guide covers what the software does, what it saves, and how to deploy it in order.

What Smart Charging Software Actually Does

The functional core is scheduling: every TZ5Y reports its state of charge and next departure, and the software allocates charging power across the fleet so each truck reaches its target by dispatch time at minimum cost. The mechanisms: staggered starts - ten trucks plug in at 6 p.m. but charge in sequence, not simultaneously; throttled rates - a truck with twelve hours available charges at 40 kW instead of 120 kW, finishing on time at a fraction of the demand peak; tariff awareness - sessions shift into off-peak windows automatically where Indonesian time-of-use rates apply; and priority override - the truck dispatched first charges first, always. The driver experience stays simple: plug in, walk away, the truck is ready at shift start. Everything else - the optimization that saves the money - happens in the software layer.

Demand Charges: the Tariff Line That Punishes Peaks

Industrial electricity bills in most Indonesian markets carry a demand charge - a monthly fee on the site's highest fifteen-minute or thirty-minute power peak - and unmanaged truck charging drives that peak directly. Work the arithmetic: ten trucks charging simultaneously at 120 kW draw 1,200 kW; at demand charges of $5-$15 per kW per month, that single unmanaged evening costs $6,000-$18,000 monthly before a single kilowatt-hour is billed. The same ten trucks under managed charging, staggered and throttled to a 400 kW ceiling, cut the demand line by two-thirds while delivering identical energy by morning. The TZ5Y's 600 kWh pack and overnight parking window give the software generous room to work - slow, flat, cheap charging is exactly what the duty pattern allows. Fleets report demand-charge savings alone frequently exceed the entire software and networking cost within the first year.

Integrating Solar, Storage and the Grid

Charge management becomes energy strategy when generation enters the picture. A solar canopy over the truck parking generates through midday; trucks on night duty are parked then; managed charging schedules their daytime sessions to absorb solar output directly, displacing grid power at its most expensive hours. Battery storage extends the arbitrage: charge the stationary battery at off-peak overnight rates or from surplus solar, discharge into truck charging during peak windows - flattening both the tariff exposure and the demand peak. The TZ5Y fleet in Jakarta with solar, storage and managed charging can source a substantial share of its energy below grid tariff, and the Indonesian climate makes the solar side productive year-round. Charger Uptime: the Other Half of Management

The software manages more than scheduling - it watches the chargers themselves, and charger reliability is fleet reliability. A failed charger discovered at 6 a.m. by a driver with a 20% battery is an operational crisis; the same fault flagged by monitoring at 10 p.m. is a maintenance ticket. Charge management platforms monitor every session - successful, failed, derated - alert on faults in real time, and log the data that holds charger suppliers to their uptime commitments. The TZ5Y's charging interface reports session detail to the platform, so diagnosis separates vehicle-side from charger-side causes immediately. Fleets running managed infrastructure across Indonesia hold charger availability above 98% by this routine; fleets without it discover their infrastructure's weaknesses at dispatch time, which is the most expensive moment to learn anything.

Charging Planning for TZ5Y Fleets

Charging infrastructure decisions made at order time shape fleet economics for a decade. The TZ5Y supports DC fast charging through a CCS2 inlet, recovering 20-80% of its 600 kWh pack in roughly an hour on a 120 kW unit, while overnight AC or lower-power DC charging covers base load at the cheapest tariff. A sound depot plan matches charger count to shift patterns rather than truck count - fleets running single shifts rarely need more than one fast charger per four to six trucks plus overnight points per bay. Grid connection capacity should be confirmed with the utility before truck delivery, because transformer upgrades carry the longest lead times in the whole project. Fleets in Indonesia should also model tariff structures: time-of-use pricing rewards overnight charging discipline, and the savings fund the infrastructure faster than most buyers expect.
