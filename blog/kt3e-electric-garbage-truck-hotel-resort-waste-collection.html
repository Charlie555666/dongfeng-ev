The Noise Case Is the Business Case

Refuse collection noise has three components, and electrification removes two of them:

  1. Engine and exhaust. The dominant source on a diesel refuse truck. Eliminated entirely on the KT3E.
  2. Compaction and body hydraulics. Present on both, but noticeably quieter when not competing with engine noise — and modern electric units run the packer from an electric PTO with lower acoustic output.
  3. Bin handling. Largely unchanged; mitigated by crew practice and bin-lift damping.

At the kerb, a diesel refuse truck measures roughly 88–95 dB(A) during compaction. The KT3E measures roughly 65–72 dB(A). That is not a marginal difference: it is the difference between a permitted 05:00 collection and a complaint file.

Route Design for Resort Corridors

Resort and hotel districts have a service geometry that changes the route plan:

  • Service yards, not kerbs. Collection happens in designated bays, often behind buildings with tight turning circles and sometimes height restrictions. Confirm vehicle height, turning radius and rear-bin-lift clearance against every service yard on the route before ordering.
  • Cluster collection. Resorts are often grouped; a route serving 8–14 properties within 10–25 km is typical. Daily distance of 60–110 km fits comfortably inside the 166 kWh option, with the 262 kWh option reserved for routes that also cover municipal kerbside work.
  • Seasonal peaks. Peak-season waste volume can rise 40–70%, which means either more trips or a larger body. Specify for peak, not average — an under-sized body in high season forces double runs and erodes the economics.
  • Glass and recycling separation. Many hospitality groups operate separated streams; specify split-body or multi-compartment options if the contract requires it.

Under-specifying the pack here manifests as a truck that cannot finish a peak-season shift, which is the operational failure mode to avoid.

Charging at a Resort or Hotel Service Depot

Tourism operators often have the advantage of a private service yard with existing electrical capacity. Two configurations:

  • Depot overnight charging — the default. A 22–44 kW AC connection per bay covers a full shift's energy in 4–8 hours. Cheapest and best for pack life.
  • Mid-day top-up — where peak season demands two shifts, a 60–120 kW DC unit at the service yard delivers a 30–40 minute recovery between rounds.

One specification note specific to hospitality: charging equipment should be sited away from guest areas and, where possible, screened. Even quiet chargers produce a low hum, and resort operators are sensitive to it.

Procurement and Contracting Models

Hotel and resort waste is usually collected under one of three arrangements, and each changes who pays for electrification:

  1. Municipal or district contract — the city specifies and the contractor operates. Electrification is driven by tender requirements.
  2. Private waste contractor — owns the fleet, carries both capex and operating saving. This is the fastest-adopting group because the full saving lands on their P&L.
  3. In-house resort fleet — usually smaller, often motivated by guest experience and ESG reporting rather than cost alone.

For contractors, the bid strategy matters: see municipal and district tender bid strategy.

Specification Checklist for Hospitality Waste Fleets

  • Peak-season daily waste volume and number of lift cycles per shift.
  • Service yard dimensions: turning circle, height limit, gradient, surface.
  • Collection window permitted by the property or district.
  • Whether separated recycling streams require a split body.
  • Depot electrical capacity and whether a private service yard is available for charging.
