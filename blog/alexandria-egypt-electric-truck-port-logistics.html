Alexandria's Mediterranean Gate: TE8M and KT3F Electric Truck Strategy for Egypt's Western Port City

Alexandria handles a large share of Egypt's external trade. The Alexandria and Dekheila port complex moves container, grain, and general cargo for a national market of 110 million people, feeding Cairo 200 km to the south and the Delta governorates — the densest agricultural and industrial belt in the country — in every direction. We have covered Cairo's fleet decarbonisation and the Suez Canal corridor; this piece is the Alexandria strategy: electric port tractors on the city's container and grain flows, plus a municipal and industrial dust-control fleet that solves one of the city's oldest problems. The vehicles are the TE8M electric tractor (CATL 400 kWh LFP, 49 t GCW) and the KT3F electric sprinkler truck (CATL 166/200 kWh, 18 t GVW). Country-level incentives and SCZone context are in the Egypt market guide.

Alexandria's Freight Rings

Three fixed flows dominate truck work around the port city:

  • Port drayage: containers from the Alexandria and Dekheila terminals to the city's container yards and CFS strip — loops of 10–35 km, two shifts, heavy gate queueing.
  • Grain and food corridors: wheat and corn from the port silos to the Delta's milling and feed industries, plus Alexandria's own food-processing belt — legs of 50–150 km.
  • Cairo trunk staging: freight staged for the desert road and agricultural road corridors to the capital — 220 km legs that suit Phase 2 electrification with mid-corridor charging.

The drayage ring and the food corridors are Phase 1 material. Both are fixed, both return to port-side yards nightly, and both punish diesel trucks with exactly the duty — idling, queueing, stop-start — that electric drivetrains eliminate.

The energy line reflects Egypt's unusual tariff environment: industrial power is subsidised and cheap in international terms, while diesel is scarce-currency expensive and supply can be administratively rationed in tight quarters. For a port operator, the electric drivetrain's independence from the diesel supply chain is worth as much as the cost gap — a TE8M's energy supply is a grid connection, not a fuel queue.

The KT3F: Dust Control as Public Health Infrastructure

Alexandria's air quality problem is structural: port operations, cement and steel plants along the Dekheila industrial strip, and the limestone dust that the wind carries off the western desert edge. Municipal and industrial road watering is therefore not cosmetic — it is daily public-health infrastructure, running fixed routes every dry day. The KT3F electric sprinkler truck was built for precisely this duty:

  • CATL 166 or 200 kWh LFP powering both traction and the electric PTO water pump — watering routes of 60–120 km per shift on a single charge.
  • 18 t GVW with tank bodies of 10–15 m³ and front spray, rear spray and side-jet configurations.
  • Zero tailpipe on the route itself: a diesel water truck watering a street to suppress dust while emitting its own particulates has always been an irony; the KT3F ends it.
  • Quiet pre-dawn operation: electric drive allows 4 a.m. watering runs in residential districts that diesel trucks cannot serve without complaints.

For cement, steel and fertilizer plants along the industrial strip, the KT3F doubles as an internal-road and stockyard dust-control unit under Egyptian environmental inspection regimes — an emissions-visible application where the electric specification simplifies compliance rather than complicating it.

Charging Around the Port

Alexandria's advantage is that port-side and industrial-belt yards sit within a 20 km ring. A combined fleet — eight TE8M tractors, six KT3F sprinklers — needs:

  1. Two 240 kW DC chargers at the port logistics yard: overnight base charging for tractors plus mid-shift top-ups during the gate-queue lull.
  2. Two 120 kW chargers at the municipal or industrial yard for the KT3F fleet's overnight cycle.
  3. Solar hybrid option: Alexandria's Mediterranean climate supports 60–100 kWp arrays; at Egyptian industrial tariffs the case for solar is about resilience as much as cost.

Total budget near USD 200,000–260,000, repaid by the tractor fleet's fuel savings alone inside two years. Egypt's recent expansion of renewable capacity — the Benban solar complex and the growing wind fleet on the Red Sea coast — means the electrons feeding that depot get cleaner every year, strengthening the emissions case that port authorities and international shipping lines increasingly attach to green-port programmes.
