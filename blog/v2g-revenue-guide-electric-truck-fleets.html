V2G Revenue Guide: How electric truck Fleets Earn Money from the Grid

How an electric truck earns from the grid

A parked electric truck is a battery on wheels, and vehicle-to-grid (V2G) lets that battery sell services back to the power network. The Dongfeng TE8M 6x4 tractor carries a 400 kWh CATL LFP pack with a 400 kW LvKong motor, rated at GCW 49 t and 280 km range, which is large enough to join frequency response and peak-shaving programs. This guide explains the mechanics, revenue per truck per year, battery wear versus grid income, depot demand-charge shaving and a pilot checklist for fleets ready to monetise idle hours.

V2G mechanics

V2G uses a bidirectional charger to pull energy from the truck pack to the site or grid when prices are high or frequency is low, then recharge when prices fall. The truck must be parked, plugged in and not needed for the next shift. A 400 kWh pack can export 200-300 kWh per event after reserving a range buffer, several times a week, without touching the 70 percent warranty floor if managed well. The depot needs a bidirectional DC unit and a grid aggregator contract.

Battery wear versus grid revenue

Extra cycles cost capacity, but LFP is cheap to cycle. At $120-$180 per kWh, 100 kWh of added wear is $12,000-$18,000 of pack value, spread over years. V2G adds maybe 50-100 shallow cycles a year, far below the 4,500-cycle warranty, so the revenue typically exceeds wear by 3-5 times if you cap depth of discharge at 20 percent. Keep the pack above 70 percent state of health and the warranty stays intact.

Demand-charge shaving for depots

The biggest depot saving is not export but shaving the peak kW the site draws, which sets the monthly demand charge. Discharging the TE8M during the site's daily peak can cut a 500 kW spike to 300 kW, trimming the demand charge by 20-40 percent. For a depot with $8,000 monthly demand charges, that is $1,500-$3,000 a month, often more than export revenue, and it needs no grid permission beyond the aggregator.

Pilot checklist

  • Confirm bidirectional charger and grid code approval locally.
  • Sign an aggregator contract for frequency and capacity markets.
  • Reserve a range buffer so dispatch never strands a truck.
  • Cap depth of discharge at 20 percent to protect the pack.
  • Meter per truck to prove revenue and isolate wear cost.

Bidirectional charger specifications

A V2G-capable site needs a charger that flows both ways at 150-360 kW DC, with a grid-forming inverter and a certificate to the local grid code, which in the UAE means DEWA or equivalent approval for reverse power. Budget $40,000-$70,000 per bidirectional bay versus $25,000-$40,000 for a one-way unit, and plan the site earthing and isolation for reverse current. The truck side is already ready: the TE8M pack speaks the standard CCS2 or GB/T protocol, so the only new hardware is the bidirectional front end and the aggregator gateway that dispatches each truck.

Aggregator contract terms

The aggregator pays you for capacity, frequency and energy, but reads your pack state every few seconds and will not dispatch below your reserved buffer. Negotiate a floor of 20 percent depth of discharge, a cap of two events per day, and a per-kWh wear settlement so a deep call is paid, not assumed. Typical UAE terms pay $2,000-$4,000 a year for frequency, $1,500-$3,000 for capacity and $1,500-$3,000 for peak shaving, which is the $6,000-$12,000 band in the table above. Read the exit clause: you should be able to pull a truck for a long shift without penalty if you give 24 hours notice.

Scaling the V2G program

Start with one bidirectional bay and three TE8M tractors on predictable regional loops, prove the $6,000-$12,000 per truck, then scale the bays as the aggregator raises your capacity cap. Each added bay serves three to five trucks, so a 20-truck fleet needs four to six bays at full rollout, a capex of $160,000-$420,000 that the grid revenue recovers in 4-7 years. Keep a reserve of trucks off the program for long shifts, and let the dispatcher pull a unit with 24 hours notice so revenue never strands a delivery. Phase the bays so each one is justified by proven truck hours, and the program stays cash-positive from the second bay onward.

Accounting and reporting

Book V2G income as a depot line, not a truck line, so the saving shows against the energy bill where the auditors look. Meter per truck to isolate wear, and report state of health quarterly so the warranty floor stays visible to the fleet controller. UAE operators under IFRS or local standards can treat the battery as a stationary asset only when it leaves the truck; while it is on the chassis, keep the revenue as ancillary income and the wear as a maintenance accrual. Clean reporting is what keeps the CFO signing the next bay, because the saving is visible and the risk is capped.

When V2G is not worth it

Skip V2G if your trucks never sit parked for four hours, if the local grid has no frequency or capacity market, or if demand charges are already low. In those cases the TE8M still wins on fuel and maintenance; the grid revenue is a bonus, not the business case. Run the pilot checklist above for 90 days and read the aggregator statement before committing capital to more bays, because the revenue band is real but market-specific, and a market without peak pricing will not pay it. A 90-day read also tells you whether your duty cycles actually leave the idle hours the model assumes.

Driver role in V2G

Drivers support V2G by plugging in on arrival and not blocking the bidirectional bay, the same charging etiquette from the training curriculum. A truck left on a one-way bay cannot export, so the depot maps which bays are bidirectional and routes parked trucks to them during the revenue window. Ten seconds of driver discipline per shift is what makes the $6,000-$12,000 per truck real, because a missed plug-in is a missed event fee from the aggregator. Train the bay habit in week one so it is automatic before the first revenue dispatch.
