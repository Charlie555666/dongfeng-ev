KTH1 Electric Cargo Truck on Tanzania's Mining Corridor: Heavy Haul Review

Tanzania's mineral corridors are long, dusty, and diesel-dependent, which is exactly why the KTH1 EV truck is drawing attention from fleet operators hauling ore and equipment between the ports, the central railway, and the mines of the northwest. This heavy electric cargo platform pairs a 424 kWh liquid-cooled CATL LFP pack with a 510 kW LvKong drivetrain to move up to 60 tonnes of gross combination weight across distances that used to demand a convoy of diesel tractors. In this review we look at the hardware, the real-world range on typical Tanzanian haul profiles, the charging plan that keeps it working through a shift, and the procurement path for fleet buyers evaluating the model against both diesel and other electric options.

Why the Tanzania mining corridor needs a heavy EV truck

The haul from the coastal terminals inland to the gold and nickel projects is a textbook case for electrification. Loads are heavy, the routes are repeatable, and the return legs are often partially laden. A diesel prime mover burning through the escarpments racks up fuel cost and maintenance that compound with every gradient. The KTH1 EV truck flips that equation: energy is cheaper per kilometre, the motor loses far less to heat on climbs, and the regenerative braking on the descents recovers energy that a friction brake would throw away. For a Tanzanian operator running fixed logistics lanes, predictable electricity cost is a strategic advantage over volatile diesel pricing.

KTH1 powertrain and battery architecture

At the core of the KTH1 sits a 424 kWh CATL LFP battery, liquid-cooled for stable cell temperatures across the hot dry season and the cooler highland nights. LFP chemistry was chosen deliberately: it is tolerant of deep cycling, thermally stable, and suited to the daily charge-and-discharge rhythm of a mining corridor fleet. The LvKong motor delivers 510 kW of peak power, enough to hold grade on loaded climbs without the transmission losses of a conventional driveline. Dongfeng backs the pack with an 8-year or 4,500-cycle warranty, which on a two-shift mining operation translates to years of covered service life.

Real range on the corridor

Range is the question every fleet manager asks first, and the honest answer is that it depends on load, grade, and cabin load-shedding. On a flat or gently rolling corridor with a full 60 t GCW, operators report 280-320 km before the pack needs a top-up. That comfortably covers a single loaded out-and-back on most Tanzanian lanes if the charging point sits at one end. Three factors move the needle most:

  • Payload: every tonne above the design point shortens range linearly.
  • Gradient: sustained climbs at altitude draw peak current and cut range fastest.
  • Ambient heat: liquid cooling protects the cells but cabin and ancillary load still draws from the pack.

Planning around a 20-80% daily window rather than a full 0-100% cycle keeps the pack healthy and matches the warranty cycle count.

Charging and battery swap strategy

The KTH1 supports DC fast charging at 120, 240, or 360 kW, recovering 20-80% in roughly 45-90 minutes depending on the supply. For a corridor fleet, the practical pattern is to install a 240 kW or 360 kW depot charger at the mine end and let the trucks opportunity-charge during loading and mandatory rest breaks. Because the battery is LFP and liquid-cooled, repeated fast charging does not punish the pack the way it would on older chemistries. Operators running swap-enabled siblings can also rotate packs in 5-6 minutes, but the KTH1 itself relies on its fixed high-capacity pack for the long haul.

Total cost of ownership versus diesel

The headline FOB price of $120,000-$160,000 looks steep next to a comparable diesel tractor, but the operating math favours the EV truck quickly on fixed corridors. Electricity per tonne-kilometre is a fraction of diesel, and the elimination of engine oil, filters, and exhaust aftertreatment removes a large slice of scheduled maintenance. Procurement and the Tanzania market

Buying a heavy EV truck for Tanzania is less about the sticker and more about the charging infrastructure and duty path. Fleet buyers should map depot power capacity before signing, secure a service partner for the LvKong driveline, and budget for spare wheels and tyres suited to unpaved mine access roads. Why the KTH3 electric cargo truck is a useful sibling

Not every lane needs 60 t of combination weight. Where the cargo is lighter or the distance shorter, the KTH3 electric cargo truck shares the same Dongfeng electric architecture at a smaller scale, giving fleets a common parts bin, a common charger, and a common driver training path. Running KTH1 and KTH3 units side by side simplifies workshop stock and lets operators right-size each truck to its route without fragmenting the maintenance program.

Driver training and fleet onboarding

Moving a corridor fleet onto the KTH1 EV truck is as much a training exercise as a procurement decision. Drivers used to diesel prime movers must learn electric torque delivery, regenerative braking behaviour, and the rhythm of charging windows instead of fuel stops. The LvKong drivetrain has fewer controls to manage, and most operators report a short learning curve once drivers feel the instant low-speed pull on graded climbs. Fleet managers should run a phased rollout: pilot two or three units on the most predictable lane, train a core driver group, and lock in a charging routine before scaling the whole corridor. That approach catches infrastructure gaps early and builds driver confidence without disrupting the ore flow.

Residual value and warranty planning

Residual value is the quiet risk in any heavy EV purchase, and the KTH1 addresses it with a conservative LFP pack warranted for 8 years or 4,500 cycles. Because LFP degrades slowly and liquid cooling protects cell health, operators can plan a second-life use for the pack or a predictable trade-in after the warranty window. Fleet buyers should account for the battery separately from the chassis, track cycle count against the 4,500-cycle cap, and negotiate a service contract covering the LvKong motor and charging hardware. Treating the pack as a managed asset rather than a sunk cost turns an unfamiliar vehicle class into a financeable, depreciable fleet investment.

Conclusion

For Tanzanian mining and corridor logistics, the KTH1 EV truck turns a fuel-exposed, maintenance-heavy operation into a controllable electric fleet. The 424 kWh pack, 510 kW driveline, and LFP durability make it a credible diesel replacement on repeatable lanes, and the shared platform with the KTH3 electric cargo truck keeps the whole fleet easy to support. With charging planned at the corridor ends and a service partner lined up, the total cost case is hard to ignore.
