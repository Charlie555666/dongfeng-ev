KT5M Electric Box Truck Urban Depot Charging: AC Overnight vs DC Opportunity Charging

The single biggest infrastructure mistake urban delivery fleets make is over-specifying charging. A fleet of 15 box trucks does not need fifteen DC fast chargers, and buying them will add six figures of capital for no operational benefit. The Dongfeng KT5M electric box truck — CATL 262/310 kWh LFP, LvKong 150 kW rated / 270 kW peak e-axle with a 4-speed transmission — typically returns to depot with 50–70% of its pack remaining, which means the charging problem is far smaller than it first appears. This article sets out how to size it correctly.

Start From Actual Daily Energy, Not Pack Size

An urban KT5M route consumes far less than the pack's capacity:

The practical consequence: a typical urban KT5M needs 60–140 kWh replaced overnight, not 310. Sizing chargers against pack capacity rather than daily energy is the error that inflates depot capex.

Option A: AC Overnight Charging

Distributed AC sockets, one per parking bay:

  • Power: 22 kW three-phase, or 44 kW where the vehicle's onboard charger supports it.
  • Time to replace 100 kWh: roughly 5 hours at 22 kW, 2.5 hours at 44 kW — comfortably inside any overnight window.
  • Cost: the lowest per bay of any configuration, and the electrical installation is simpler.
  • Battery benefit: low charge rates are the gentlest option for long-term pack health, which matters when you are holding the asset for eight years.
  • Operational requirement: plug at parking, every time. No scheduling needed — which is why compliance is higher with AC than with shared DC.

Option B: DC Opportunity Charging

Shared DC units, with vehicles rotating through them:

  • Power: 60–180 kW per unit, one per 4–6 vehicles.
  • Time to add 100 kWh: roughly 1 hour at 120 kW, or 30–40 minutes at 180 kW.
  • Cost: substantially higher per unit, plus civils and often a supply upgrade.
  • When it earns its cost: two-shift operations, mid-day redeployment, or fleets where routes occasionally extend well beyond plan.
  • Operational requirement: active scheduling. A fleet that relies on shared DC without disciplined rotation will have vehicles that were never plugged in.

Demand Charges: The Hidden Cost of Simultaneous Charging

Most commercial electricity tariffs include a demand charge based on the highest kW drawn in a billing period. Fifteen trucks plugging in at 18:00 and drawing 22 kW each create a 330 kW peak, and the demand charge on that peak can exceed the energy cost itself.

  1. Measure your tariff structure first. Find out whether you are billed on peak kW and over what averaging window.
  2. Stagger charge start times — a 30-minute offset per vehicle is often enough to halve the peak.
  3. Install load management so the site controller enforces a ceiling automatically rather than relying on driver discipline.
  4. Use time-of-use windows where available, shifting the bulk of charging into the cheapest period.

Physical Depot Design

Three design points that cause problems when missed:

  • Charge point within one parking movement. If a driver must reposition to connect, they will not connect. See depot layout and queuing design.
  • Cable management. Loose cables across a working yard are a trip hazard and get run over. Specify cable management or overhead reel systems.
  • Enclosure rating. Urban depots are outdoor and often coastal; specify IP-rated enclosures and consider corrosion protection in salt-air environments — see corrosion protection.

Scaling From Pilot to Full Fleet

Most urban fleets electrify in three tranches, and the charging design should anticipate the third even when only the first is funded.

  1. Tranche one (2–4 vehicles): install AC sockets at the bays used by the pilot vehicles, plus spare capacity in the distribution board. Do not optimise — the purpose is data, and the cheapest installation that works is correct.
  2. Tranche two (8–15 vehicles): extend AC to all bays and add a load-management controller. By this point the measured kWh/km data tells you the true nightly energy, and the controller is what keeps demand charges under control as the fleet grows.
  3. Tranche three (20+ vehicles): review the supply. Above roughly 20 box trucks the depot connection usually needs upgrading, and the lead time for that work should start a full quarter before the vehicles arrive.

The recurring failure is designing each tranche independently, which produces an electrical layout that has to be reworked at every stage. Designing the final layout once and building it in phases costs marginally more in tranche one and substantially less overall.
