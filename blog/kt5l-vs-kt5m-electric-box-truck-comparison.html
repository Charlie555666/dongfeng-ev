KT5L vs KT5M: Which Electric Box Truck Fits Your City Distribution Fleet?

City distribution fleets keep asking us the same question: the KT5L electric cargo truck and the KT5M electric box truck sit in the same weight class, on the same CATL LFP platform — which electric truck should the fleet buy? It is the right question to ask, and the answer is that the two vehicles are not competitors: they are specialist tools for different distribution physics. Choosing wrong costs a fleet either payload it cannot use or energy it hauls for nothing. This comparison sets out the differences, the decision framework we use with clients, and the eight-year economics of each — the city-distribution companion to our earlier tractor and dump-truck comparisons, with route examples drawn from the Vietnamese and Southeast Asian distribution fleets we plan most often.

The Physics That Should Drive the Choice

Three questions decide the assignment:

  • What limits your route — cube or drops? If the binding constraint is pallet volume (DC-to-store trunk runs, appliance and furniture delivery, FMCG full-pallet work), the KT5M's full-size body wins: maximum legal cube on the 18 t chassis means more revenue per trip. If the binding constraint is stop count and handling time (pharma, e-commerce, convenience-store replenishment), the KT5L's compartmentalisation and body flexibility win: the truck earns its keep at the kerb, not on the highway.
  • Does the cargo need protection or temperature? The KT5L is the reefer platform of the pair — our cold-chain guide covers the electric compressor PTO and insulation engineering — and the vehicle of choice for food, pharma and flowers. The KT5M's high-cube box is the ambient and general-cargo specialist.
  • How far is the average day? Both cover a standard city day on one charge. But for trunk legs above 250 km — DC-to-regional-hub runs — the KT5M's 310 kWh long-range trim adds the margin that route profile needs, while the KT5L's 262 kWh is the right-sized (and cheaper) pack for pure metro work.

Eight-Year Economics: Nearly Identical, Deliberately

Because both ride the same platform, the operating economics converge — which is the point: the choice should be made on mission fit, not cost. On a 120 km/day distribution duty at USD 0.10/kWh:

  • Energy: both near USD 0.085/km blended (KT5M's heavier body config slightly above, KT5L reefer duty with compressor slightly above that).
  • Maintenance: both near USD 0.045/km — the platform's brake, coolant and inspection regime, with the reefer compressor adding its own (modest, electric) service line.
  • Eight-year TCO vs a diesel 18-t rigid: both finish 25–35% below diesel at typical Southeast Asian tariffs, with payback on the electric premium in roughly three years of distribution duty.
  • Where they diverge: the KT5M 310 kWh trim costs more up front and is only worth it on routes that use its range; the KT5L reefer costs more than an ambient body and is only worth it on cargo that needs it. Match the configuration to the mission and both vehicles beat the diesel baseline by similar margins.

The Mixed-Fleet Answer Most Fleets Should Hear

The most common correct answer to "KT5L or KT5M?" is: both, in the ratio your route book dictates. A distribution fleet running 60% trunk, 40% multi-drop assigns its KT5M units to the trunk book and its KT5L units to the drop book, and because the two share a platform, the whole fleet shares one charging depot, one spares inventory and one technician certification. That shared-platform economics — the same logic behind our sanitation and tractor pairings — is the quiet advantage of specifying a fleet from a single manufacturer family rather than assembling it from three brands' catalogues.
